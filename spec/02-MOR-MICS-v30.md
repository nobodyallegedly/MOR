# The MICS: MOR Inscribed Core Spec

*Version 30, freeze-ready, for Nobody, allegedly, to approve.*

*Reading this document: normal text is the protocol itself; italic text is commentary, reasoning and examples. A rule of a MIR is cited by that MIR's name and the rule's number ("Agreements rule 76"), the numbers of the six freeze-ready drafts this document names: Identity draft 15, Text draft 11, Envelopes draft 11, Money draft 11, Agreements draft 17 and Development draft 11, which hold the exact formats and rules. This document is the map, and stands on its own. The core names no particular use of MOR.*

## Purpose

**The test.** MOR is the law where bytes can prove it, and keeps the record where they cannot: what a signature, a hash or an order on a chain proves, every verifier decides alike; what bytes cannot prove (a fair price, an intention, whether a promise was kept in the world) MOR keeps signed and placed, and leaves to the people, their courts and their reputation, so its promise is not "nobody can take unfairly" but "nobody can take unseen", and the record is what lets the core stay small and right. The chain that places an act, a collective's own chain among them, is where bytes get their order: the one move behind a collective's line, behind what a line placed staying placed, and behind transfers counted where the line placed them, turning a fact nobody can time into a fact one chain has ordered.


This document defines the core of MOR (Media Over Relays): the **MICS**, MOR Inscribed Core Spec. The MICS is the core, and the six **MIRs**, MOR Inscribed Rules, offer a layer by layer look at it: each MIR is a set of rules, in its own text (the Identity MIR, the Money MIR, and so on), and this document is the map of all six. Once frozen, the core never changes. There is no MIR process; all choice and competition happen in cMIPs and Modules.

The core is deliberately agnostic. It describes identities, media, money, agreements and specifications, never what anyone does with them. What MOR could become is shown by the cMIPs and Modules built on it, never here.

*Reviewers should try to break it, above all identity continuity, ordering without a clock, and whether any use needs something this core cannot express.*

## Structure

The core is six layers, one per MIR, each depending on those beneath it. They are numbered in this order wherever a format names a layer: 0 Identity, 1 Text, 2 Envelopes, 3 Money, 4 Agreements, 5 Development. No rule of a lower layer depends on data only a higher layer's client holds; where a higher layer needs a lower one to carry something, it is a field there.

| Layer | MIR | Contains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 Identity | Identity | The ground: identity hash, keys, rotation, homes and receipts, sequences, routes and the inbox, links, succession, the way out |
| 1 Text | Text | Canonical text, the one native media: the text act, plain text always shown, the text format task. Text is universal |
| 2 Envelopes | Envelopes | Acts (outside and locked inside), public and private, key delivery, chains, media and the bake, vows, relays, delivery. Media other than text runs on Modules |
| 3 Money | Money | Obligations, settlement receipts and payer claims across rails, payee pointers, flow and vault, the price tag |
| 4 Agreements | Agreements | Agreements, keepers, claims, ownership agreements and their shares, publications and splits, time, collectives, grants, cloning, abandonment, contests; a stake's holders' rule, the owners' matters, issuance and proxies |
| 5 Development | Development | How specifications are named, uploaded, adopted and paid, and what each kind must carry; tasks, extensions and xtypes; the trust root |

**From bake to publication.** A work reaches its public in four acts, each saying one thing: a **bake** (Envelopes), "I uploaded this"; a **claim** (Agreements), "I claim to have made this"; an **ownership agreement** (Agreements), "we have agreed that this is how we claim joint ownership"; a **publication** (Agreements), "this is how we agree to publish it with these specified partners". The price is a fifth thing, a **price tag** (Money), which any wallet can pay. Ownership is one thing, distribution another: one work has one ownership agreement and may have many publications, each with its own price, partners and split, the owners' shares read from the ownership agreement in all of them. *Unless there is a publication, money sent to a bake goes to its uploader, with no split possible.*

**A collective acts through its members, and is judged in Agreements.** A collective is an identity with a chain key and no signing key of its own; its members act for it with their own keys, under the rules its founding names for each of five layers (Text, Envelopes, Money, Agreements, Development), and Agreements judges each such act. *Stated cost: a client knowing only Identity, or only Money, judges nothing a collective does; it shows every act for a collective unknown, and a Money-only wallet cannot pay a collective. Customers use agreements; only tips do not.*

**Tasks.** A task is a point where a MIR hands work to a cMIP that an agreement may name by the task's number. Tasks exist only where the MICS defines them; the full list is in Development. A hand-off with no task number is no task: a proven offer names its own proof checker.

**Extensions.** An agreement may also name cMIPs outside the listed tasks, for work nobody foresaw. An extension may add rules, never relax the core's.

**Xtypes.** A cMIP may also define new act types from core pieces, for anything the MICS did not foresee, filling no task: an **xtype**, "extra type". Clients that have not adopted it show its acts as unknown. So a cMIP plugs in three ways: a task, an extension, an xtype.

**Client conformance.** Every client implements Identity, Envelopes and Text. Every other MIR is optional per client, but all-or-nothing: a client implements it fully, or shows its acts as unknown. A client must implement every MIR whose acts it acts on: a wallet that only sends tips needs Money; a client that acts on agreements needs Agreements. Rules that only the signer's own client can honour (what you sign is what you saw; showing what a rule computes; never choosing a device-bound policy by default) are marked as conformance, not validity: no verifier can check them, and the text says so.

**Admission test.** Something belongs in the core only if it is part of the shared language every act is written in, or a guarantee no module may be able to break.

**Guiding question for every MIR:** what is the least the core needs to know to make this work?

**Four design rules,** applied to every rule in the core: a record that decides status or money counts only alongside something the party who benefits cannot produce alone; a layer's validity depends only on data its own clients hold; every protection an owner chooses is removable by the owner's chain key alone, and every protection others hold over a party needs that party's signature to change; a rule that rests on a fact nobody can check becomes an act someone signs, a conformance rule, or nothing.

**Options with a default.** Where the core offers the owner several options, one of them is the default when none is selected; the default is always one of the declared options, never a third, weaker rule.

**Nothing in MOR is updated.** Acts, agreements, specifications and the core itself are only uploaded or signed again.

**Evolutionary model.** The core is an ossified layer. Above it, MOR continuously sub-forks through the cMIPs and Modules people adopt. The market cuts some branches over time, but it is not a contest one fork wins: some branches are meant to stay specialized. A **specialized fork** is a branch above the frozen core, a set of cMIPs and Modules made for one domain, speaking the same core language and meant to stay in that domain. Each specialized fork talks to other forks to the degree it chooses; Identity and Text always apply across all of them, so an identity is the same identity, and a text can always be read, on every fork. Costs that fall only on rare cases are acceptable: MOR expects markets to appear where there is value to protect.

**Layers and granularity.** Wherever a choice can be left to the owner (how many homes, which home rule, auditors or not, public or private, a vault or not, anchoring or not), the core offers it and states the consequences, rather than choosing for everyone.

## Common conventions

- **Encoding:** deterministic CBOR (RFC 8949, section 4.2.1), floating-point values in the shortest form that preserves them (section 4.1), tags and simple values as they are; formats written in CDDL, every map a format defines closed. Unknown fields make an act invalid, and so does a data item nested more than 128 levels deep. The encoding and the CDDL conventions are written once, in Identity's Part three ("Encoding and conventions").
- **Outside its specification's format, invalid; not implemented, unknown.** An act whose type its specification does not define, or whose payload is not in the format that specification gives for the type, is invalid; a verifier that does not implement the specification shows it as unknown (Envelopes rule 30). This holds for every MIR and every cMIP alike. *Nothing in MOR is updated, so a later format is a new specification with a new hash, shown unknown by older clients, never invalid: strictness costs no flexibility.*
- **A malformed specification is no specification.** An act naming a specification whose content is not in Development's format names no specification: it is unknown to every client, since no client can have adopted it, and a verifier holding that content shows it as no specification. The invalid above stays for acts outside a specification the verifier implements.
- **Invalid beats unknown** (Envelopes rule 30). A verifier that can show an act invalid by any rule it can apply (its shape, its text, its acknowledgements) does so, whatever else in it the verifier cannot read; unknown is the verdict only where no rule it can apply fails.
- **Hashes:** SHA-256, with tagged hashes in the style of BIP-340, so hashes of different kinds of object can never be confused. Every tag begins with `MOR/`; a successor protocol must use a different prefix.
- **Written for people**: an identity, an act, a public key, a specification and a work are written in bech32m, lowercase, under the labels `mid`, `mact`, `mkey`, `mspec` and `mwork`, a checksum refusing a mistyped or cut string and a string of another kind; an identity's where-it-lives form adds `?r=` hints outside the checksum; bare hexadecimal stays accepted; no form for any secret (Identity, "Written for people").
- **Signatures:** a scheme is a founding number or the hash of a signature-scheme specification. The founding schemes are schnorr over secp256k1 (BIP-340) for signing keys and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205), a hash-based post-quantum scheme, for chain keys. A client that meets a scheme it does not implement treats the act as unknown, unless a rule it can apply shows the act invalid (invalid beats unknown, above). *New schemes, including a post-quantum signing-key scheme, are added by publishing a specification, never by reopening the core.*
- **Encryption:** XChaCha20-Poly1305 for content, with a new key per object; hybrid X25519 plus ML-KEM-768 (FIPS 203) for identities' encryption keys.
- **No clock.** Timestamps are hints, never load-bearing. Every "before" is judged inside one named chain, by reference to a specific act. Agreements that need real time name a time reference, such as a block height.
- **Canonical text** (Text MIR) in every text field.
- **One signer per act.** Anything that needs two identities is two acts, the second naming the first.
- **An act counts only where it is held.** A receipt, an acknowledgement or a keeper record counts only alongside the valid act it names, and only while its signer's chain keeps it valid (Identity, "Evidence counts only while its signer's chain keeps it"), save where a collective's own line placed it (Agreements rule 6) and where an Agreements rule read a keeper's record to place an act or a relied-on version's completeness rests on it (Agreements rule 42); nothing forces propagation, and delivery is the signer's interest.
- **The verdict vocabulary, said once.** *Pending* has one meaning in every layer: waiting on something outside the act (Identity: bound to a rotation that does not count yet; Money: a payment the rail has not answered; Agreements: a condition not yet decided, or a payment its seller's side has not yet recorded). *No line, no fork, no closing, no ending, no declaration, no specification* are one verdict under six names: an act claiming to be X that fails X's conditions is no X, and decides nothing as X. *Counts for nothing* is an umbrella over void, not done, uncited, outside an area and in a broken stretch, as the Agreements MIR uses them. The records a client shows (*disputed, contested, creator mismatch, wrong receipt, bypassing, mismatch, not found, deferred*) are displays: they decide nothing, and two verifiers holding the same acts show the same ones.
- **A like is never an acknowledgement.** Only act types the Identity, Money and Agreements MIRs define may carry acknowledgements; any other act carrying one is invalid. *An acknowledgement carries weight (it can keep a thief's act alive as a dispute), so nothing as light as a like or a reply may carry it by accident.*

## MIR: Identity

**The identity.** An identity is the hash of its genesis act. It never changes. Keys are only the pens that sign for it:

- a **signing key** for everyday acts;
- a **chain key**, post-quantum, kept offline, committed in advance by its hash and used exactly once, to sign a rotation or a chain signature;
- an **encryption key**, published so others can deliver content to it (defined in Envelopes).

There is no third signing key: the chain key is the recovery key. A person may add **scoped keys**, each limited to what a higher MIR scopes it to, such as a person's grant key (Agreements). **A collective has no signing key**: its genesis declares its founding agreement in place of one and is signed by a chain key revealed in its signature, committing the next; its members act for it with their own keys (Agreements). *A one-way door in the format, written knowing it.*

**Genesis** declares the first signing key, the commitment to the first chain key, the homes, optionally a home rule and an audit requirement, and a declarations slot for high-risk settings that higher MIRs define, such as Money's vault. Only the chain key can change what genesis declares; a declaration can be removed by a null entry.

**Identity chain and sequences.** The identity chain holds only key events: genesis, rotations and chain signatures. Everyday acts form sequences, lines the owner keeps, one or several (for example one per device), each act naming the previous one. Every everyday act carries its position and a running summary of its sequence (over the acts before it; a rotation records, for each kept tip, the summary including it: Envelopes, "The running summary"), so it can be proven cheaply to lie on the line leading to a later act. Position and summary are not checked act by act: they are checked when a line is rebuilt, and an act whose position or summary does not fit the line leading to a kept tip cannot be proven kept (Envelopes rule 13). An identity may also hold **scoped keys**, installed by an act of a higher layer, such as a collective's grant key: Identity checks only the signature, and the layer that installed the key says whether the act counts; a client without that layer shows the act as unknown.

**Homes.** A home is a relay that focuses on identity, run by an operator: an ordinary identity that signs everything the home states. A home stores identity chains and serves them, with the identity's routes (where its content lives, and where others deliver to it), names and public links. Homes are declared by operator identity plus address, so a home can move servers without anyone rotating, and they are counted per operator: several homes run by one operator count as one, under honest labelling. An identity may host its own home: a self-hosted identity is trusted on its own signatures, its rotation counts with no receipt; a thief holding its chain key can serve a rotation of its own, and where the owner serves one too at that position, nobody can settle it: the position is contested for good, and the owner leaves it by the escape with both keys. That is a trust model chosen knowingly, and other homes declared beside it are backups by default.

**Receipts.** For every identity-chain act it accepts, a home signs a receipt: which act, at which position, at which point in the home's own log (a counter, never a time), and that it was the first it accepted there. A receipt counts only alongside the act it names, held by the verifier and valid: a receipt of nothing is neither support nor conflict. *Receipts are proof, not permission: they make a home answerable, since two receipts for two genuine rotations at one position prove it dishonest, and a stolen operator key alone can produce nothing that changes any identity's chain.* A receipt under a log summary that other operators have cosigned can never be erased by the operator's own rotation.

**Rotation.** The owner signs a rotation offline with the chain key and delivers the exact same bytes to every home. It reveals the committed chain key, binds a new signing key (of any scheme the owner chooses), commits the next chain key, names the latest genuine act of each sequence it keeps, with its position and running summary, so anyone can check kept history without opening private acts, and may disown acts, change homes, rules, declarations, declare a succession, or, for a home operator, declare the home closed. The protocol does not restrict why an owner rotates; rotation is expected to be rare. A home checks a rotation before storing it and never drops one it accepted; a home may apply its own acceptance conditions, such as proof from a registered device, which is a market service, and which can refuse the owner as well as a thief: losing that device at a single or authoritative home, or at homes enough to block the home rule, can mean losing the identity, so clients never choose such a policy by default there, and should require a backed-up signing seed first. A rotation that fails the rotation checks was never a rotation: it counts nowhere, so the owner may sign again with the same chain key. The air-gapped Module accepts only bytes as showing that a rotation it signed failed them: the identity chain from genesis to that rotation's predecessor, which the Module checks offline itself; the online side's word alone opens no exception.

**What a rotation keeps.** Acts in the kept ancestry stay valid, however old. Acts outside it, or disowned inside it, are void, unless another identity acknowledged them (by an Identity, Money or Agreements act) or a named keeper recorded them before recording the rotation, in which case they are visible disputes. Disowning costs the owner nothing else: the genuine acts after a thief's act stay valid. Evidence another identity signed (a receipt, an objection, an absence statement, a cosignature, an acknowledgement) counts only while its signer's chain keeps it valid: voided by its signer's rotation, it is shown as a dispute and decides nothing, acknowledged or not; a signer's own acts that others relied on stay visible as disputed (its pair is Agreements'). Every conforming verifier holding the same acts, the signer's sequences up to the kept tips among them, computes the same answer; a verifier that does not hold what a rule needs (a kept line it cannot rebuild, a scoped key's installing act, a chain it cannot resolve) answers unknown, never valid, void or invalid: void needs every kept line of the rotation rebuilt and the act on none of them, and invalid a rule the verifier can apply and sees fail. Only these rules, and a grant's revocation (Agreements rule 76), make an act disputed.

**The witness act.** To rely on someone's post or message deliberately (a public promise, a threat kept as evidence, a statement someone relies on), an identity signs a witness act: an Identity act whose only content is "I received this act and rely on it", naming the act in its acknowledgements. It keeps that act visible as disputed even if its author later disowns it, for as long as the witness's own chain keeps the witness act. Like every Identity act it is public: it shows whom the signer relies on, never what was said. *Client conformance:* a client never signs one as a side effect of another gesture, and says what it does before signing.

**Several homes and home rules.** With several homes, the owner may declare one authoritative home (the others are backups) or a threshold greater than half the operators (a safety network). If they declare neither, the majority rule applies by default, or, beside a self-hosted home, that home is authoritative. A single home stays possible at genesis. Competing rotations revealing the same chain key: the first one each home holds wins there, and the home rule decides between homes. *A stolen chain key is like a stolen bitcoin key, unless the owner chose homes that guard against it.*

**Audits.** Homes may audit each other: a home publishes signed summaries of its whole log, other operators check that each extends the last and cosign it. An identity may require audited receipts, declaring its auditors; a rotation's receipts are judged under the requirement that rotation declares, so an owner can always drop auditors who have gone. Conflicting receipts make an identity contested at that point, never frozen; the conflict ends when the home rotates or an audit shows which receipt is real; a home proven dishonest at a position counts for nothing after it and keeps counting before it. Two summaries that do not extend each other stop the auditing, and carry no verdict on past receipts.

**The way out.** If its homes are gone, an owner can leave with a homeless rotation, signed with the current chain key and naming new homes. A live old home voids it with an objection naming it, while its operator's chain keeps that objection, which proves the home was alive after the rotation was made; a home closes by a rotation of its operator, so a stolen signing key can never close one; declared auditors can attest that a home no longer answers. *Stated cost: an objection signed with a stolen operator key voids the owner's homeless rotation until the operator rotates; an owner holding both keys leaves at once by the escape (below).* A homeless rotation becomes final once the owner's next rotation counts under the home rule the homeless rotation declared, which every Identity client can check; until then a late objection can overturn it. A rotation that the old homes held at the same position beats it always, final or not, however late its receipts surface: finality never lets a used chain key found years later replace the rotations made since. One accepted only on a client's own failed attempt to reach the home is shown as re-homed without audit, keepers, vault payments and agreements must not rely on it, and the next rotation never makes it final: an objection from an old home voids it whenever it surfaces, with every rotation built on it, unless the owner endorses it with the current signing key. *A thief holding the chain key controls the next rotation, and a censor can keep the objection from arriving; neither can make a theft final.* A homeless rotation also endorsed with the current signing key cannot be blocked by objections, even after a home refused a normal rotation, or where a thief contests a self-hosted identity's position (the air-gapped Module signs that escape only on the competing rotation's bytes, checked offline, never on the online side's word): an owner holding both keys can leave any home. An owner who loses the chain key can never rotate or leave; back up both seeds. *Keep your keys separate.*

**Reaching an identity.** Routes are of two kinds: outbox, where an identity's content of a type is found, and inbox, where others deliver acts addressed to it. An identity may declare no inbox. Any two identities can find each other without already sharing a relay: identity, home, routes. Whether a relay accepts deliveries, from whom and at what price, is the relay market's business.

**Links.** A link is a two-way statement that two identities belong to the same entity; one side may be an account on another protocol. Its purpose is signing in with MOR and building bridges. It needs a claim by one side and a confirmation by the other; either side can end it; an unconfirmed claim is never displayed; it confers no authority. Private links are stored encrypted.

**Succession** is declared in a rotation, naming a successor identity in MOR or another protocol, and confirmed by the successor: for a MOR identity, by a link confirmation or a witness act naming the rotation; for another protocol, as its link cMIP says. Clients show it as declared until it is confirmed, and follow it only confirmed. *Nobody can be made a successor, of followers or of a collective's seat, without agreeing to it. Stated cost: a thief holding the chain key can name and confirm an identity of the thief's own.*

**Tasks:** identity proofs and credentials; naming; outside link proofs.

## MIR: Text

- **Text is the only native media,** because the protocol itself speaks in text: terms, agreements, grants, names, messages.
- **Canonical text:** valid UTF-8; LF line breaks only; no control characters other than LF (no TAB); no byte order mark anywhere; no noncharacters; no trailing spaces and no final line break; NFC, checked with the tables of one pinned Unicode version, Unicode 17.0, so every verifier agrees and new characters stay usable.
- **Text is universal:** every client can open and show any text act addressed to it, as plain text. A text act may name a format cMIP; a client without it still shows the plain words, and a format may hide only its own declared markup, never what a floor the protocol sets protects (letters, digits, combining marks, and the signs that carry what an amount or a word says), never adds text, and shows the rest in the order of the bytes, never hiding it by styling (Text, the text format task); that bound is checked by a client implementing the format, as conformance.
- **The text act is closed** (Text rule 14). It carries a fixed set of fields: outside, its signer, its binding, its content key if public, and `to` where it is addressed or answers someone; inside, its specification and type 0, the everyday fields, its payload, and optionally `refs` and `hint`. A text act carrying `acks` is invalid, and so is one carrying `objects`, save two cases, each one object: a text signed with a grant key naming the grant its binding names, `[grantor, grant]`; and a text a member or grantee signs for a collective, naming the collective and the decision it acts under, `[collective, decision]` (Agreements rule 58). A Text-only verifier shows the second unknown. Chained texts (a co-written document, a collective's log, a thread in one agreed order) are a cMIP's: each version stays a plain text act, and the cMIP carries the chain in a companion act of its own that names it. Its payload is decoded strictly: a closed map, the text in field 0, an optional format hash in field 1, nothing else. A text that is not canonical makes an act invalid even under a specification the verifier does not implement. *Allowed any other `objects`, anyone could attach text acts to another's chain, a deal or a collective's record, for a careless client to show as if they belonged there.*
- **What you sign is what you saw:** before signing, the plain text is always available, and it is the default for agreements, terms and grants; before signing terms, a grant or a clone, bidirectional controls are shown visibly, so no clause can display in an order different from its bytes. Lookalike and invisible characters are otherwise client warnings, not rules.
- **Task:** text format.

## MIR: Envelopes

- **Every act has an outside and a locked inside.** The outside carries only what a relay needs: the signer (or nothing, inside a sealed container), a commitment to the inside, the lock's details, the content key if the act is public, and recipients if it is addressed. Everything else, including type, pointers and payload, is locked. The inside carries a random salt, so short private replies cannot be guessed from their hash, and the outside commits to the unlocked inside, so one signature can never open to two contents.
- **Everything encrypted by default.** Public means the key travels with the act; private means the key reaches chosen recipients inside a sealed container, locked to their encryption key or to a bare key they supplied, with the act itself or in a key delivery. Going public later is one act: publishing the key. A key delivery is valid as an act by its shape; its key is used only if it opens what it names, and anyone holding a key may deliver it. *Relays cannot read private content at all; public acts carry their key, so anyone can read them. A private message is not a special kind that stands out, though an addressed act shows its recipient.* Identity acts are always public: they are an identity's public face. A thief holding the signing key can redirect the encryption key, or the inbox, until the owner rotates; deliveries made into that window are re-sent afterwards, and a seller who delivered into it did nothing wrong.
- **Public receiver, private sender.** A sealed container shows its recipients, never its sender, and only the recipients open the act inside and see who signed it. **Its outside is unauthenticated by design**: `sealed = [to, locked-act]`, with no signature; all proof lives in the inner act, signed by the sender and naming its recipients in its own `to`; what a relay can do to the outside, leave a recipient out, it can do by withholding, and delivery is the relay market's. *Signed by the sender, the outside would reveal the sender; signed by a key that is nobody's, it proves nothing. Adding a recipient, opening the act or swapping what it holds stays impossible.*
- **Types and specifications.** Every act names the specification defining its type by hash, and a type number within it. An act outside that specification's format is invalid, and one under a specification the verifier does not implement, or naming one that is no specification, is unknown (common conventions).
- **Chains and forks.** Acts on one object name, for each chain they belong to, that chain and the act they follow in it, so a merge of two branches is never confused with one act touching two objects; each MIR defines its own objects' chain rules. Two acts naming the same predecessor are a fork, provable from signatures. Every everyday act carries `prev`, empty for the first act of a sequence; one without it is invalid, and so is one carrying two entries in `prev`, judged act by act, a shape rule like a missing `prev`. The running summary's empty value and its construction are fixed, with a test vector.
- **Acknowledgements and references.** An acknowledgement says "I received this act", and counts only alongside the act it names. Only act types the Identity, Money and Agreements MIRs define may carry one: a text act, a bake or any cMIP's or Module's act that carries one is invalid. The rule limits which acts acknowledge, never which acts can be acknowledged: a post still becomes a dispute, not void, when a witness act, a buyer's claim or a signature acknowledges it. A reference says "look at this", pointing to an act or a web resource, optionally with the hash of what was there. A reference to an act may name the act's signer beside its id: a reader uses it only to know where to look, through the signer's identity, homes and routes; it never decides validity, a wrong one showing only when the reference is followed. *Client conformance:* a client referring to a public act holds it and names its signer; naming a private act's signer is the writer's choice, since it would expose the sender a sealed container hides. A repost is a reference: it pays nobody by itself.
- **How acts reach people.** An act counts for a verifier only once the verifier holds it. Identity acts must reach the home; acts that concern a counterparty should reach that counterparty's inbox; bakes and posts are uploaded to outbox routes and pulled from there; drafts never leave.
- **The bake.** A bake (Envelopes type 0) is media made into an act: its uploader **uploads** it. The work hash fingerprints a work's complete plaintext, and is what a claim points to. Media is always stored locked, its locked bytes fingerprinted so relays can check storage; a bake states the size of the media once unlocked; buyers check unlocked media against the work hash. A bake carries no price and names nobody it is made for: a bake carrying field 7 or 8 is invalid, those numbers reserved and never reused. A bake says only who uploaded it; it claims nothing. **Money sent to a bare bake is its uploader's.** A bake is never withdrawn: Envelopes' withdrawal act (type 3) is retired, never reused; what stops is a price tag or a publication. Segmentation and streaming belong to the media cMIP; a live stream has no work hash until it ends, so paid live access is sold by a publication. **Stated cost: whoever uploads a bake receives the tips and tag payments sent to it**, a fan who re-uploads someone else's work included; the owners' claim and their own publication stay visible beside it. *In Nobody, allegedly's words: "Absolutely, piracy cannot be stopped. But truly rewarding creators can be made easy."*
- **Vows.** A vow names in words something MOR cannot hash, existing or to come: its first act is its genesis, whose id is its name for good, and later versions are signed again on its own chain (Envelopes, "Vows"). It holds one or more promises, which its seller may bundle, and names what is promised to the buyer, never an event: two vows on one event stay separate, and a vow naming another moves none of its sales. Publications naming it sell it, with their words, and each sale is pending, confirmed (acts the buyer acknowledges) or contested (a contest act naming the sale, and optionally the promise not kept); only someone who signed onto it, at any price including none, may confirm or contest it, and one nobody signed onto carries no state (Agreements rule 13). Every sale of a physical good or of a service names a vow, unless its delivery is proven. A vow is owned by its signer; a publication selling a vow that an ownership agreement claims, not signed under it, is shown so, as for a work; a resale under a fresh vow escapes the marking, a stated cost: the fresh vow, signed by the reseller, is where to look. The core keeps only this grammar: what is promised, delivered or became, quantities, kinds and streams belong to cMIPs (streams: the live media cMIP). A claim still binds works only, or a vow by its name.
- **Relays** publish, fetch and mirror, untrusted by default, operated by identities; a relay's statement of the acts it holds is the relay transport cMIP's, not the core's: the relay's commitment and its type, Envelopes type 2, are retired, never reused. Of two copies of one act differing only in their signature, the one whose signature is valid counts, and a relay holding a copy it could not verify SHOULD replace it with one it can.
- **Fail closed, scoped.** A client never signs, pays or accepts under a specification it does not implement; it shows such acts as unknown, and may adopt the specification by opt-in; a verifier that can show an act invalid by a rule it can apply does so, whatever else it cannot read (invalid beats unknown).
- **Tasks:** media interpretation; anchoring (a cMIP proves an act existed by a point on a named clock; every layer above may rely on it; only anchors place anything on the clock, Agreements rule 33).

## MIR: Money

Money defines how a simple payment moves: never the rails, and nothing about who owns what. Its job ends when money reaches a payee pointer.

- **Settlement receipt.** Whatever the rail, Lightning, stablecoin or fiat, the receiver signs the same receipt: who paid whom, how much, in what unit, under which agreement. Only the rail's proof differs, checked by the rule of the rail Module it names. Card and bank rails name the trusted party their proof rests on.
- **Rails are Modules; a text signs nothing.** An agreement names one payment cMIP; each rail is a rail Module beneath it. A rail Module's specification states the rail's kind, a request rail or a push rail (Development, field 11), and what its proof binds, of payee, purpose and previous hop (Development, field 13); a verifier reads both there, never from the Module's code, and a rail Module's specification without them is no specification, a receipt or claim naming it unknown. A rail Module is a Module whose field 5 names a payment cMIP, a cMIP whose field 2 lists Money's task 6, decided by decoding that cMIP; a verifier not holding it answers unknown for a Module lacking fields 11 and 13, and for every receipt or claim naming it. A request rail's field 13 holds payee and purpose, or its specification is no specification. Where a proof binds part of the three, a disagreement is settled element by element: for each of payee, purpose and previous hop, the proof decides where field 13 holds it, the payer's claim where it does not, a contrary receipt shown as a dispute; where the payee is unbound, the payment address the payee's rails gave (Money rule 4) decides whose payment it is (Money rule 11). Receipts are signed by receivers and claims by payers; no specification signs anything.
- **The payee chooses its rails.** The agreement names the payment cMIP; the payee's own pointer or vault names the rail Modules it accepts. A receipt or claim counts only on one of those, and, under an agreement, only on one implementing that agreement's payment cMIP. *A payer cannot pay on a rail the payee does not accept, except through a conversion service that pays the payee on a rail it does. A new rail needs a new pointer or vault, never a new agreement; in a collective, the members its rule for Money names choose the rails.*
- **Double entry.** A receipt is signed by whoever received on that hop. A payer's claim, carrying the rail's proof, is evidence on equal footing: private by default, published by the payer's choice. Where the two disagree, the disagreement is shown on the receiver and the greater amount counts until the receiver signs a receipt matching the proof. *Hiding income requires the payer's silence or collusion; that is the measured promise.* One rail proof discharges one payment, or one named batch.
- **Routes across rails.** When payer and payee use different rails, conversion services, identities paid under their own publications, carry the money in hops; each receipt names the previous one and the agreement it fulfils, so the route reads as one chain. Where the next payee signs no receipt, the paying hop's own claim, naming the previous hop's receipt, shows the forward passed on, one payment counted once (a named batch, on receipts only, letting one payment carry several forwards in parts; a forward may also be paid in several payments, the stop owing only the shortfall); the buyer of a purchase so paid is the first payer whose claim of the first hop cites in its `refs` the tag it buys; where a receipt and a claim of one hop name different previous receipts, the payment's commitment decides, else the payer's claim, the contrary receipt a dispute; and the claim is disclosed to the final creditor as a receipt would be (Money rules 11, 12, 24, 26 and 27). If it breaks, the receipts name exactly which hop and which agreement failed, and Agreements take over.
- **Obligations and discharge.** An obligation is signed by the debtor and says who owes whom, how much, in what unit; a creditor's statement is a claim, never an obligation. It is discharged when valid routes bring the amount to the creditor's payee pointer, or released by its creditor. An undeliverable payment stays an open obligation. An obligation's states are shown by clients that read Agreements; a Money-only client shows every state of an obligation as unknown, an IOU's included. *A small Money view answering the IOU case without Agreements is noted for MOR 2.*
- **A debt ends by payment or by its creditor's word.** A creditor's release, a Money act signed by the creditor alone, ends an obligation wholly, without full payment; a collective creditor signs it under its rule for Money. *Forgiving a sum is a money decision; forgiving it in exchange for future terms is also a deal, an Agreements agreement of its own.*
- **The price tag** (Money type 5) prices a bake or a publication: it names one of them, a price and a payee. It is versioned on its own chain; **a version with no price withdraws it**. On a bare bake only its uploader's tag counts; under a publication, only the split service's, or that of a party the publication designates. **A payment naming a counted tag is a purchase**, so a Money-only wallet can buy; what a purchase under a publication buys, and how it is shared, is Agreements'. *Two stops, two different people's: the signer withdraws a tag alone; a publication ends only by a later version every party signs (Agreements).*
- **Anonymous refunds go to a key, not to a proof.** A payer who stays unnamed may put a one-time key of its own into the payment commitment; a refund owed on that payment goes to whoever signs with that key. *Showing the rail's proof proves nothing about who paid: on Lightning the payee and every node on the route learn it. A payment that committed no key can be refunded to nobody, and nobody else can take its refund.*
- **Units** are small specifications of their own (Development kind 2), so one unit has one name on every rail; a verifier reads only a unit's hash.
- **What Money reads of Development.** A Money client decodes Development's specification format for a rail Module (whether it is a specification at all, and its fields 5, 11 and 13), and nothing more of Development; it never depends on Agreements.
- **A collective's Money acts** (its pointer, price tags, obligations, receipts, claims and releases) are signed by a member or grantee for it, with their own key, and judged by Agreements under its rule for Money; a Money-only client shows them unknown (Money rule 7).
- **Only identities have payee pointers,** each signed for that identity: a person's with its own key, a collective's by a member for it. A payment goes to the pointer of the payee its price tag names, or, sent to a bare bake, its uploader's.
- **Flow and vault.** Payee pointers form a versioned chain. An identity's **flow pointer** changes with the signing key. Its **vault** changes only with the chain key: a set of entries, per unit and per rail, each with a source under which its rail Module obtains a fresh address for every payment, and a limit; payments above the limit go to the vault; a limit of zero means everything in that unit goes to the vault; a unit the vault does not cover cannot be paid to the flow. Where several entries cover one unit, the smallest of their limits applies. *The vault is the grammar for setting up safer pointers, if the owner wishes to. A true rate limit needs a clock and a global view, which the core has not; the two ends of the range are given, and the middle is a market for custodial flow services.* The flow pointer that counts for a debt is the latest the payee's own acts hold (Money rule 17); a thief who changes the flow pointer cannot collect older obligations after the owner's anchored lock change, nor re-issue them; during the theft window the stolen key can re-point the payee's deals, a cost the owner bears, bounded per payment by the vault limits.
- **The owner hears of what the vault refuses.** A payment the vault leaves undeliverable leaves no act behind, so the clients tell the owner instead: the owner's client warns before pricing in a unit the vault does not cover and shows such debts as owed in a unit the vault cannot receive; a payer's wallet that refuses sends the payee an ordinary inbox message saying what it tried to pay and why. *Client conformance: whether a notice arrived can never be checked.* The debt stays open, and is paid once the unit is added.
- **Good faith.** A payment that followed the published pointer and vault counts as made until the lock change (a rotation after which such a payment would no longer follow the published chain) has its home quorum anchored on the clock the owner declared; after that point, only where the payee's own kept receipt or a payer's claim anchored by the point shows it: name your clock, anchor or bear the loss (Money rule 29). The loss from a theft window falls on the owner, never on a payer who followed the rules. *Client conformance:* a payer's client that cannot anchor on any reference the payee's clock names says so before paying: the payment will not be protected against a lock change.
- **Receivers and senders.** A receiver's identity is public; the visibility of its incoming flows is its choice, counterparties only by default. The core never requires a sender's identity.
- **A plain tip carries no module fees** beyond the rail's own. A referral counts as evidence only when the payer signs it.
- **Tasks:** payment, one payment cMIP per agreement with a rail Module for each rail beneath it; conversion. Receipts are signed by receivers and claims by payers; no specification signs.

## MIR: Agreements

*Agreements say who has agreed to what, who owns what, and who may act for whom. Money moves a payment to a payee pointer; Agreements say how it is shared, what is owed when something is not done, and how a group of people acts as one identity. This section is the map; the Agreements MIR holds the exact rules. Read it in order: agreements and deals first, then claims and ownership, publications and splits, time and keepers, then collectives, which carry most of the rules.*

### Agreements and deals

- **An agreement is terms plus signatures:** the terms, and one signature act from each party. An agreement binds identities, not keys, so it survives a party's key rotation. Its terms carry the hash of at most one cMIP for each task (so the exact version is fixed), plus any extensions.
- **Only your own signature binds you.** A rule in the terms decides when the agreement, or a clone of it, comes into force; no rule ever decides that someone owes something they did not sign.
- **Everyone signs the founding terms.** Founding terms exist only once every party has signed them, and nobody is added to a collective without signing.
- **A deal** is an agreement that founds no collective. It changes only with every party's signature. Majorities, layer rules, areas and tiers belong to collectives, never to deals.
- **An agreement may be a party of another.** A collective signs through a member, under its rule for Agreements; a deal consents when its own parties sign under its own rule. Named by its line, it carries what it signed through every later version; loops are refused, a verifier that cannot follow every layer answering unknown, and nesting is bounded at eight layers.
- **A deal ends by a clone** every party signs, stating where its shares went and what is still owed; a deal has no closing act.
- **A deal's version, once relied on, stays complete.** Once a party's or a keeper's later act relies on a complete version (a split service's receipt naming it, a split dividing by it, a keeper's record of it, a later version cloning it), the version keeps its completeness for everyone, whatever a signer's later rotation disowns; the disowned signature is shown as a dispute, the relying act its place. *A deal has no line of its own; "what others relied on stands".*
- **A judge never handles what it judges.** A specification named for condition evaluation, time reference, anchoring or absence proof (the judicial tasks) serves no other task and is not an extension, in a deal as in a collective.
- **What you sign is what you saw.** Before signing, clients show the plain text, with bidirectional controls visible (invisible characters that can reorder how text displays), and what any rule computes.
- **Clone, never modify.** An agreement is never edited. A change is a clone: a new version naming the old one as its parent.
  - A clone states which rules its changes require and whose signatures meet them; this statement is its mark, and a false mark makes the clone invalid.
  - A clone touching several layer rules or areas needs each one's rule, and comes into force all at once.
  - It is a draft until those signatures are in; then it closes its parent.
  - A clone never reduces a share of a stake without its holder's signature, member or not. Nor does a version of a collective's split agreement lower what the collective's stake in itself is paid, by raising what it takes off the top or changing its split cMIP, without the signature of each holder it pays less who has no voice in the power making that change: a departed holder, a stake successor or a buyer alike. **No advance permission reaches what one holder is paid unlike the others**: a holders' rule the holder accepted, by signing or by buying, reaches what every holder of the stake is paid alike ("Owners", below), and nothing reaches what one holder is paid unlike the others, which that holder alone signs; abandonment moves a share only by succession, and never lowers its pay while it stays with its holder. A share added by an issuance under the holders' rule is not "paid less"; one added any other way still is. A deal's clone needs every party. *Stated cost: a holder of a work's stake who is no party, a buyer of a share, is paid the owners' line of each publication the parties sign, without signing it.*
  - **Two complete versions of one deal.** *Most of the time a deal has one line of versions, and the latest is in force.* Rarely, every party signs two new versions of the same version, and the deal has two branches. While it does, **the version before the split is the reference**: the last version every party agrees on. **A new act may follow either branch, and counts**: a split, a sale under either branch stands, since every party signed both; *the buyer is protected from the parties' human error.* **For an ownership agreement, the shares a payment pays are the reference's until the fork is settled**: a split service's receipt naming another version of the fork is a wrong receipt. A branch that grows settles nothing. **The split is settled by a version that names every tip it drops**: beside its one parent, the latest version of every branch it drops, which it also cites; **the parties of all the branches sign it**, so every signer saw every option and nobody on a dropped side is dropped without consent. **A settlement is final for everything its signers could see**, which, with no clock, is what its history holds: a version on a dropped branch it did not hold, uploaded late, kept back, or grown there by a party left alone, a whole branch it never saw, and a second settlement made after it reopen nothing; only a new version on the line in force changes the deal. *Stated cost: whoever signed a version the settlement's signers could not see loses it; nobody who settled in good faith loses anything.* A settlement that holds a newer version of a branch than the one it names settles nothing; one naming a version the verifier does not hold is read as a plain version until that version is held. **Where the fork turns tangled** (a branch splitting again, three versions of one, two competing settlements of the parties), **the deal stays on its reference until one settlement naming every tip**: the parties lose what the tangled versions changed. Where the parties cannot agree, **the judge of forks the reference names may settle the fork, only once a party who signed it asks**, weighing the keepers' record of who signed what and when; never on its own initiative; it follows the chain of judgment where it does not act in time, and speaks once per fork; **where the reference names no judge of forks, none settles it.** *Client conformance:* a seller's client and a split service raise the alarm when a payment names a version of the deal that does not descend from the version they hold, or one they do not hold, showing both branches and pointing to settling the fork; a payment naming an older version raises a plain notice, the alarm kept for forks; a holder's client raises the alarm when the numbers on a service's splits skip, one numbering running across every split under a publication, and a split with no number, or a number another carries, breaks the plan; and, a strong SHOULD, a buyer's client checks that the publication and price tag it pays are the latest in their chains.
  - In a collective, the clauses that could move a member's stake or voice or decide a dispute change only with every member's signature, and there is one version of them for everyone: the abandonment clause, the succession plans, the keepers, the time reference, the arbitrators or verifiers, the condition, anchoring and absence-proof cMIPs, the chain of judgment, and, outside the terms, the collective's own split agreement. These are the protected clauses. *A member who will not sign holds the change back; that standoff is settled by talking, or by a fork of the collective.*
  - Activity on a fading cMIP moves to another by cloning.

### Claims and ownership

- **A claim binds a work to its creators**: "I claim to have made this." A bake is a neutral carrier: carrying, quoting or linking a work claims nothing, and a bake says only who uploaded it. The core records claims and their order, never who is right; order is not evidence of authorship. Conflicting claims are decided only where the claims meet: by anchoring, or by a keeper that recorded both. *A claim is used to build agreements and as a defence; with no claim and no agreement built on one, nothing in Agreements speaks for the work, and a claim even with an anchor proves only "first on MOR": MOR is not a court.*
- **An ownership agreement says who owns what, and how the owners decide**: terms carrying a target, `[kind, hash]`, one act type for two targets. Kind 0 is **a claim**: a work's ownership agreement, a deal. Kind 1 is **the identity it founds**, written `[1, null]`, this collective: a collective's ownership agreement, its founding and every clone of it, which also carries its key grammar, its rule for each layer and its finer areas. A work's refuses the collective's fields; a collective's requires them. It carries no split, no fees and no split service: **ownership is one thing, distribution another.** Where no ownership agreement targets a claim, the default holder is the creator who opened it. **A claim a member signs for a collective is the collective's**, the collective its opener and a creator, members listed beside it as co-creators where they wish.
- **The stake** is on the target, one per ownership agreement, written in millionths and summing to exactly 1,000,000. Leftover units of a payout go to the holders whose exact shares were cut the most; ties take turns, counted along the split service's receipts across every version, a new holder starting at zero; the order of the list decides nothing.
  - Any act that changes a share needs its holder's signature.
  - A transfer is signed by the seller and the buyer, and names the agreement's chain; the seller signs it by a chain signature too, which places it on the seller's own line, a collective by its chain key under its key grammar; the other holders are notified. **A share's payouts become its buyer's once the transfer is complete, both signatures placed.** For a collective's owners' matters, a transfer counts only where the collective's line placed it, and the buyer's signature is kept there whatever the buyer's later rotation disowns; money follows transfers as before.
  - **A vow has an owner: its signer.** An ownership agreement on a claim of a vow's name gives the vow's signer the whole stake at its founding; a platform's publication under it takes its share as a partner, off the top. A publication selling a vow and naming no ownership agreement is read as a lone seller's: its drafter is its owners' side, whoever signed the vow. *Stated cost: a vow's owner is paid only where she drafts the publication, is its partner, or claims the vow under an ownership agreement the publication names; her client flags a publication selling her vow that pays her nothing, "No owner", before she signs anything about it.* *Somebody owns the vow. Budgets are not vows: the vote placing a common budget is a cMIP's, and the vows answering the need are sold with their owners.*
- **Public domain.** The owners may release a work to the public domain by a release act, at once or at a point it names on the time reference: the claim ends, nobody earning from the work as its owner; its history, who made it and who owned it, stays named; and its content key is made public, or delivered at that point by an identity the act names. It needs every direct owner of the work, unless a release rule set at founding says otherwise, its threshold counted by share, never by head, which only a clone every owner signs changes; an owner that is a collective signs by its own rules, under its rule for every layer a release touches, and its release, like its sale, of a share it holds is an owners' matter for the holders of its stake in itself. A claim the release does not name is shown beside it, openly contested. *Cost, stated, for a timed release: the key's delivery rests on the identity named; the owners may always make it public earlier.*
- *A sale is tied to the seller's ownership agreement, not to the work's claim alone: the core cannot tie a sale to the one right ownership without judging which claim is the claim. It makes a sale outside the claim's ownership agreement visible, and gives builders the pieces to refuse or rate such sales.* **A publication, or a bare bake's price tag, not signed under the ownership agreement of the work's claim, is a valid act visibly contradicting it; a payment following it is a purchase under it, read as the signer's own sale and shown outside that ownership agreement, nothing owed back to the buyer.**

### Publications, splits and time

- **The publication is the offer**: "this is how we agree to publish it with these specified partners." It names what it sells (bakes; a vow, a promised delivery; or a proven delivery under a cMIP's terms), the ownership agreement whose holders are its owners (by its founding version), its partners (any identity, each with its share and an optional label, which nothing reads), its split service, its split plan, and its terms (words, deadline, refund terms, on its time reference). Every party signs it: the owners' side, each partner, the split service and its backups. **Self-publishing included**: an owner may be its own split service, with no partner. **One work, one ownership agreement, many publications**, each with its own price, partners and split, the owners' shares read by reference in all.
- **Its price is a price tag** (Money): signed by its split service, or by a party it designates, naming it; a tag naming it signed by anyone else is nobody's offer under it. **A payment naming such a tag is a purchase under the publication**, so a Money-only wallet can make a valid purchase. A payment naming the publication itself, with no tag, is a tip through it: divided as a sale is, buying nothing. *A tip to a work goes through the publication where it was found, which pays its partners and gives the owners their line. Unless there is a publication, the tip goes to the uploader, with no split possible.*
- **Two stops, two different people's.** **A publication is ended by a later version that sells nothing**, a version every party signs, so no party ends it alone. **A price tag is withdrawn by its signer**, the publication's side, alone. Past sales stand. **A payment racing the ending is owed back by the split service from what it holds, then by each party in the share it received**, on the owners' line each holder paid, their own payout, each an open obligation. **A publication whose ownership agreement names a closed collective as owner, or names an ownership agreement itself closed, sells nothing from the closing's line**; a payment after it is owed back.
- **The deal is complete** when the key is delivered or, for a bake whose key is already public, when the split service receipts the payment naming the tag. Otherwise a refund is owed; for an anonymous payer, it is owed to whoever signs with the key the payment committed to (Money).
- **Two classes of delivery.** A **proven** delivery leaves a proof any verifier checks from data alone, no one's word trusted: it is sold in the access form, naming the cMIP whose checker reads the proof, and the verifier checks the proof, never the cMIP's say-so; a cMIP that checks proofs of delivery declares so in its specification. A **promised** delivery names a vow, and each sale is confirmed or contested by those who signed onto it. What is sold decides the class: a key delivered in keeping a vow is evidence beside the buyer's confirmation or contest, never replacing it.
- **Shares by reference, from one named version.** The split service's receipt of a purchase names, in its field 9, the ownership agreement and a version on its line; **the version in force for that receipt is the latest complete version on that line its history holds**; the owners' line of that payment is divided by its stake. **A receipt naming a version on no line of the agreement, or a fork's branch other than its reference, is a wrong receipt.** A publication names the ownership agreement by its founding version and reads its line. *A share change racing a payment applies from the next payment; the trail shows which version paid.* **Which version of the publication a payment buys is settled where the payment takes place**: on a request rail, the tag and version the seller's request committed to; on a push rail, the split service's own receipt, before or after its signature on the ending. **The payment decides**: a receipt naming another tag or version than the payment's commitment is a wrong receipt, shown as such, counting for nothing. Until a verifier has the rail's answer, such a payment is pending. *The burden is passed to the step where the action truly takes place. No payment is left undetermined.*
- **Splits.** The split service receives every payment under the publication, in its own name, at its own pointer (the tag's payee), and pays everyone: **partners first, then role, metric and fee shares, all off the top, then the owners' line**, divided by the shares. *No partner holds others' money.* **A tag whose payee is not the split service bypasses it**: the payment is still a purchase; the pointer is shown as bypassing, and what the other shares would have been is an open obligation of the identity that received it.
  - **Attempts run per payment, not by stages**: the publication may name backups of its split service, the first service tried first, the next only where that attempt failed as the split cMIP defines failure, the next payment from the first again. **Money goes only to an address the parties signed**: a cMIP chooses among them, never invents one. **Each participant checks**, once a purchase triggers the publication, whether its cut is at its flow address, or its vault where needed. *Backups are not judgement: they are no link of the chain of judgment.*
  - **An owner that is a collective is paid at its own pointer**, which leads to the split service its own **split agreement** names: a publication-shaped act selling nothing, pointing at the collective's ownership agreement, a protected clause; *it adds one layer of splitting when the money gets to that identity.* Where a collective names one, its pointer counts, for Agreements, only if every address in it also appears in the service's own signed pointer in force, and every vault entry in the service's own vault, or a backup's. *Stated cost: a wallet that reads only Money cannot check it.* An owner that is another ownership agreement (a compilation's parts) is paid through it, its part divided by its own stake, read by reference. **A collective may keep money for itself, off the top of its own split agreement**, by naming itself as a partner; that part is paid to its vault and never split again, and is invalid where the collective declares no vault. Raising that part pays its holders less, an owners' matter; spending it is the collective's business.
  - For every incoming receipt or payer's claim, the service signs a split that sums exactly, with evidence for every role share signed by someone other than the service and the payee, **counting only when the payer the payment's commitment names acknowledges it in its claim** (a relay's delivery record, a service's use record, whatever cMIP defines it: a helper's word alone would let it claim help it never gave; never the payer a receipt names, which the service writes), and pays each payout as a simple Money payment, deducting no more than the plan's maximum fee. Every split is delivered to every holder and partner it pays, member or departed, and names each fee and who received it: the core cannot know who owns a service, so it never hides a fee. *A role share is a share for a role filled at payment time, such as the reposter who led to the sale.*
  - A rail Module's role share is the one exception: its evidence is the receipt or claim naming that rail Module, which counts even when the service itself signs it, provided its own pointer or vault names that Module. *The service cannot invent a Module it never named. Cost, stated: where its pointer names two rail Modules for one rail, the service, which issues the invoice, chooses which of them earns; the pointer shows it publicly, and the publication's terms can constrain it.* The use of a service someone runs is evidenced by a record that identity signs, which pays nothing on its own: a service the parties choose in advance is a partner, one the payer chooses is paid once the payer acknowledges its record; a Module signs nothing.
  - Every payout matches its share exactly, but for one smallest unit of rounding per payout, every fee alike for every share; any deviation, either way, breaks the plan. It cannot pay outside the plan or change the plan. Receipts it has not split and payouts it has not paid are its open obligations.
  - A fee is one total with an agreed split, consented by whoever bears it; a fee left out of a split is visible. The split plan may cap the rail fee a payout loses; where it states no cap, there is none, and a conforming client flags the absence before signing. Amounts too small to send are held until they can be moved.
  - *No cMIP or Module can force a fee; developers earn through services, maintained originals, bounties and shares clients choose to pass on.*
- **Time.** Each agreement and each publication names one time reference, such as a block height, and its deadlines are judged on it. An anchor of the act itself, at or before the deadline, shows it met; a deadline is shown missed only by a named keeper's sequence anchored past the deadline that holds no record of the act in its records up to and including the anchored one; a keeper's word alone never says late; otherwise the answer is undetermined. With several keepers, the keepers' rule is read the other way round: met when it is met by keepers whose record of the act lies before one of their records anchored at or before the point, missed when enough anchored sequences lack the act that it can no longer be met; an anchor of the act wins over any keeper's silence (Agreements rule 33).
- **The chain of judgment.** For each judge it names (a condition, time reference or anchoring cMIP, a keeper, an arbitrator, the abandonment authority; the split service is none, its backups being attempts per payment), an agreement may name in its terms the ones that take over, in order, when it answers "unknown" or does not act within its period. Every link names a period, measured on the agreement's time reference, which the terms must name wherever a judge can stay silent; it runs from a signed request to the judge by someone with standing, and an answer after it counts for nothing. Everyone signs the chain with the terms, so a judge that fails is replaced with no new signature. *A client that does not know a judge's specification shows the question as unknown; that never passes it down the chain.*

### Keepers

- **Keepers** are signed ordering mechanisms the agreement names, serving proofs. Each records the acts around a deal as they arrive, one keeper record (type 2, `{ 0 => [act, signer] }`) per act, in one sequence of its operator's: it signs an order (this act, then this, then this rotation), checkable from any copy. Keepers are always sealed: they record act ids and signers, never content (Agreements rules 36 to 44, "Keeper record").
  - A keeper's record made before its record of a rotation keeps the recorded act visible as a dispute after that rotation, as does one made before its record of a grant's revocation (Agreements rule 76). A record counts only alongside the act it names, and only from a keeper named by an agreement the act belongs to, or, for an act of a collective placed against its own line, by the collective's agreement (Agreements, "Belongs to", rule 42). An act belongs to an agreement that it names, or an act of whose chain it names, in `objects`, or through the act it answers: a Money receipt or payment claim through the ownership agreement it pays the shares of and the obligation, price tag or publication it follows (or an act that belongs to one); an obligation through the agreement or publication it arises from; a publication through the ownership agreement it names, a price tag through its publication. An act belonging to no agreement (a post, a bake, a rotation) has no keeper: only an anchor places it.
  - A keeper keeps one sequence for its records; with several keepers, each record is judged against its own keeper's record of the rotation, and the agreement's rule counts them (Agreements rules 37, 38). A keeper's later rotation never undoes what its records placed, shown as a dispute (Agreements rule 42). The keepers that count for an act are those of the version in force for it: for a deal, the latest complete version the act's history holds; for a collective, its agreement in force at the act, every act for the collective belonging to it (Agreements, "Belongs to").
  - A keeper orders; it never times. Only anchors place anything on the clock: a keeper may anchor its own sequence, placing everything it recorded up to that record, and an agreement that needs deadlines may require it in its terms.
  - A keeper's records hold in every copy of them, so a keeper that disappears loses nothing it had recorded.
  - Third parties who must judge content are arbitrators or verifiers, given keys by ordinary key delivery.
  - *What remains to trust is that a keeper records acts as they arrive: a keeper that delays recording a rotation, or records an act late, is trusted not to (Agreements rule 42). Choose keepers as carefully as homes.*

### Collectives: an identity its members act for

*A collective is one identity, with a chain key and no signing key of its own, whose members act for it, each with their own key, under its ownership agreement. The rules below answer five questions in turn: who acts for it; how members come and go; who may decide what; who decides when someone is gone; and, with no clock, what was done before or after a member left.*

**Words used below.**
- **The collective's chain** is made of the acts done for it: each names the collective's identity as chain in `objects`, with the decision it acts under and the other acts it knows. A member's own sequences are never read for it.
- A **record** is the collective's everyday act, which any member draws, that writes a complete clone of its agreement on its chain, naming the signature acts that complete it, and registers its members' departures and rotations. It decides nothing by itself.
- A **line** is an act of the collective (a record, a rotation of the collective, a fork or a closing) at which a change in who may act for it takes effect. What it names on the collective's chain are its **tips**, and everything they reach is its **history**.
- To **place** an act is to give it a position on the collective's chain, so that it is before or after a line.
- An area or a layer's rule is **frozen** when no actor is left; its **refit** is the change that gives it actors again.

**No key of its own.**
- A collective is an identity with a chain key and **no signing key**: its genesis declares its founding agreement in place of a first signing key and is signed by a chain key, which signs rotations, chain signatures and such a genesis, nothing else (Identity rules 1 and 7). Its chain key is held under its key grammar: by one holder, by a custodian, or as shares any k of n members can rebuild. *A one-way door in the format, written knowing it.*
- **A member acts for the collective with their own key, the act naming the collective**, and Agreements judges whether the signer may act there; an act that does not name it is the member's own; a stranger's act naming it is nobody's. *The collective grants powers to its members, not to keys: there is no member key, and no grant key of a collective.*
- Every key grammar leaves a way to rotate that needs fewer than every member: a threshold below the member count, or a named recovery path. **A single chain-key holder still needs a named successor and an escrowed share**: no key of its own is not no key. *The rule is about the exit, not the number.*
- **A collective is sealed to by sealing to each of its members, or by publicity**; a collective has no encryption key. **Its homeless rotation, signed by its chain key, is its own escape endorsement** (Identity section 11), decided by Identity from its own data. *Stated cost: an old home's objection never voids a collective's homeless rotation; what protects a collective from a thief is its key grammar, k shares, not its homes.*

**Who acts for it: every layer named.** **A collective's founding names, for each of five layers (Text, Envelopes, Money, Agreements, Development), who acts for it there**: a rule in the terms' own grammar (any one member alone, any k of them, these named members together). A founding that leaves a layer unnamed is outside the format and founds nothing, and one naming a layer the MIRs do not define likewise. Identity's acts of a collective are its chain acts, governed by the key grammar. A layer's rule governs that layer's everyday acts and, in Agreements, grants and revocations, which reach no further than their issuer's power; it never governs a clone, a fork or a closing, which take the rules their changes need, nor a record, which any member draws. **Areas** finer than a layer, by act type or operational field, may narrow it, each held by some members with a number of them who must agree, counted by head in MOR 1. *Client conformance:* **before a party signs a founding, its client shows, layer by layer, what the rule lets one member do alone, Money first.** *The risk of a silent default is between members; a choice written and signed by all answers it.*
- **Choosing specifications.** For each task a MIR hands to a cMIP, the collective's agreement names the cMIP it uses, adopted under the rule of that task's layer; an extension under the rule of Development and of every layer it declares. A specification the agreement names nowhere counts for nothing for the collective, unless an area names its act type. Four tasks judge rather than act (condition evaluation, time reference, anchoring and absence proof); which specification fills them changes only with every member's signature.
- **When a holder goes.** A holder leaves an area by a constitutional change, or steps down at once, alone; co-holders carry on. An area, or a layer whose named actors have all left, stands frozen until a constitutional clone names others, shown as an emergency; a departure that empties it ends every grant in it at that line. At the refit, the new holders reinstate the grants they choose by cloning them, one by one.

**Members come and go.**
- **Members change by a clone of the collective's agreement put in force by a record that names its signature acts; and by a rotation of the chain key where the clone changes the key grammar or removes a party holding the key or a share of it**, the rotation's history that of the signature acts it names. A clone put in force by a record where a rotation was needed puts nothing in force.
- A rotation that declares no new agreement changes the chain key, not rules: the agreement in force carries forward, with the clones already recorded.
- A member can always leave alone, giving up their voice and keeping their share. **A member's power ends at the line that registers their departure; their own key continues to sign their own acts.** Removal, where a constitution allows it, takes the voice, never the share.
- **A broken collective, and its way back.** A collective is broken when its chain key moved by a rotation whose new version of the agreement fails Agreements' checks (not complete with the signatures it names, a false mark, not a new version of the one in force): Identity counts the rotation, but Agreements find no agreement in force. Every act whose history holds the broken act, and not the rollback, stays shown and counts for nothing, for good. The way back is a **rollback**: a later rotation declares a new version of the agreement in force just before the broken act, naming the broken act; it needs that agreement's constitutional change rule, counted among the voices that remain. *Client conformance:* a client never proposes a rollback unless Agreements read the collective as broken. *Costs, stated:* a member whose signature the rollback needs can ask a price for it; holders of enough of the chain key can break the collective on purpose and refuse the repair. **A broken collective keeps exactly what its rules before the break allowed, a payment judged by the publication it was made under**; a payment under a publication the collective signed during the stretch is owed back to its payer unless the sale is signed anew after the rollback, a visible open obligation repaid like any debt (Money rule 17), a refund to an anonymous payer's bare key repaid where its claim said; while it is broken no fork or closing counts, and it cannot close while money from the stretch is owed back, save money owed back to nobody and money owed back to a payer who gave no address, after a notice sealed to them with a deadline, visible and unpaid. **The rollback may register a declaration of absence made during the stretch.** **A declaration counts only if it is public, or addressed to the member it names.** **A resignation is spent once its signer comes back.**
- **Leaving a broken collective.** A member can leave a broken collective too: the resignation names the agreement in force just before the broken act, and takes effect at the collective's next valid line, normally the rollback. *Client conformance:* where the member leaving is the last voice that remains, the client says so in plain words before the resignation is signed: the works will be frozen; and where the member leaving is the last holder of constitutional power while others keep voices in their areas, it says: "You are about to break the collective's constitutional layer. Once you leave, nobody will be able to change its rules again. The other members keep acting in their areas.". *Cost, stated:* when the last voice has left, the collective's works freeze as they stand.
- **Departed holders.** The collective's stake decides every holder's money; its departed members entry records only who left, without voice. A departed holder has no control and no veto: the collective's current rules and judges apply to them; it is consulted on the owners' matters, by the stake's holders' rule where it accepted one, else by its own signature. Their share never shrinks without their signature, nor is what it is paid lowered without it, save alike with every holder under a holders' rule they accepted, and is a share of all the collective's income. Declared absent, a departed holder's share passes to its stake successor, or, with none named, is redistributed. Every term applies equally to every share, member or departed; unpaid shares stay open debts until the holder can receive.
- A removal takes only the seat part of the removed member's succession plan with it; the plan for their share stays with the share.
- **How a collective ends.** Four shapes.
  - *A group splits off* with the powers to do so: an ordinary change of members; the collective keeps its identity, and those who leave become departed holders.
  - *The fork proper*, where both groups want changes and disagree: each side first founds its successor; then a fork act naming the successors is drawn by a member and chain-signed by the members on its sides, under the constitutional change rule; each successor a debt is assigned to chain-signs it too. Where the founders set a rule below every member, a member who signs no side keeps their share in each successor as a departed holder. Once it is complete the original is closed: nothing done for it afterwards counts. Its shares in works pass to the successors, in the shares the act names, by default by the members' shares; departed holders keep their share in every one of them; the fork cites the collective's chain up to its line, and hands out every obligation in that history, or it does not take effect; whatever lies outside that history is void; **a complete fork or closing is final**, a later ending naming it counting for nothing; two endings sharing no signer and neither naming the other are concurrent, neither counting until a third names both; every grant ends; the original's publications sell nothing from the line. Each successor names the original as "forked from", a back-link only.
  - *Actively abandoned*: a plain collective that keeps paying its holders until winding it down makes sense.
  - *Dissolution*: its shares in works are sold or released; then a closing act, chain-signed under the constitutional change rule, ends a collective that holds nothing and owes nothing. A collective cannot close while it owes anything, save what the closing names as left open; an absentee's money share follows the collective's own rules and stays visible wherever they sent it. *Whether a fork or closing took effect is judged by its own history only.*
- **An act for a collective is done only once everyone can read it.** It binds the collective only once sealed to every member (or public) and on its chain, citing its latest decision as its signer knew it. Before that, even signed, it is planning. Where it is held never matters. **Owners without a say see its Money acts**: where the collective's stake in itself has holders who are no members, a Money act for it is done only once sealed to every holder of that stake as well, or public. *One thing is to act, another to read. Cost, stated: a collective keeps no secret from its own members, and anyone can see that it acts, never what.*
- **A debt ends by payment or by its creditor's word.** A creditor may end an obligation without full payment by a creditor's release, which only the creditor signs: a Money act, signed for a collective creditor under its rule for Money alone; where it is traded for future terms, those terms are an Agreements agreement of their own. A collective that cannot pay can settle so; its members are never personal debtors of what it owes.
- Every complete clone is written on the collective's chain at once. Only clones that change the key grammar, or remove a holder of the key or a share of it, rotate the chain key.

**Owners: what a stake's holders decide together.** *A collective's income can belong to people who do not run it: a member who left, a successor, a buyer of part of the stake. Each of these rules is optional: with none of them chosen, every holder signs for itself.*
- **The holders' rule** (Agreements rule 94): how the stake's holders consent together, every holder (the default) or owners holding together at least a stated share; it reaches only the owners' matters, binds only holders who accepted it, and changes only with every holder. *Stated cost: an owner bound by it can be paid less without signing, alike with every other owner.*
- **The owners' matters** (rule 95): what the collective's split agreement pays the stake, the collective's own part among it; an issuance in it; a collective's transfer or release of a share it holds in a work; and a fork's completeness where a successor pays a kept holder less. *Prices and publications are no owners' matter: they are public, and the owners can leave.*
- **Counting by share.** A change rule may count "holding together at least this share of the stake"; one voice per member stays the default; layer rules and areas count heads.
- **Who holds what at the vote.** For the owners' matters, a transfer counts only where the collective's line placed it; a vote counts the roster at the line putting the version in force. *Stated cost: a buyer has no vote until the collective places its transfer; members can delay it, visibly, until the next record any member draws.*
- **Issuance, with pre-emption** (rule 96): new holders by a version naming its issuance offer, every existing share scaled alike, each existing holder first offered its proportion on the same terms; waived only by every holder. *Stated cost: an owner who does not take up its proportion is diluted.*
- **Proxies** (rule 97): a holder may hand its vote on the owners' matters to a proxy; the owner's own vote always beats the proxy's; never on what needs every holder, nor on the owner's money or property, and no sub-proxies.
- **Owners may choose who runs the collective, as a founding choice** (rule 69): its constitutional change rule may count the power over membership among the stake's holders; it binds only through the chain key, and such a constitution SHOULD put a share of the chain key, or the recovery custodian, under the owners' control. *Cost, stated: a majority of capital could remove those doing the work, chosen at founding.*
- **A collective's only.** A deal carries no holders' rule and no proxy; in a deal, the version carrying an owners' matter names the transfers it counts, and an issuance is signed by every holder.

**Three tiers of rules.** Deals have no tiers; a collective's rules fall in three:
- **Constitutional:** who decides (the parties, the change rules, the key grammar, the layer rules, the areas). It changes by the constitutional change rule, which is every party unless the founders agreed otherwise.
- **Judicial:** who judges and by what: the protected clauses. It changes only with every member's signature, one version for everyone.
- **Operational:** everything else. It changes by the clone rule, or, where a layer's rule or an area covers it, by that rule.
- Which tier and which rule a clone touches is read from the fields it changes, never from what it says about itself.

**Nobody's absence freezes the collective.**
- Every member whose signature the constitutional change rule counts is covered by an abandonment clause that can remove their voice, so a lost member never freezes the constitution.
- Where the constitutional change rule counts membership among the stake's holders, the abandonment clause must allow moving a vanished holder's share (outcome 1, redistributing it), or the terms are invalid, as for parties.
- When fewer voices remain than a rule's number asks for, all the remaining voices meet it.

**Before and after, for a collective.** *There is no clock. So when a member leaves, or rotates their own keys, the collective draws the line: an act any member draws, after which the change counts. Everything done for it is then before or after that line by what the line cites, never by a member's personal devices, which nobody else can see.*
- **Two chains.** A collective keeps a chain of decisions (its founding, the changes to its terms, who joins and leaves, its grants and their revocations, its fork or closing), **each naming the previous decision**, and a chain of actions (its debts, payments, bakes, publications, releases, sales, and its grantees' acts), each citing the decision it acts under and the other acts it joins. A decision naming none, or one that is no decision of the collective, is no decision. An act within its signer's powers counts for the collective as soon as it is done and cites the collective's latest decision as its signer knew it. *"The chain makes acts unmissable, not confirmed."*
- **The line.** An act for the collective is before a line when the history the line cites holds it. **Unknown is not after**: while an act the line's history names is not held, whether an act is before that line is unknown, and nothing is void on it.
- **The ending wins.** Where a decision ending powers (a fork, a closing, a departure, a grant's revocation, a departure emptying an area) and an action using them cite neither each other, the action is void, or, against a grant's revocation, void or disputed. **The ending wins only over acts the collective never took on**: an act a counting act for the collective cites is adopted. *A counterparty protects itself by waiting until the collective's chain visibly moves past its act before delivering. Client conformance: a member's client signs no fork or closing until it holds every member's latest acts for the collective.*
- **Departures take effect at the line.** A resignation, a stepping down or a declaration of absence takes effect at the line a record draws, which registers it. Until then the departed member still counts. **Once the line registers it, it stays placed for the collective**, whatever their signers' later rotations disown, shown as a dispute on the line. *Cost, stated: a thief holding a member's signing key can resign the member.*
- **What puts a clone in force.** A record names the signatures that put its clone in force, and counts only with those; so does the rotation that declares a constitutional clone. Of two records drawn one after the other on new versions of the same parent, the second puts nothing in force; records on concurrent lines leave the parent in force until a clone of the latest clone of either branch is recorded after both.
- **Who else can place an act.** The collective's keepers may place its own acts that a line left out, never a member's signature or a deal. An acknowledgement by anyone else places nothing.
- **A member's own rotation** is registered the same way, at the collective's line: an act of the member's for the collective placed before that line stays valid for the collective, whatever the rotation kept or disowned; placed after it, it is judged by Identity alone.
- **What a departure does not undo.** A member who left can still complete an act the collective signed before its line; a signature placed before the line still counts for the act or version it signs, its signer still counted among the voices that remain for it; a declaration never undoes a clone a record or rotation put in force before the collective's line.

### Succession, grants and abandonment

- **Succession.** A party may attach a succession plan to an agreement: stake successors and seat successors, each divisible, possibly different people.
  - It is triggered by the party's own act or by the abandonment authority's declaration, and carried out as a clone.
  - In a collective, a seat passes automatically only when every member still counted signed that version of the plan; otherwise the successor is nominated and approved under the constitutional change rule. **A seat may pass to an identity already a party, which then holds both seats, every threshold unchanged; a key holding passes to exactly one successor**; anything else is no plan's alone, but an ordinary clone. *Client conformance:* before a party signs a succession plan, its client shows what each successor would hold after it.
  - Where no plan exists, a succession the party declared under Identity and its successor confirmed makes the successor a nomination for the party's seat, never an automatic seat; the share stays with the party's identity until a transfer or the abandonment clause moves it.
  - An automatic succession clone puts the successor in the departed member's place in the key grammar, every threshold unchanged, and drops the used plan; the rotation declaring it re-deals the chain key to include the successor; where the departed member held the chain key alone, that clone carries a succession plan for the successor, so the key always has a named successor.
  - A seat never carries the areas its holder held.
  - *The core gives the language; death is left to the parties and their named authorities.*
- **Grants** delegate authority, with limits, revocably, and never beyond the power of whoever issued it: a collective's grant reaches only what its signers could do together.
  - **A collective's grantee acts with its own key**, naming the collective and the grant; **a person's grant hands its grantee a grant key**, a key of the person scoped to the grant, added and removed by everyday acts, never a rotation. *A stolen grant key acts within its scope until revoked.*
  - **A grant's dials.** Its limits cMIP may set an expiry, judged on a time reference. Sub-granting stays excluded. Exclusivity is no grant but a promise written as a deal term; conditions are a cMIP's; licences, leases and trusts are publications, stakes and deals; a proxy is no grant. *MOR is not the law; on these matters it keeps the record.*
  - Revoking a grant ends it: a decision ending powers. An act the revocation's history holds binds; one racing it, or after it, is void, unless another identity, never the grantee itself, acknowledged it, or a keeper recorded it before recording the revocation: then it is valid and shown as disputed, and confers nothing. Void or disputed, it binds once the grantor adopts it. *Client conformance:* a revocation cites every act under the grant its signer holds.
  - A grant whose grantee is its own grantor is valid, its acceptance the grantor's: a person's grant to an application acting for it.
- **Abandonment** (when a party no longer answers) is decided only by the authority the clause names, always an identity.
  - The authority signs a declaration naming the agreement, the version of its clause the party signed last, the party, and the outcomes, judged against the time reference, under the clause in force.
  - Liveness acts prevent it; a contest objects to it.
  - One outcome removes the party's voice from then on, in a collective from the collective's line registering the declaration. It never takes their share, and never undoes a clone a record or rotation already put in force.
  - A declaration moves a share only by succession: the share, and its pay with it, goes to the stake successor the holder's plan names; with none named, outcome 1 redistributes it. **Outcome 2, a stake transferred to parties named, is struck from MOR 1.** Abandonment never lowers what a share is paid while it stays with its holder.
  - In a collective, a declaration may also name a holder of its stake in itself who is no party, with the share-moving outcomes only. *So a holder who vanished does not block every owners' matter for good.*
  - **In a collective,** the authority may be a threshold of the other parties, counted at the collective's line registering the declaration. Where no member but the declared one remains to draw a record, the recovery rotation removing them names the other signature acts.
  - **In a deal,** the authority is one identity (a party, a keeper's operator or a collective), never a threshold of the other parties.

### Disputes

- **Non-performance.** *The core never enforces; it makes default impossible to hide.*
- **Negotiation.** Two sides negotiate by negotiation messages, an Agreements act carrying text: each names the previous message of its thread and acknowledges the latest one received from the other side, so the record is provably complete up to the last acknowledged message. A text act in the thread is no part of the record: it cannot carry an acknowledgement. Either side may disclose the record by making its keys public. *Cost, stated: a client without Agreements shows a negotiation message as unknown; negotiating needs an Agreements client, as signing the deal does.*
- **Contests.** Only parties, affected holders and named keepers, arbitrators or verifiers have standing to contest, and the buyer of a purchase has standing to contest that purchase. A contest changes nothing about any act's validity: it is shown alongside what it names.
- **Tasks:** split (a publication's); condition evaluation; time reference; absence proof; grant limits; claims (anchoring is the Envelopes').


## MIR: Development

- **Specifications are named by the hash of their content,** wherever they are uploaded. A specification is uploaded as a public bake naming the Development MIR's spec hash as its media spec. cMIPs, Modules and MIPs name their creator inside, so names cannot collide or be stolen; a creator named by a specification it never uploaded, or whose rotation disowned the bake, is shown as unclaimed, whether another identity relied on the bake or not, the bake shown as disputed: authorship is the creator's own claim, which the rotation withdrew. MIRs have no creator: they are the protocol itself; nor have the MICS document and a test suite, specification kinds of their own (kinds 6 and 7), so their hashes are fixed at the freeze. A **MIP**, a proposal toward a successor protocol, is a kind of its own (kind 8), with a creator, naming in its predecessor field the MIR or MIP it forks; a listing shows MIPs in a part of their own, by what they fork; the predecessor is always required, a MIP proposing a new layer naming the MICS document, where layers are added. Signature schemes and units are specifications too.
- **What a specification must carry.** Per kind, a few fields are required: a cMIP's tasks where it fills one; a Module's cMIP, or the MIR it works under, field 5 naming either; a rail Module's kind and what its proof binds (fields 11 and 13); a MIP's predecessor. The rest serve discovery, the act types and dependencies a specification has being those its text gives. An uploading tool writes every required field and no optional one it cannot fill from the text, so two honest tools make one specification of one text; two bakes differing in optional fields are two specifications, shown as versions of one text. **A specification not in the format is no specification**: a verifier holding its content shows it so, and an act naming it is unknown to every client, never invalid unless a rule the verifier can apply shows it so.
- **Frozen at publication.** A changed specification is a new one, which may name its predecessor for migration; a successor by a different creator is shown as such.
- **Tasks are the contract** between the core and the community, each with one global number, by which an agreement may name the cMIP it uses: for each, the defining MIR states what a cMIP accepts and produces. **Extensions**, named in an agreement's own extensions field, cover what no task foresaw, adding rules and never relaxing the core's; an extension declares in its specification the layers it acts on, so a collective knows whose approval adopting or dropping it needs. **Xtypes** define new act types from core pieces, filling no task, and are shown as unknown until adopted.
- **Verification rules** that anyone must be able to run are programs for one frozen machine, RV32IM, with fixed memory, an exact step budget stated in the specification, a fixed input and output encoding, and no clocks, networks or randomness; a rule over budget, or outside the profile, answers unknown everywhere. A binary rule is carried as a locked object the specification names. The rule decides validity; the text explains it; a mismatch is a bug, fixed only by a new specification. **The profile** (memory layout, input and output encoding, entry point) **is a specification of its own, named by its hash and uploaded with test vectors, not a parameter fixed at the freeze**; until one is published, no rule is published under this promise, and each founding cMIP and Module with a verification rule says its rule is a reference implementation. *The profile evolves as a specification; freezing it would make little sense.* A rule names the profile it runs on: its reference (`rule-ref`) carries the profile's spec hash beside the rule's hash, size and budget, fixed before the freeze; the profile is a Module whose field 5 names the Development MIR.
- **Adoption by opt-in; no registry.** Discovery happens through use. Below the MICS the one trust root is the six frozen MIR texts, uploaded, and committed in the genesis repository, as canonical text (no trailing space; a file may end with one line break, which is not part of the text; a repository check refusing any that are not); a MIR's spec hash is `tagged_hash("MOR/spec", content)` over the content map `{ 0 => 4, 9 => text }` (Development rule 26); every client ships the texts and recomputes from them the six hashes it accepts, so two clients can differ only about a text. A fork presenting six others is a different protocol, and a successor must change the `MOR/` tag prefix; a successor's specifications have new hashes, and agreements name them afresh by cloning.
- **A proof checker is no task.** Each proven delivery names the cMIP whose checker reads its proof, so no agreement sets one checker for all its deliveries; a cMIP that checks proofs of delivery declares so by one field of its specification, so checkers can be found; the field serves discovery only, a verifier asking the checker of whatever cMIP the publication names.
- **Earning through signed terms.** Creators earn through fees and role shares evidenced by signed records: a creator who charges states its fee in its specification, for information, and only an agreement's own fee entry makes it owed; nothing is taken from a payment without a signature.
- **Evidence of use comes from a party, never from a Module.** A Module is a specification: it signs nothing. That a rail Module carried a payment is shown by the receipt or claim naming it, signed by the receiver or the payer; that a service someone runs was used is shown by a record signed by the identity running it. Several Modules may work under the one cMIP an agreement names for a task, such as several rail Modules under the payment cMIP, one per rail.

*Zero democracy on the MICS; a free market on cMIPs and Modules, where adoption decides.*

## MOR 2

*A core frozen forever also freezes its cryptography, so a successor protocol is certain in the long run.* MOR is built to be a good ancestor:

- the post-quantum chain key keeps identities continuous through a break of elliptic-curve signatures, and signing keys migrate to a post-quantum scheme by adoption, before a break, since afterwards everything signed with the old scheme can be forged and only owners holding both keys still leave;
- succession declared in a rotation, and two-way links, let identities move and be confirmed from both sides;
- agreements are always in core format, so they can be cloned across: a work crosses as its bake, its claim and its ownership agreement, while its publications, which are distribution, are remade;
- anchoring before a break keeps history trustworthy, at each party's choice: the chain key secures the exit, not what is left behind;
- extensions in wide use show MOR 2 which tasks it needs;
- a successor changes the tag prefix, so nothing replays across the two;
- proposals toward it are MIPs, a specification kind of their own, each naming the MIR or MIP it forks, so forks are seen as forks.

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Acknowledgement | An act naming another identity's act as received; counts only alongside that act; carried only by Identity, Money and Agreements act types. |
| Act | Any signed object: an outside, a locked inside, and a signature. |
| Agreement | Terms plus the signatures its rules require, binding those who signed. |
| Area | A part of a layer, finer than it, by act type or operational field, that a collective's agreement gives to some members, who alone decide there and may grant within it, a number of them agreeing. Each keeps a permanent id from version to version. Optional: the layer rules come first. |
| Audit | A home's log summary checked and cosigned by another operator. |
| Bake | Media made into an act (Envelopes type 0): it says only who uploaded it, and claims nothing. It carries no price; money sent to a bare bake is its uploader's. Never withdrawn. |
| Belongs to (Agreements) | An act belongs to an agreement when it names it, or an act of its chain, in `objects`, or through the act it answers: a receipt or payment claim through the ownership agreement whose shares it pays and the obligation, price tag or publication it follows, or an act that belongs to one; an obligation through the agreement or publication it arises from; a publication through the ownership agreement it names, a price tag through its publication. Each act named in `objects` (chain or predecessor) and each act answered is one step, whatever it is; an Identity act ends the path. Its keepers' records count for it: those of the version in force for the act; every act for a collective belongs to the collective's agreement. |
| Chain | The acts on one object, each naming the chain and the act it follows. |
| Claim | An Agreements act (type 3) binding a work, by its work hash, to its creators: "I claim to have made this." The core records claims and their order, never who is right. |
| Client | Software that implements Identity, Envelopes and Text, plus any other MIRs it chooses. |
| Closing | The act that ends a collective holding nothing and owing nothing, drawn by a member and chain-signed under the constitutional change rule; after its line nothing done for the collective counts in Agreements. |
| Creditor's release | The Money act by which a creditor, alone, ends an obligation owed to it without full payment; a collective signs it under its rule for Money. |
| Decisions chain, actions chain | A collective's two chains: its decisions (founding, changes to its terms, who joins and leaves, its grants and their revocations, its fork or closing), each naming the previous decision, and every other act done for it, each citing the decision it acts under and the actions it follows. |
| Done | Of an act for a collective: sealed to every member (or public) and on its chain, citing its latest decision, wherever it is held. Before that it binds no one. Records, forks and closings too. A Money act of a collective whose stake in itself has holders who are no members is sealed to them too, or public. |
| Everyday act | Every act other than an identity-chain act (genesis, a rotation, a chain signature): signed with a signing key or a scoped key, it sits in a sequence, carrying `prev`, a position and a running summary. |
| Purchase | A payment naming a counted price tag: of a bare bake, by its uploader; or of a publication, by its split service or the party it designates. A Money-only wallet can make one. |
| Proven delivery, promised delivery | The two classes of what a publication sells: a proven delivery leaves a proof any verifier checks from data alone, under the access form; a promised delivery names a vow, its sales confirmed or contested by their signers. |
| cMIP | A community specification for one task, an extension, or an xtype. Frozen once uploaded. |
| Closure | A declaration in a rotation that the signer's home stops serving. |
| Collective | An identity with a chain key and no signing key, whose genesis declares its founding agreement; its members act for it, each with their own key, naming it, judged in Agreements under its rule for each layer. |
| Chain of judgment | For a judge an agreement names, those that take over, in order, when it answers "unknown" or does not act within its period; signed by everyone with the terms. |
| Contest | A signed objection to an act, by someone with standing; changes nothing. |
| Contested (Identity) | Four things, each with its own noun: a contested position of an identity chain, where no rotation counts while a conflict stands (for good at a self-hosted identity, left by the escape); a contested rotation, one competing there; a contesting receipt, voided by its operator's rotation and acknowledged, shown as a dispute and deciding nothing; contested routes, two routes acts naming one predecessor. Agreements' contest act is none of these. |
| Conversion service | An identity that receives on one rail or unit and pays on another, under its own publication. |
| Deal | An agreement that founds no collective; it exists and changes only with every party's signature. |
| Departed holder | Someone who left a collective keeping a stake: a share of all its income, with no voice and no veto, consulted on the owners' matters by its holders' rule or its own signature. |
| Disputed | The status of an act a rotation voided, or a grant's revocation made void, that another identity acknowledged or a keeper recorded first: valid, shown as disputed; set only by Identity's rotation rules and a grant's revocation (Agreements rule 76). |
| Encryption key | The public key an identity makes public to receive private content. A collective has none: it is sealed to through its members. |
| Extension | A cMIP named by an agreement in its extensions field, outside the listed tasks; it adds rules, never relaxes the core's. A proven delivery's checker is none. |
| Concurrency rule | Retired: the field is withdrawn. Every fork of records is settled by the default: the parent stays in force until a later clone resolves it. |
| Fork of a collective | The end of a collective whose members split into sides that will not agree: each side founds its successor first; a fork act naming them, signed under the constitutional change rule, closes the original; each successor names it as "forked from", a back-link only. Not a fork of records. |
| Fulfils (Money) | What a receipt's field 5 or a claim's field 4 names: the act the payment follows. Money judges it only where a rule of Money or Agreements gives a payment following that act a meaning (an obligation, a price tag, a publication, a payee pointer, a stake transfer, a payment it repays or refunds, a take-up, a split, for instance); naming any other act, a payment fulfils nothing in Money's sense. |
| Grant | A delegation of authority, with limits, revocable. A collective's grantee acts with its own key, naming the collective and the grant; a person's grant hands its grantee a grant key. Its limits may include an expiry. A revocation voids the acts under it it does not hold, except those another identity acknowledged or a keeper recorded first, shown as disputed and conferring nothing unless the grantor adopts them. |
| Grant key | A scoped key of a person, scoped to one grant, made and held by the grantee; what it signs is the person's own act. A revocation removes it. A collective has none. |
| Holders' rule | How a collective's stake's holders consent together on its owners' matters: every holder, the default, or owners holding together at least a stated share; binding only holders who accepted it, changed only by every holder; it may carry a standing waiver of pre-emption, set or removed only by every holder. |
| Home | A relay focused on identity, run by an operator, storing identity chains and signing receipts. |
| Homeless rotation | A rotation that leaves homes that are gone, voided by a live home's objection unless endorsed with both keys; final once the next rotation counts under the home rule the homeless rotation declared, except one accepted only on a client's own failed attempt to reach the home. |
| Identity | The hash of a genesis act. |
| Inbox | A route where others deliver acts addressed to an identity. |
| Issuance | A version of a collective's agreement adding new holders to a stake, every existing share scaled alike, by the holders' rule, each holder first offered its proportion by an issuance offer (type 26) naming its period. |
| Keeper | An identity's relay an agreement names to record the acts around it as they arrive: act ids and signers, never content, one keeper record per act, in one signed sequence. It orders; it never times: only anchors place anything on the clock. |
| Layer, layer rule | One of the six layers, one per MIR, numbered 0 Identity, 1 Text, 2 Envelopes, 3 Money, 4 Agreements, 5 Development. A collective's founding names, for each of the five above Identity, the rule saying who acts for it there; Identity's acts are its chain acts, under the key grammar. |
| Keeper record | A keeper's signed statement (Agreements type 2) naming one act and its signer; counts only alongside that act, for an act that belongs to an agreement naming the keeper (or, placed against a collective's own line, named by the collective's agreement), and only while the keeper's chain keeps it, save where a rule read it to place an act, a relied-on version's completeness rests on it, or a collective's line placed it (Agreements rule 42; "Made before, made after", 4). |
| Link | A two-way statement, claim plus confirmation, that two identities belong to the same entity. |
| Negotiation message | An Agreements act carrying text, by which two sides negotiate; it acknowledges the latest message received from the other side. |
| Mark | A clone's statement of the rules that brought it into force and whose signatures met them; checked by verifiers. |
| Machine profile | The memory layout, encoding and entry point of the RV32IM machine that verification rules run on: a specification of its own, a Module whose field 5 names the Development MIR, named by hash, uploaded after the freeze, and named by every rule's reference. |
| MIR | MOR Inscribed Rules: one of the six core specifications, a set of rules giving a layer by layer look at the MICS (the Identity MIR, the Money MIR). Frozen forever. Has no creator. |
| MICS | MOR Inscribed Core Spec: the core, the six MIRs together, mapped by this document. Frozen whole. This document is uploaded as a specification kind of its own, with no creator. |
| MIP | Reserved for proposals toward MOR 2. MOR 1's core has none: its core texts are MIRs, and the community's specifications are cMIPs, which keep their name. A MIP is a specification kind of its own, with a creator, naming the MIR or MIP it forks, or, proposing a new layer, the MICS document. |
| Module | An implementation or extension under a MIR or cMIP, named by hash, frozen once uploaded. A specification: it signs nothing. |
| Operator | The identity that runs a relay and signs what it states. |
| Ownership agreement | The agreement (terms) saying who owns a target and how the owners decide: kind 0 a claim, a work's, a deal; kind 1 the collective it founds. It carries the one stake on its target, and no split. |
| Partner | An identity a publication names with its share of each payment, off the top, and an optional label nothing reads. |
| Owners' matters | What a stake's holders consent to together: what the collective's split agreement pays the stake, an issuance in it, a collective's sale or release of a share it holds in a work, and a fork's successors paying a kept holder less. |
| Payee pointer | Where payment to an identity goes: a flow pointer, and a vault of safer entries. A price tag names the payee; money sent to a bare bake goes to its uploader's. |
| Payment claim | A payer's record of a payment, with the rail's proof; equal evidence to a receipt. An anonymous payer's claim carries the signature of the key its payment committed to. |
| Payment cMIP | A cMIP whose field 2 lists Money's payment task (task 6); the one an agreement names for payment; rail Modules plug in beneath it. |
| Price tag | A Money act (type 5) pricing a bake or a publication: a price and a payee, versioned; a version with no price withdraws it. On a bare bake only its uploader's counts; under a publication, the split service's or a party's it designates. |
| Rail Module | A Module under the payment cMIP for one rail: its addresses, its proof, the rule that checks it, and, in its specification, its kind and what its proof binds (fields 11 and 13). It signs nothing; the payee's pointer or vault names those it accepts. |
| Protected clause | In a collective, a clause that can move a member's stake or voice or decide a dispute; together, the judicial tier, changed only with every member's signature, one version for everyone. |
| Publication | The offer (Agreements type 6): what is sold, with which partners, which split service and split plan, the owners' line read from the ownership agreement it names; signed by every party, ended by a later version selling nothing. A first version selling nothing is a split agreement. |
| Proxy | An act of a stake's holder (type 27) handing its vote on the owners' matters to another identity, for one version or until revoked (type 28); the owner's own vote beats it; never on what needs every holder, nor on the owner's money or property. |
| Receipt (home) | A home's signed statement of which identity-chain act it holds; counts only alongside that act. |
| Line (Agreements) | An act of a collective (a record, a rotation of the collective, a fork or a closing) at which a change in who may act for it takes effect; what was done for it is before or after by what the line's history holds. |
| Record (Agreements) | A collective's everyday line, which any member draws: it writes a complete clone of its agreement on the collective's chain, naming the signatures that complete it, so its later acts are judged under it, and registers its members' departures and rotations. It decides nothing by itself. |
| Release to the public domain | An act ending a work's claim, at once or at a named point, naming its history and making its content key public; every direct owner signs it unless the release rule, its threshold counted by share, says otherwise. |
| Route | Where an identity's content of a given type can be found (outbox), or where others deliver to it (inbox). |
| Chain key | A post-quantum key, committed in advance, used once to sign a rotation, a chain signature or a collective's genesis. |
| Self-hosted | An identity that is its own home, trusted on its own signatures. |
| Sequence | A line of an identity's everyday acts. |
| Settlement receipt | The common proof of one payment hop, signed by the receiver. |
| Specialized fork | A branch above the frozen core: cMIPs and Modules made for one domain, meant to stay there, talking to other forks as it chooses; Identity and Text apply across all forks. |
| Split agreement | A collective's publication-shaped act selling nothing, naming its ownership agreement, its split service and split plan: how the collective's income is divided. A protected clause. |
| Split service | The identity a publication names to receive every payment under it, in its own name, and pay everyone: partners and other shares off the top, then the owners' line by the ownership agreement's stake. It is a party of the publication. |
| Stake | The shares of an ownership agreement's target, a work's claim or a collective, in millionths summing to 1,000,000, held by whoever the transfer chain names; one per ownership agreement. |
| Succession (Identity) | A declaration in a rotation naming the identity that succeeds it, followed only once the successor confirms it. |
| Succession plan | Part of an agreement naming a party's stake successors and seat successors. |
| Task | A point where a MIR hands work to a cMIP that an agreement may name by the task's number. |
| Tie rule (Agreements) | Where a decision ending powers (a fork, a closing, a departure, a grant's revocation, a departure emptying an area) and an action using them cite neither each other, the ending wins: the action is void, or, against a grant's revocation, void or disputed under Agreements rule 76. |
| Tier | One of a collective's three levels of rules: constitutional, judicial, operational; each changed by its own rule. |
| Upload, uploader | To upload is to make a bake and put it on relays; its signer is its uploader. |
| Vault | An identity's chain-key-protected set of receiving entries, per unit and per rail, with limits; several entries for one unit take the smallest limit. |
| Vow | An act naming in words something MOR cannot hash, existing or to come, its genesis id its name for good, on its own chain; owned by its signer; sold by publications naming it, each sale pending, confirmed or contested. |
| Work | A piece of media identified by its work hash, bound to its creators by a claim. |
| Witness act | An Identity act saying "I received this act and rely on it", carrying the acknowledgement; never a side effect, explained before signing; it counts only while its signer's chain keeps it. |
| Work hash | The fingerprint of a work's complete plaintext. |
| Xtype | "Extra type": a cMIP defining new act types from core pieces, for anything the MICS did not foresee, filling no task; shown as unknown until adopted. |
