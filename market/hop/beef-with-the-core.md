## 1. A split service that stays present can block its own replacement (H7)

**In one sentence.** A split service that keeps working can refuse every new version of the publication it serves, its own replacement included, and nothing in the core can remove it.

**The story.** We wrote "leaving us is cheap" as our third commitment, resting on backups. Then we read how the parties replace a split service. They need a new version every party signs, and the split service is a party. A deal's clone completes only with every party of the parent whose voice remains. The abandonment clause reaches only an absent party, and a service that keeps receipting is not absent. Backups take a payment only where an attempt at the first service fails, and a working service never fails. Ending the publication needs every party too, and only the tag's signer can withdraw its tag. So a present, hostile split service can freeze a publication: no new measurer, no new weights, no replacement, no end. The owners' only way out is a new publication beside the old one, while the old tag keeps selling through the old service. We expected the parties to be able to replace the one party that holds their money.

**Where.** Agreements rule 31: "The parties switch services by a new version of the publication naming another split service (field 3), every party signing it, the new service among them." Rule 47: "A clone of it completes only when all the parent's parties whose voice remains have signed it." Rule 85: "Absence means absence from duty: no act by the party on the agreement, for a period measured on the agreement's time reference." Freeze scenario 7, step 7, where the old service signs its own replacement. Money rule 18 (only the signer withdraws a tag).

**Who loses, and how much.** Every publication's owners and partners: a way out. While the service stays, it holds the money and the tag. It cannot pay anyone outside the plan (rule 27), but it can stop every change, including the change that would take the money away from it. Our customers lose the ability to leave us; so do every other split service's.

**Above or below the line.** **A guarantee is missing.** No module can supply it: the signing rule of a deal is the core's.

**The way out.** Yes: this is leaving. A publication's owners cannot leave their split service without its consent.

**Who else reached it.** Plain Words, independently ("a publication is signed by its owners' side, each partner and its split service", its correction to a client on 6 November). Counterparty confirmed it against the texts and put its name beside it. Longhouse accepted the same promise for itself as a partner.

**What we would want instead.** The other parties replace the split service by a version it need not sign, the old service still owing what it received, as rule 31 already says. Cost: one exception to "a deal changes only with everyone's signature", for one named role, and a sentence on what the replaced service still owes. A simpler core loses the rule that every party of a deal consents to every change.

**How badly.** It blocks freeze scenario 7, step 7, as a guarantee rather than a courtesy. It costs our business the promise we most wanted to make; we replaced it with a promise in blocks that the core does not enforce.

## 2. A refund for a broken vow has nowhere to wait (H1)

**In one sentence.** The core leaves a broken vow's refund to the publication's terms, then forbids the split service, the only party holding the money, from paying it.

**The story.** We tried to write a refund cMIP for a promised delivery: a season, a service, an event. The core says what is owed back is the bundle's terms, the publication's words or a cMIP's. A contest prevails over a confirmation and voids nothing. A contested sale is still a purchase, so the split service must split it like any other, summing exactly, and may pay only owners, partners, evidenced roles and fees. The payout format has no entry for money owed back. Batching lets the service hold money, but when it splits it still pays the owners. An escrow hop on the same rail and unit is not a forward. A role share held open until the buyer confirms is owed to no one (Plain Words and we checked: evidence acknowledged after the split fills nothing, and an open obligation names one receiver). So the only refund a cMIP can write is each holder owing back their own payout after it is paid out: the buyer chases every owner. The workaround we will sell is an escrow as seller of record, with a public notice naming the maker for every contested sale. It works, but the buyer contests a middleman, and the maker's own record shows nothing.

**Where.** Agreements rule 13: "What a buyer is owed back when part of a bundle fails is set by the bundle's terms: the publication's words, or a cMIP's. The core fixes no partial refund." Rule 27: "A split service MUST NOT pay anyone outside the owners as the ownership agreement's version in force for the receipt names them, the partners, the roles as evidenced, and the fees the plan makes owed." Rules 20, 21, 26 and 30; "Split plan and split (type 8)", payouts. Money rule 25: "Only conversion hops forward."

**Who loses, and how much.** Buyers of anything promised rather than delivered: their money, on the bad day. The Back Row marked three of its six refund cases "nothing automatic" for this reason. Makers lose a clean way to stand behind a vow.

**Above or below the line.** **The grammar is missing.** No cMIP can name a refund as a payout: the payout format and rule 27 are the core's.

**The way out.** No.

**Who else reached it.** Plain Words, independently (its PW-3; a joint note links the two). The Back Row's cases 2, 3 and 6. Ampersand promises no refund on a vow until H1 is answered.

**What we would want instead.** A split cMIP may name, as a payout, money owed back under the publication's refund terms, paid to the payer the payment's commitment names, at the refund rail its claim names; or the texts say plainly, as a stated cost, that a vow's refund is owed by the holders after payout. Cost: one payout kind and one line in rule 27; a simpler core loses the rule that a split pays only those on the publication's side.

**How badly.** It blocks our business's refund product for vows; we will sell the escrow instead.

## 3. A metric share no record measures can only fall to the owners' line (H5)

**In one sentence.** A plan can say where an unfilled role goes, but not where an unmeasured metric share goes, so a measurer's failure pays every holder, departed ones included, for good.

**The story.** A bundle's members hold its stake so the metric can pay them for use (case 10, H4). The stake is meant to draw nothing: the metric and the fees take the whole payment. When the measurer fails, or a pass is unused and the record is empty, the metric share is not taken off the top and falls to the owners' line. That pays every holder, and a departed member keeps its stake: Plain Words reads that a departing member cannot be bound to release it. For an unfilled role, the plan chooses: owners, held open, or a named party. For a metric share, it has no choice.

**Where.** Agreements rule 22: "what is left, the owners' line, goes to the owners alone, by their shares", and "A role share nobody fills follows the option the plan chose: to the owners pro rata, held open as an obligation, or to a named party." Rule 29 and the split's key 7 (a metric share is paid only where records measure it). Rule 70 (a departing member keeps its stake).

**Who loses, and how much.** Current members of a bundle: the share of every unmeasured payment that goes to departed holders, forever. Small per payment; it never ends.

**Above or below the line.** **The grammar is missing.** A plan cannot write the choice; a cMIP cannot add it.

**The way out.** Indirectly: it is what a member who leaves keeps drawing.

**Who else reached it.** Filed jointly with Ampersand. Plain Words' reading on departure made it real, and it supports the change.

**What we would want instead.** The plan's choice for an unfilled role (key 3) applies to a metric share no record measures. Cost: one sentence; a simpler core loses nothing it relies on.

**How badly.** It costs our customers something they can live with today: the bundle's terms make the record rarely empty, and a keeper must show it missed (our split cMIP). Departed holders still draw on every failure.

## 4. A relay cannot be paid by role share for carrying a bundle's works (H6)

**In one sentence.** Under a pass, a relay's delivery records never fill a role share, however much it carries.

**The story.** Both pools meant to pay the relay that carries their works by a role share, evidenced by its delivery records, acknowledged in each subscriber's claim. A pass is a publication selling access. The works the relay carries are other bakes. The verifier counts a delivery record only where its object is the media of the publication the payment fulfils. So every record fills nothing, and the share is a role nobody fills. We moved the relay to a partner share: named in advance, paid whether or not it carried anything, and a party whose signature every later version needs (case 1). A smaller point: deliveries under a pass come after the payment, and the texts say evidence is acknowledged in the claim written at payment; whether a later claim of the same payment may acknowledge it before the split is not said plainly.

**Where.** Agreements rule 22: "For a relay's delivery record, the object it names is the one the payment was for, where the verifier reads the record's format." The verifier's `payer_acknowledged` (`core/src/agreements/view.rs`). Rule 11 (the access form).

**Who loses, and how much.** Relays: payment by use in every bundle. Pools: a relay paid for nothing on a quiet month, and one more signature on every version.

**Above or below the line.** **The grammar is missing.** A delivery record cannot point to access.

**The way out.** Indirectly: it adds a partner, and so a party who can hold every version (case 1).

**Who else reached it.** Longhouse accepted it and corrected a line it had given Backlot. Plain Words drafts the relay as a partner because of it.

**What we would want instead.** A delivery record counts for a payment whose publication sells access to the object it names, as the access form's cMIP says; and a sentence on whether a later claim of the same payment may acknowledge evidence before the split. Or the texts saying, as a stated cost, that a relay carrying works under access is paid only as a partner. Cost: the verifier reads the access cMIP's reach; a simpler core loses a check that stays local to one publication.

**How badly.** It blocks the relay's role share in both pools as drafted; we live with the partner share.

## 5. Who receives a split that pays the stake nothing (H10)

**In one sentence.** Two rules disagree on whether a holder paid nothing receives a split, and the answer decides what a buyer of a sliver of a stake can read.

**The story.** Rule 20 delivers the split to every holder and partner it pays. Rule 9 says every split for a stake goes to every holder of it, paid or not. In a bundle whose stake draws nothing, does a split that pays the stake nothing count as one "for a stake"? Counterparty read rule 20 and concluded that a sliver buyer sees only unmeasured payments. We pointed to rule 9, and Counterparty agreed. Rule 9 is a client conformance, so delivering less could be non-conforming. We deliver every split to every holder, conforming either way. The cost: anyone who buys a sliver of a bundle's stake reads every payment's spread, for good.

**Where.** Agreements rule 9: "The split service delivers every receipt for a stake to every holder of it, paid or not (client conformance)", where "'Receipt' in this rule means the split act". Rule 20: "It delivers the split to every holder and partner it pays, departed or not."

**Who loses, and how much.** Members of any bundle: privacy of their books against anyone who buys a sliver of the stake (Counterparty's C14).

**Above or below the line.** **Wording**, with a privacy consequence. A cMIP can deliver more, never less.

**The way out.** No.

**Who else reached it.** Filed jointly with Counterparty (its C15).

**What we would want instead.** One sentence: whether a split whose owners' line pays nothing is delivered to every holder of the stake. Cost: none.

**How badly.** Wording only; it decides what our terms must tell every member.

## 6. No test vectors for the rules a split service pays by (H9)

**In one sentence.** Every verdict a split service needs before paying anyone but owners and partners exists only in the verifier's code, never in a vector.

**The story.** We needed to check our reading of role shares, delivery and use records, and metric payouts against the core. `core/vectors/` holds nothing for any of them. We read the code (cases 4 and 10) but could not check it against a vector. The freeze suite names the scenarios.

**Where.** `core/vectors/`; freeze scenarios 134 and 230; Agreements rules 22, 29 and 30.

**Who loses, and how much.** Every split service and every client paid by a role share: we cannot run them on real money.

**Above or below the line.** **A guarantee is missing**, in the core's own code: the verdict is the core's, so only the core can give its vector.

**The way out.** No.

**Who else reached it.** Pointer, independently (its P3); filed jointly.

**What we would want instead.** Vectors before the freeze for: a role share counting only when the payer's claim acknowledges its evidence; a delivery record and a use record, acknowledged and not; a delivery record whose object is not what the payment was for; a metric payout to a holder paid nothing; a metric payout whose measurer is the split service. Cost: the vectors.

**How badly.** It blocks freeze scenarios 134 and 230 as checkable steps, and our business on real money.

## 7. A replaced split service's price tag keeps counting (H8)

**In one sentence.** After a vanished split service is replaced, its old tag still counts, so on a push rail a buyer can make a valid purchase that pays the vanished service.

**The story.** We followed a publication after its split service vanishes and is replaced through an abandonment clause, the way out of case 1. The verifier counts a tag where its signer is the split service of the publication's version in force for the tag. That is the latest version the tag's own history holds, and an old tag never holds the replacement. Only its signer can withdraw it. On today's rails this is harmless: Lightning and on-chain are request rails, and a vanished service issues no requests. On a push rail, a buyer whose wallet found the old tag pays the vanished service's address. The buyer's only defence is rule 12's client conformance, a strong SHOULD.

**Where.** Money rule 18 (only the signer withdraws a tag); Agreements rules 12, 16 and 31; the verifier's `tag_under` ("its signer is the split service of the publication's version in force for the tag") and `pub_version_for`.

**Who loses, and how much.** Buyers on a push rail: the price paid, stuck at a vanished service's address. Owners: that sale.

**Above or below the line.** **A guarantee is missing**, narrowly.

**The way out.** Yes: it is a hole in replacing a split service.

**Who else reached it.** Counterparty's C9 sits beside it (on a push rail, the first stranger to claim becomes the payer). Plain Words writes our warning into every summary naming a push rail.

**What we would want instead.** A tag under a publication counts for a payment only where its signer is the split service of the version in force for that payment. Cost: the verifier reads the payment's version, not the tag's.

**How badly.** Nothing today. It bites the day a push rail Module exists; we will publish none until it is answered.

## 8. A metric measured by each buyer has no grammar (H3, held as a question)

**In one sentence.** A metric's measurer is one identity named in advance, so the buyer who used the works can never be the one who measures them.

**The story.** Both DAOs wanted each subscriber's payment divided by that subscriber's own use, measured by their own client. The plan names one measurer in advance; a subscriber who joins later cannot be it. In the meetings, Counterparty proposed a countersigning measurer: the payer's client computes the record and the measurer only checks it against the plan's published weights and signs it. That fits the core's form, and our Module's draft 2 makes it the default. What remains is a measurer the payer must trust with its statement, and that must sign.

**Where.** "Split plan and split (type 8)": "metric = [ module: hash, measurer: hash, ? params: any ] ; the measurer named in advance (rule 29)". Rule 30: "A metric pays only identities the plan already pays."

**Who loses, and how much.** Subscribers: what they used is seen by one more party than themselves.

**Above or below the line.** **It could be a Module**, and is: ours. We keep the question only because a payer-measured metric would remove a trusted party.

**The way out.** No.

**Who else reached it.** Ampersand and Backlot, each designing its pool.

**What we would want instead.** Optionally, a metric whose measurer is the payer the payment's commitment names, as a referral's evidence counts only when that payer signs it. Cost: a self-measuring payer could weight its own payment as it likes; per-subscriber division makes that harmless, but pooled division would not.

**How badly.** A cost we can live with.

## 9. A batch's "do not exceed" is checked nowhere (Counterparty's finding; it binds us)

**In one sentence.** Money says a batch's amounts together do not exceed what the payment shows, but the reference code never checks it, and no cMIP defines a batch.

**The story.** Our threshold payouts to one receiver, paid in one rail payment, would be a batch (rule 26 says so). The payment cMIP leaves batches "not yet defined", and Counterparty found Money rule 12's limit checked nowhere in the reference code. So we pay no batches: every payout is its own rail payment, at our cost in rail fees.

**Where.** Money rule 12 ("amounts together do not exceed what the payment shows"); Agreements rule 26 ("Payouts paid with one rail payment name the same batch in their receipts"); freeze step 7.4, which relies on batches.

**Who loses, and how much.** Every split service: rail fees on every payout, which our line absorbs.

**Above or below the line.** **A guarantee is missing in the core's code**; the batch's format is a cMIP's.

**The way out.** No.

**Who else reached it.** Counterparty found it; we report it because it binds our split cMIP.

**What we would want instead.** The check in the reference verifier, and a vector. Cost: the code.

**How badly.** It blocks freeze step 7.4 as checkable; it costs us fees we can live with.

## 10. Wording: H4 and H2

**H4. "A metric pays only identities the plan already pays."** The text does not say whether a holder paid nothing in a split is "already paid". The verifier says yes: it counts the publication's partners and every holder of the stake in the version in force, paid or not (`check_owners_line`, read by the metric check). Every bundle in the market now depends on that reading. A bundle's members are paid by the metric only because they hold a stake that draws nothing. **Wording only; we want the text to say what the code does, never the code changed to a narrower reading.** If it went the other way, both pools would break.

**H2. Money rule 18's wording against backups.** Money rule 18 says a tag "pays the price it states to the tag's payee"; Agreements rule 17 and a vector read a backup's payment, still naming the tag, as a purchase. Rule 18 already hands the purchase to Agreements. **Wording only:** a pointer in rule 18 to rule 17's backups.
