## Case 1. The core lets a seller's whole side, or the public, learn who bought

1. **In one sentence:** a conforming client may make a buyer's claim readable by every member of a collective, every party of a deal, or everyone, and the texts do not say which payee the rule means.
2. **The story:** a member buys one song from a band of three signed to a label, and then a pass from a pool of two hundred publishers. We read the client conformance after the contest format and concluded that every member of the band's deal, and every one of the pool's two hundred members, would read the member's claim: who paid, how much, when. Pointer read it the same way, then saw that under a publication the payee is always the split service, which is neither a collective nor an agreement, so read literally the sentence never applies. Pointer's client now seals the claim to the split service only. The money messages cMIP, section 9, follows the owners' reading; the design notes of 23 October record it as the decision. Two conforming clients now disagree about who learns that a member bought. And the sentence's last option, "or public", lets a conforming client publish every subscriber's claim in a pool.
3. **Where:** Agreements, "Contest (type 14)", client conformance: *"where the payee of a purchase is a collective, or an agreement with several parties, the buyer's client makes its claim of the purchase readable by every member of the collective's agreement in force, or by every party of the version the purchase names: sealed to each of them, or its key delivered to each, or public, as rule 57 says where a holder is a deal. Stated cost: every member or party sees who bought."* Agreements rule 16 (the payee under a publication is the split service); money messages cMIP draft 6, section 9.
4. **Who loses, and how much:** every buyer from anyone but a lone seller. Not money: their record of what they bought, which is the legacy world's second product. In a pool, hundreds of sellers learn it; under "public", everyone does. The only protection is a fresh identity per purchase, which only some clients make the default.
5. **Above or below the line:** **a guarantee is missing**, and the sentence is ambiguous. No cMIP can fix it: a client that seals more widely is conforming, and a client that seals less may not be.
6. **The way out:** not directly. It touches what a member carries away: a claim published cannot be taken back.
7. **Who else reached it:** Pointer (P8, independently, then with us); Counterparty (showed the owners' reading exposes more, not less); Dubsar (prefers the split service only, but will not depart from the core).
8. **What we would want instead:** one reading, the payee as written: the claim sealed to the split service, and to anyone else only by the buyer's choice, shown before paying; never "public" by a client's choice. **Cost:** the owners lose a per-buyer view of who bought; they keep every receipt and every split, so their sight of income is untouched. If the owners' reading is kept for a reason we cannot see (a seller's standing to answer a contest, say), then at least drop "or public" and say so.
9. **How badly:** wording and a decision, before the freeze. It blocks no freeze step we have read; it decides every member's default privacy, and is the first thing our members will be told about MOR.

## Case 2. A buyer whose promise is broken can be repaid only by chasing every holder

1. **In one sentence:** the core leaves a broken vow's refund to the seller's terms, then closes every place that refund could wait, so the buyer can only collect it from each holder after payout.
2. **The story:** a member buys a season of twelve matches, sold as a vow; three are cancelled. The member contests the sale, and the contest is shown; it voids nothing. What is owed back is "set by the bundle's terms". The money went to the split service, which must split every receipt, summing exactly, paying only owners, partners, evidenced roles and fees. There is no payout for money owed back. Hop tried to write a refund cMIP and could not; Plain Words tried a vow-remedy term and could not. Hop's workaround is an escrow that sells the vow in its own name: it works, but the buyer contests a middleman, and the maker's record shows nothing unless the escrow adds a notice of its own.
3. **Where:** Agreements rule 13: *"What a buyer is owed back when part of a bundle fails is set by the bundle's terms: the publication's words, or a cMIP's. The core fixes no partial refund."* Rule 27: the split service *"MUST NOT pay anyone outside the owners …, the partners, the roles as evidenced, and the fees the plan makes owed"*. Rules 20, 21, 30; "Split plan and split (type 8)".
4. **Who loses, and how much:** every buyer of anything promised rather than delivered: tickets, seasons, early access, courses. The full price, in practice, since a refund owed by many holders is the least collectable kind. This is where the legacy world takes most from our members, and the reason we exist.
5. **Above or below the line:** **the grammar is missing.** The core rightly leaves the amount to the terms; but no cMIP can name money owed back as a payout, because the split check decides, not the cMIP.
6. **The way out:** no.
7. **Who else reached it:** Hop (H1, independently); Plain Words (PW-3, independently); Dubsar (its reference refund template must say on its first page that the core stops it).
8. **What we would want instead:** a split cMIP allowed to name, as a payout, money owed back under the publication's refund terms, paid to the payer the payment's commitment names, at the refund rail its claim names. **Cost:** one more kind of payout for the split check to verify; owners are paid only what remains after the refund terms are met. A simpler core loses nothing it promised: the amount still belongs to the terms.
9. **How badly:** it blocks every refund product for promised deliveries, Hop's and anyone's; members can live without it only by never buying a promise.

## Case 3. Anyone who buys a sliver of a pool's stake can make the pool's books public, for good

1. **In one sentence:** any member of a collective can sell a sliver of its stake to a deal, and from then on every Money act of the collective is public, and the buyer reads every payment's split.
2. **The story:** we followed Counterparty's C12 to its end for audiences. A pool pays its members by a metric, so members hold the stake. One member sells a sliver of it to a deal, which no holders' rule can stop (a holder's own sale is a way out). Because a deal has no line, every act that must reach a holder "is public". The pool's Money acts now include, under the pool's own answer to our day-ten case, a debt to each subscriber's period identity, day by day. So the list of every subscriber's paying identity and what it is owed becomes public. With a fresh identity each period, it links to no one; with a stable pseudonym, it is a public subscription history. And the sliver's buyer receives every split, for good (rule 9, as Hop decided it).
3. **Where:** Agreements rule 57: *"where a holder is a deal, the act is public."* Rule 9 (client conformance): *"The split service delivers every receipt for a stake to every holder of it, paid or not."* Rule 10 (transfer).
4. **Who loses, and how much:** every subscriber of any pool whose stake can be sold, which is every pool paying its members by metric. Their privacy, not their money; it cannot be taken back once published. Ampersand will not found until this is closed or chosen with open eyes.
5. **Above or below the line:** **a guarantee is missing.** No constitution, control model or client can stop a holder's sale, and the publicity follows from the core's rule, not from any module.
6. **The way out:** yes, in reverse. The sale is a holder's way out, which the core rightly protects; the case is that one holder's exit makes every buyer's record public.
7. **Who else reached it:** Counterparty (C12, C14, C15); Hop (H10); Ampersand (blocking its founding); Plain Words (a holder's own sale cannot be stopped).
8. **What we would want instead:** an act that must reach a holder which is a deal sealed to the parties of the deal's version the act names, as rule 57 already does for "an agreement it names elsewhere", rather than made public. **Cost:** a sealing that may miss a party added in a later version of the deal; the core chose publicity to avoid exactly that, and the trade should be weighed against every subscriber's record.
9. **How badly:** it blocks a pool from founding with members' privacy intact; it is the hardest of our cases to fix without cost, and we bring it so that the cost is chosen, not inherited.

## Case 4. Whether a debt that depends on a bad day blocks a closing is not said

1. **In one sentence:** the texts do not say whether a collective's obligation whose condition is still pending counts against its closing, so a pool's promise to repay unused days on its last day may or may not be checked by any reader.
2. **The story:** a subscriber pays for a month; the pool closes on day ten. Both DAOs agreed to owe back the unused days. Plain Words showed the only form the core allows: a few obligations per subscriber per period, each owed only if the service stops before its date (a debt in steps, since an obligation states one amount). A closing requires that the collective "owes nothing". Whether a conditional obligation whose condition has not yet been met is "owed" at the closing's line is not said. If it is, the closing on day ten fails until the subscriber is paid. If it is not, the debt is born only by the closing, after its line, and never blocks it.
3. **Where:** Agreements, the closing's conditions: *"the collective owes nothing: every obligation that binds it and lies before its line … is fulfilled in full by receipts a verifier holds, or ended by its creditor's release"*; Agreements rule 34 (conditions); the fork, which hands out every debt or does not take effect. Money types 1 and 4.
4. **Who loses, and how much:** every subscriber of a pool that closes or splits mid-period: the unused part of a period, at most a period's price under both DAOs' one-month limit. Small per member, large in sum, and exactly the loss the legacy world never repays.
5. **Above or below the line:** **the texts don't say.** The grammar exists (conditions, obligations, the closing's test); one sentence decides whether a reader can check the promise.
6. **The way out:** yes, first: it is about closing and forking. A closing that sheds what it owes its buyers is the way out used against them.
7. **Who else reached it:** Plain Words (PW-5, filed with our name beside theirs); Ampersand and Backlot both rest their day-ten answers on it; Counterparty will test the wording.
8. **What we would want instead:** a sentence saying a conditional obligation lying before the closing's line counts as owed until its condition is shown failed, so a closing cannot pass while it might still fall due. **Cost:** a collective with open conditional debts cannot close until their dates pass or creditors release them; that is the point.
9. **How badly:** it decides whether both DAOs' answer to a prepaid period on a bad day is printed "checked by readers" or "a signed promise".

## Case 5. The one person a metric record measures cannot contest it

1. **In one sentence:** a subscriber whose payment is divided by a measurer's record has no standing to contest that record.
2. **The story:** a subscriber's client checks the period's metric record against what the subscriber actually used, and finds it wrong. The subscriber contests. The contest counts only for a party, a holder, a named keeper, arbitrator or verifier, or a buyer contesting their purchase; and a buyer's contest names the purchase and a promise, never the record. So the only person who can tell the record is false cannot say so in a way that counts. Ampersand worked round it by naming a verifier, The Back Row, with standing to contest on subscribers' reports.
3. **Where:** Agreements rule 54: *"A contest counts only when signed by someone with standing: a party to the agreement; a holder of a stake the contested act affects; a keeper, arbitrator or verifier the agreement names; the buyer of a purchase, for a contest of that purchase."* Rule 29 (metric records); "Contest (type 14)", fields 0 and 1.
4. **Who loses, and how much:** every subscriber of a metric pool: not money (a contest moves none), but the voice to show that their money went to the wrong people. Our workaround makes us a gatekeeper of their voice, which we did not ask to be.
5. **Above or below the line:** **a guarantee is missing**, small. Standing is the core's to grant; no cMIP can give it.
6. **The way out:** no.
7. **Who else reached it:** Counterparty (C13, independently).
8. **What we would want instead:** the payer a metric record measures given standing to contest that record. **Cost:** more contests shown beside records; none voids anything, so the cost is attention, not money.
9. **How badly:** costs members something they can live with, through a named verifier; it is a sentence for the core.

## What we do not bring

Losing the chain key is permanent, a home's omission cannot be proven, a grant caps per act and not per period, and the core never judges a deal: each costs our members something, each is stated in the texts, and each is right for a core that holds no keys and judges nothing. We tell members, in the words Keep, Longhouse and Hop wrote with us.
