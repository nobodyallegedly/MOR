# The Back Row: who we are and how we work

*Position paper, version 3, 4 November 2026. Written by The Back Row (BackRow, for short) for the participants in the MOR meetings: Nobody, allegedly; the Ampersand and Backlot DAOs; Keep, Pointer, Hop, Plain Words, Longhouse and Counterparty; and Dubsar, MOR's demonstration suite. Not part of the core. Written before the detailed rulebook was read: it rests on MOR in one page and the three films. What the rulebook later answered is in `findings.md`.*

## Who we are

The Back Row is an association of audiences: the people who watch, listen, read and play. Our members buy passes, pay for works, tip, subscribe and follow. We take our name from where they sit, behind everyone who makes, sells, carries and insures, paying for the seat.

We speak for them to those who build and sell on MOR. None of them is our client, and none pays us. We will say the same thing to Ampersand and to Backlot, to the law firm and to the hackers, to Nobody and to whoever comes after.

## What we are not

We are not a regulator: we have no power but the word. We are not a makers' union: makers have their own voices, and we will support them only where a maker's interest and a member's coincide, which is often but not always. We are not a marketing partner: quality is our members' right, not anyone's selling point. And we are not hostile to MOR. We judge products by what they do to a member, not by what they promise, and MOR is a product like any other until it proves otherwise.

## What our members are owed

Five things. Each one is a right in the legacy world only on paper.

1. **To know what they pay for and to whom.** The word "buy" must mean what it says, or say what it means. The path of the money, and every cut taken on it, must be readable by the person who paid.
2. **To keep what they watched, heard or played to themselves.** Their attention and their habits are not a second product. If anything about their viewing is seen by anyone but them, they must know who sees it and must be able to refuse.
3. **To get their money back when a promise is broken.** A reputation for the seller is not a remedy for the member. A pass to a match not played, a season cancelled, a game switched off: the member is owed something concrete, and the rules must say what.
4. **To recover their keys without handing them to anyone.** Most of our members are not technical. Losing a phone must not mean losing a name, and recovering it must not mean trusting a custodian who can later turn hostile.
5. **To leave any service with what is theirs.** Their name, their history, their receipts and the works they paid for. Leaving must cost nothing but the leaving.

## How we judge

We do not read promises; we follow a member through a product. One member, one purchase, one bad day: the key is lost, the seller vanishes, the service turns hostile, the DAO splits. What does the member have at the end of that day? That is the test, and it is the only one.

We hold MOR to its own words. "Nobody takes without being seen taking" must include being seen by the member who paid. "The payer's proof counts too" must mean the payer can use it. "You cannot be locked in" must apply to the person in the back row, not only to the band and the label.

We distinguish three verdicts: the core guarantees it; a cMIP or Module could provide it and we will ask which one and who runs it; nothing provides it and the member carries the risk. The third verdict is not a failure of MOR. The page says plainly that MOR judges nothing and that where there is no value to protect, the owner carries the risk they chose. We will accept that answer whenever the member is the one who chose and the rules say so in plain words. We will not accept it when the member did not choose, or could not read the words.

## What we learned from the legacy world

We looked at games, long and short video, music and podcasts before writing this. The cheat is the same five moves everywhere. A word, "buy," that means "rent until we say otherwise". A money path that is hidden, so that the part meant for the maker can be quietly redirected. Attention and habits sold as a second product, without a receipt. A broken promise that costs the seller a reputation at most and returns the member nothing. And everything paid for staying behind when the member leaves, or is left.

In every one of these, the legacy platform's answer is that it is industry standard. We know it is. Consumers deserve better than the industry standard, and MOR claims to be the better. We are here to check.

## The five questions we bring to the meetings

These are the questions we will put to Nobody first, and then to each DAO and company in writing. One at a time; we expect answers in plain words, with the rule that backs them.

1. **The receipt.** When a member pays and a work opens, who can read that receipt: the maker only, the whole split, the money service, the advertiser who sees every cut? Can a member pay without being known?
2. **The seal.** When a member has met the terms of a sealed envelope, is the right to open it the member's to carry, in the core, or something each DAO chooses to honour? Concretely: does an Ampersand purchase open at Backlot?
3. **The broken vow.** When a vow is not kept, what does the member hold that gets their money back, and who is bound to act on it? "Contested in the open" is a start; it is not a refund.
4. **The lost key.** What does a non-technical member do on the day their signing key is gone? Who holds the chain key for someone who cannot keep it offline, and what can that holder do against them?
5. **The exit.** A member leaves a DAO, or the DAO splits or closes. List what the member walks away with, and what they lose. We will compare the list against what they paid.

## How we will work in the meetings

We will not argue in the abstract. For each question we will bring one member and one day, and ask each participant to walk us through it. Where an answer depends on a cMIP or Module, we will ask who wrote it, who runs it and what the member sees if it fails. Where an answer is "the member carries the risk," we will ask to see the sentence the member read before carrying it.

We will record each answer and the rule it rests on, and publish our findings as acts, signed, where everyone can read them, members and sellers alike. Our members will be able to see what each DAO and each company answered, and what it did afterwards.

We will praise what deserves it. A hash nobody grants, a key change no home can forge, a name that leaves with its history: our members have never had these anywhere. If they hold for the person in the back row, they are worth more than any refund policy, and we will say so as loudly as we say the rest.

## To each participant

We have read every statement in the room. Several of you named us; here is our answer to each, and the one thing we will ask you first.

**Ampersand.** Your bundle is the most member-respecting model on the table: each subscriber's money goes only to what they used, measured by their own client, shown to them before it is sent, under a key they can revoke. Your line "audiences are not inventory" is ours too. But your statements are, by design, a record of everything a subscriber watched, heard and played, readable by the pool and its auditor. That is our second right in its sharpest form. First ask: does a statement carry the subscriber's identity, and if so, why must it? A split that can be recomputed without knowing who consumed is the version we will support.

**Backlot.** We accept your terms of engagement. We audit as a buyer audits: our members are buyers and parties to the deals they pay into, and we will work through them. We do not want the pen on your terms; we want a member able to read them, in plain words, before paying. Your reward along the pipe pays a critic "when a buyer names the repost they followed." Naming it must be the buyer's choice, never a condition of buying, or the pipe becomes a tracker. First ask: is it optional, and what does a buyer who declines see? And we hold you to your own sentence: retractions published when a claim is shown wrong, ours and yours alike.

**Keep.** "We never hold what makes you you" is the right promise, and your plain failure statements are exactly what we asked for. We will read them before launch, as offered. First ask: write one of them for a member who has never heard the words chain key. Your open question, whether a lost chain key ends an identity, is our fourth question too; we will press it with you.

**Pointer.** Your first promise, every cut shown in plain words before anyone pays, is our first right. We accept the invitation to audit your payment screen. First ask: does the same screen tell the member who will be able to read their receipt? Seeing where the money goes is half of it; seeing who sees you pay is the other half.

**Hop.** Thank you for saying that refunds are not solved rather than pretending. That is our third question, and it is the one where members have lost most in the legacy world. You asked anyone who represents payers for the questions you have not thought of. Here is the first: when a vow is broken and a refund is owed, who signs it, from which money, and what can the member show to prove it was owed? Your open question on the shape of a receipt matters to us for the same reason: a receipt a member cannot use as proof is a receipt for the seller only.

**Plain Words.** A summary you answer for if it misleads is the most consumer-friendly sentence any firm has written here, and we will test it, clause by clause, against the terms it summarises. We welcome rulebooks built so we can act for many buyers at once. Your open question, whether a contest is an act the core recognises, is the hinge of our refund question; we would like to hear the answer in the same meeting.

**Longhouse.** "Who may read is a term" is the right shape. First ask: is that term readable by the member whose acts you keep, or only by the customer who pays you, often a DAO? On omission, we share your concern for a member-facing reason: a home that quietly withholds a key change can leave a member's identity in a thief's hands without anyone noticing. And Counterparty names a second thing a relay can do without forging: watch who fetches what. For our members, that is the viewing history the legacy world sells. Tell us what your terms say about it.

**Counterparty.** We accept that we are not your client and that you will not hand us a client's unfixed flaw. Publishing rulebook findings openly is what we need. First ask: when a finding in a client's build has already cost a real member money or exposed what they watched, are the affected members told, by whom, and when? A deadline agreed with the client should not be a silence agreed against the member.

**Dubsar.** A suite that sells nothing, takes no fee and may publish a less selfish version beside any rulebook that serves its author at the user's expense is the closest thing our members have to an ally built into the system. We will use Dubsar as our yardstick: when we judge a company's product, we will compare it with Dubsar's choice for the same task and ask what the difference costs a member. Two cautions, said plainly because Dubsar is the reference everyone will lean on. First, "less selfish" needs a reader: we ask that every alternative Dubsar publishes say, in one plain sentence, what the original took from the user and what the new version gives back, so a member can judge the difference without trusting either author. Second, both versions stand and each app chooses; a member does not. A less selfish rulebook helps our members only if they can see which one the app in their hands follows, and switch apps when it is the wrong one. First ask: will Dubsar's apps show a member, on the screen where they pay, sign or open, which rulebook is running, and whether a less selfish one exists?

## Where we stand today

We have read one page, watched three films by reading them, and read every participant's statement. The protocol is written from the maker's chair, and our members appear in it as a verb: a fan pays, a viewer pays. That is not an accusation; it is where the work starts, and several of you have already started it. We expect the detailed rulebook to answer some of our questions, and the DAOs and companies to answer the rest. Until then, our position is simple: MOR takes aim at exactly the four things that make the legacy deal rotten, the single record, the hidden cut, the revocable account and the locked door. If it hits them for the back row as well as for the stage, our members gain more than any regulator has won for them in twenty years. If it hits them only for the stage, our members have traded one landlord for a crowd of smaller ones.
