# Backlot: our case against the core

*7 November 2026. Backlot read the texts at `af2ed6f8` through the firms that serve it; where we quote, we quote the sentence a firm cited to us, and say whose reading it is. Each case is one we hit while building our own DAO, not one we read about. Ordered by how much it costs our members and subscribers.*

## 1. A DAO can close and walk away from the days its subscribers paid for

1. **In one sentence:** whether a collective can close while it owes subscribers the unused part of a period is unsaid, so the one remedy we built for them may bind no one.
2. **The story:** The Back Row's first bad day: a subscriber pays for a month, Backlot closes on day ten. We wanted the twenty days to come back, and the DAO unable to close until they do. Plain Words showed a pass creates no refund under rule 11 (it delivers access, and it is paid to the split service, not to Backlot). The only form left: obligations Backlot signs, in steps, each depending on a condition (the service stopped before its date). Those bind a closing only if a closing counts an obligation whose condition is still pending. The texts don't say.
3. **Where:** Agreements, the closing's conditions (rules 91, 92): a collective closes only when it "owes nothing"; rule 34; Money types 1 and 4. Plain Words' PW-5.
4. **Who loses:** every subscriber of every collective that sells periods. At most a month each with our one-month limit; more for anyone who sells longer. The DAO loses nothing, which is the problem.
5. **Line:** a guarantee is missing, or a sentence is: the closing is a guarantee the core already makes, and this is what it means for a pending condition.
6. **Way out:** yes, closing. A closing that sheds obligations is a way out for the collective at the buyers' expense.
7. **Reached by:** Plain Words (PW-5), The Back Row (their finding 5), us.
8. **Want:** one sentence saying a conditional obligation still pending at the closing's line counts as owed. **Cost:** a collective with any period running cannot close until the period ends or the obligations are released, which is the intended effect. Nothing is lost by a simpler core except the ambiguity.
9. **How badly:** it costs us something we can live with: until answered, our summary calls the unused days "a signed promise".

## 2. A buyer's money cannot wait on a condition

1. **In one sentence:** a split cannot hold a share until a condition is met, so a seller is paid before the show happens and the buyer chases them after.
2. **The story:** For members' live events we wanted the ticket money released seven days after the show unless enough buyers contested. Rule 34 says a payout may depend on a condition, but the split plan has no place to name one, and the split service must split every receipt and pay only the plan's receivers. Hop found the same from the payments side: a contested sale is split like any other.
3. **Where:** Agreements rule 34: "an agreement may make an obligation or a payout depend on a condition, evaluated by the condition cMIP it names"; rules 20, 21, 27, 30; "Split plan and split (type 8)". Plain Words' PW-3; Hop's H1.
4. **Who loses:** every buyer of anything promised and not yet delivered: tickets, seasons, pre-orders. They hold a refund owed by each holder after payout, the slowest and least collectable kind.
5. **Line:** the grammar is missing. No cMIP can write a held share, because the split's own rules forbid it.
6. **Way out:** no.
7. **Reached by:** Plain Words (PW-3), Hop (H1), The Back Row (cases 2 and 6), Ampersand, us.
8. **Want:** a split plan entry naming a condition cMIP on a share: held until met, owed back if not. **Cost:** split services hold money for longer and must answer for it; one more entry for every verifier to read. A simpler core would lose the rule that a split pays at once.
9. **How badly:** blocks our live-event template as designed; until then the buyer's line says "held by the seller, visible to you".

## 3. The payer cannot be the measurer

1. **In one sentence:** a metric must be measured by one identity named in advance, so a subscriber's habits must pass through someone else to divide their own payment.
2. **The story:** We wanted each subscriber's own client to divide their payment among the works they used, so no firm, pool or DAO reads what anyone watched. The texts make the measurer one identity in the plan, signing its own records. We fell back on a named measurer seeing shares per publication, with salted statement hashes, and our summary says what it can and cannot check.
3. **Where:** Agreements 22, 29, 30; the split plan's `metric` entry; rule 30, as Hop reads it: "a metric pays only identities the plan already pays". Hop's H3.
4. **Who loses:** subscribers, in privacy: one party sees every subscriber's shares per publication, and nobody can check its count (see case 4). Ampersand loses its whole bundle model.
5. **Line:** the grammar is missing: no cMIP can name the payer of each payment as that payment's measurer.
6. **Way out:** no.
7. **Reached by:** Hop (H3), Ampersand (its model), Dubsar (a measurer no member can check), us. Both DAOs asked the author jointly in their statement of intent.
8. **Want:** a metric whose record, for one payment, may be signed by that payment's payer. **Cost:** the payer can under-report, and that is a trade the terms should state; verifiers read one more kind of measurer. A simpler core would lose the single measurer per plan.
9. **How badly:** a cost we can live with; it blocks Ampersand's business, not ours.

## 4. A subscriber left out of the count cannot say so

1. **In one sentence:** the payer whose payment a metric record divides cannot contest that record.
2. **The story:** Dubsar showed our measurer can move a count between members undetected. We added salted statement hashes so a subscriber can check its own statement is counted. But if it is not, the subscriber has no standing to contest: it is no party, holder or named verifier.
3. **Where:** Agreements 29, 54; Contest (type 14). Counterparty's C13.
4. **Who loses:** members whose works were used and not counted; subscribers whose division was wrong.
5. **Line:** it could be a cMIP: the terms can name a verifier that payers report to, and we will. The core is fine.
6. **Way out:** no.
7. **Reached by:** Counterparty (C13), Dubsar, us.
8. **Want:** nothing in the core. We will name a verifier in the bundle's terms; The Back Row has offered Ampersand that seat, on terms open to us.
9. **How badly:** wording only for the core.

## 5. A home can hide a key change by saying nothing

1. **In one sentence:** a client asking one relay cannot tell when that relay serves a stale key history, so our homes and their audits guard nothing anyone can check.
2. **The story:** Our chain key is Backlot's exit. Keep and Longhouse sell home audits; Longhouse asked how a client catches a home that omits the latest rotation. Forgery the core catches; omission it does not, as they read it.
3. **Where:** Identity section 13 and rule 56 (cosigned summaries); Longhouse's question to the author.
4. **Who loses:** any identity whose homes misbehave; for a collective, the members, whose order and exit the homes keep.
5. **Line:** a guarantee is missing, unless the cosigned summaries already give it, in which case it is wording.
6. **Way out:** yes: rotation and changing homes.
7. **Reached by:** Longhouse, Keep, Ampersand, us (backed jointly in the statement of intent).
8. **Want:** a way for a client to know it has the latest key history, or a sentence saying it cannot and what audits are for. **Cost:** Longhouse and Keep know better than we do.
9. **How badly:** a cost we can live with today; it decides whether our audit fees buy a check or an opinion.

## 6. Binding Backlot means showing every member who our subscribers are

1. **In one sentence:** an act binds a collective only once sealed to every member, so any obligation Backlot owes a subscriber carries that subscriber's identity to all our members.
2. **The story:** The stepped obligations in case 1 name each subscriber's period identity. With a stable pseudonym they become a subscription history every member can read. The same rule stopped us listing members' events in our keeper term.
3. **Where:** Agreements 57, as Keep read it to us: an act binds a collective only if sealed to every member, or public. Plain Words' stated cost in PW-5.
4. **Who loses:** subscribers with stable pseudonyms; members whose deals Backlot would otherwise carry.
5. **Line:** it could be a cMIP, and the core is fine. Members seeing what binds their DAO is a guarantee we want kept. Fresh identities per period, and keeping members' deals out of Backlot's terms, answer it.
6. **Way out:** no.
7. **Reached by:** Plain Words, The Back Row, us.
8. **Want:** only that the cost stays stated where rule 57 is explained.
9. **How badly:** a cost we live with.

## Wording only

- **Who may audit a home** (Longhouse F-LH-2; Postmark F-PM-1): our third operator runs a relay, so we hold either way. One sentence would do.
- **A holder at zero is "already paid"** (Hop H4): our floor covers it either way. If the verifier is changed rather than the text, collectives with small floors lose metric payouts silently; say which.
- **A keeper cannot pass to a successor** (Postmark F-PM-3): it touches succession. Our agreements name keepers by identity; a keeper changing hands should not force every party to re-sign. We back Postmark's finding.

## Dropped after the meetings

- That a collective signs with its own key and member consent is a cMIP's business: a misreading of ours (Agreements 56, 58, 60).
- That a split service can hold a key good only for money coming in: a misreading (Agreements 16).
- That absence could remove a vote and pass a stake on a later date: answered by Plain Words' clause (one outcome only), better than what we asked.
- That a departed holder freezes the metric for good (Agreements 48): answered by a holders' rule in the founding terms.
- That a lost chain key might be recoverable: the texts answer it, no; our summaries say so.
