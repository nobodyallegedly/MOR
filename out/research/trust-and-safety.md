# Trust and safety for newcomers: research for DubWeb

Research session A. Prepared for Nobody, allegedly. A record of research, not a decision.
2026-11-20

## Summary

1. People heed a warning about as often as they have found it to be right. A false alarm spends trust that every later true warning needs.
2. Layout and timing change behaviour more than wording does. A step the person must pass at the risky moment beats a passive mark in a feed.
3. Marking some posts makes unmarked posts look safe. Silence is read as "checked".
4. Asking ordinary people to check keys by hand has failed in study after study for 25 years, unless they were coached. Software that remembers and compares for them has worked.
5. No studied system stops a stranger with a copied name at first contact by asking the newcomer to check something.
6. Flagging honest warners for using the scammers' words is a known, measured error (confusing mention with use). The remedy is to judge what a post asks the reader to do.
7. Social recovery moves the weak point from one secret to several people who can be talked round. A phone call from an acquaintance got a code from 45% of helpers.
8. The loss reductions that are measured came from changing structure (who bears the loss, removing a risky feature), not from messages.
9. Shared warning lists fail in known ways: entries too wide, entries too old, raw report counts, and impostors reporting the real person.
10. Nobody has measured whether showing account age or "first to use this name" stops people being fooled. DubWeb would be testing it first.

## How to read this

- **[measured]** means a study or published data, with its method. **[opinion]** means practitioners' opinion, documentation, specification text or news reporting.
- The honest limit: this session's network blocked most source sites. Most sources were cited from search-result summaries and were not read in full. Treat every number as "seen in summary; check before quoting".
- Sources marked **(read in full)** were opened and read: NIP-05, NIP-56, NIP-85, the atproto handle specification, Bluesky's labelling proposal, Schechter, Egelman and Reeder (CHI 2009), and the MetaMask eth-phishing-detect README and false-positive policy.
- Each "For DubWeb" paragraph is this session's suggestion. It is not a decision and not a rule of the protocol.

## 1. Security warnings

**Warnings that are usually right are usually heeded.** Field data from real Chrome and Firefox users showed that people clicked through only 9% to 23% of malware and phishing warnings, but through 33.0% of Firefox certificate warnings and 70.2% of Chrome's ([Akhawe and Felt, USENIX Security 2013](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/41323.pdf)) [measured, browser telemetry]. Certificate warnings were very often false alarms. One economist's argument put it as "100% of certificate error warnings appear to be false positives" ([Herley 2009](https://www.nspw.org/papers/2009/nspw2009-herley.pdf)) [opinion].

**Design moved behaviour; plain words did not move understanding.** Chrome's redesigned warning made the safe choice the obvious button and hid the unsafe one. Adherence rose from 37% to 62% after release. Simple text and a picture did not markedly raise understanding of the threat ([Felt et al., CHI 2015](https://adrifelt.github.io/sslinterstitial-chi.pdf)) [measured, field experiment and surveys]. People can be led to act safely without understanding why. That makes a wrong warning more harmful, because people follow it blindly.

**People trust what they already know over the warning.** A survey shown right after real warnings to over 6,000 users found no single dominant failure; reasons "are many and vary with context" ([Reeder et al., CHI 2018](https://research.google/pubs/pub46632/)) [measured, experience sampling]. Chrome users reliably heeded malware warnings on sites new to them and often ignored them on sites they had visited ([Almuhimedi et al., SOUPS 2014](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/soups2014/soups14-paper-almuhimedi.pdf)) [measured, field data and survey].

**Repeated look-alike warnings stop being seen.** Brain imaging and eye tracking over a five-day week showed attention falling sharply. In a three-week field test, adherence fell more slowly when the warning changed its look ([Vance et al., MIS Quarterly 2018](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/6495); [Anderson et al., CHI 2015](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2702123.2702322)) [measured; sample sizes not verified]. A message that interrupts a demanding task is more often disregarded ([Jenkins et al. 2016](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/isre.2016.0644)) [measured]. In a lab phishing test, 79% heeded a warning that interrupted them. A passive one did not differ significantly from no warning; the two summaries seen give different figures for it (13% closed the window; one participant heeded it), so check the paper ([Egelman et al., CHI 2008](https://css.csail.mit.edu/6.566/2018/readings/youve-been-warned.pdf)) [measured, lab].

**False alarms set the ceiling.** In a lab study of 138 students, about 90% matched their response rate to the share of alarms that had been true ([Bliss et al. 1995](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus1990/1980)) [measured, lab]. The effect is not found in every setting ([Wickens et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720809344720)) [measured, mixed]. Attention is a shared budget: each needless prompt costs every other prompt ([Böhme and Grossklags 2011](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2073276.2073284?cid=81100623349)) [opinion].

**Labels change the meaning of no label.** In two experiments (5,271 and 1,568 participants), tagging some false headlines made untagged false headlines seem more accurate. Marking some true ones as verified removed the effect ([Pennycook et al. 2020](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/130380)) [measured]. A warning beside the risky link cut clicks more than a banner at the top; disabling the link until the person passed the warning worked best ([Petelka et al., CHI 2019](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3290605.3300748)) [measured, online experiment]. In a lab study, 13 of 20 people did not notice in-line warnings; all 20 noticed full-page ones ([Kaiser et al. 2021](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec21-kaiser.pdf)) [measured, small lab].

**Word-based detectors flag the people who speak against the harm.** This is the "use versus mention" error: quoting or condemning a phrase is treated as using it. In a standard test suite, leading hate-speech models got counter-speech that quotes hate right only about a quarter to a third of the time ([Röttger et al., HateCheck, ACL 2021](https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-long.4.pdf)) [measured; the summary seen was garbled, check the paper's table]. Teaching a model the distinction reduced the errors ([NAACL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-long.331.pdf)) [measured]. All this evidence is from hate and toxicity detection. Its transfer to scam labels is this session's inference.

For DubWeb: these are suggestions. First, the early false alarm on honest warners is the expected default of any word-triggered mark, not an odd bug. Decide on what a post asks the reader to do (send a key file, type recovery words, pay a fee). "Never send your key file to anyone" asks for nothing and should carry no mark. Keep a fixed set of test posts that quote, negate and condemn scam wording, as HateCheck does. Second, use two tiers. Quiet words for uncertain facts ("same name as another identity"). An interrupting step only for the rare, high-confidence, high-harm act, shown at the moment of the act, which would have met the newcomer who sent her key file to the false "Welcome Desk". Third, say somewhere plain that an unmarked post has not been checked.

## 2. Key and identity verification

**"Why Johnny Can't Encrypt" and successors.** What failed: every step done by hand. In 1999, 4 of 12 experienced email users managed to sign and encrypt in 90 minutes ([Whitten and Tygar](https://www.cs.jhu.edu/~rubin/courses/fall03/papers/johny.encrypt.pdf)) [measured, lab]. In 2015, 1 of 10 pairs of novices finished the task ([Ruoti et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.08555)) [measured, lab]. What worked: software that remembers the first key it saw and warns on change. With 43 subjects, this significantly reduced success of key-change attacks, but "did not inoculate subjects against the 'new identity attack'", a new address with a new key ([Garfinkel and Miller, SOUPS 2005](https://cups.cs.cmu.edu/soups/2005/2005proceedings/p13-garfinkel.pdf)) [measured, lab]. That is the copied display name, measured in 2005.

**Signal safety numbers.** What failed: finding the check, doing it, and knowing why. In one study 21 of 28 participants failed to compare keys, and most of them believed they had succeeded ([Schröder et al. 2016](https://eprints.cs.univie.ac.at/4799)) [measured, lab]. Told only of the threat, 14% of pairs succeeded; told the check exists, 79% did, in about 11 minutes ([Vaziripour et al., SOUPS 2017](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2017/technical-sessions/presentation/vaziripour); [SOUPS 2018](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2018/presentation/vaziripour)) [measured, lab]. What worked: a prompt at the right moment and a simpler step, though many remained "unsure or confused about the purpose" (same 2018 paper). When people must compare by eye, more than 10% missed attacks on hexadecimal strings and sentences did best ([Dechand et al. 2016](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity16/technical-sessions/presentation/dechand)) [measured, 1,047 online participants]. The industry has since moved the check into software. Signal says its automatic check "does not prove who controls the associated phone number or username" ([Signal](https://signal.org/blog/automatic-key-verification/)) [opinion, vendor statement].

**Keybase social proofs.** What worked: the question changed from "is this key right?" to "is this the account I already know elsewhere?". A mail tool using Keybase lookups beat a standard tool on speed and errors with 15 lawyers and journalists ([Lerner et al. 2017](https://www.franziroesner.com/pdf/confidante-eurosp17.pdf)) [measured, small lab]. What failed: a proof is only as strong as the outside account, and free sign-up drew "crappy fake accounts" once identities were worth money ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/14672/keybase-ends-stellar-airdrop-thanks-hordes-crappy-fake-accounts)) [opinion, news]. No study of the proofs themselves was found.

**Web of trust and key-signing parties.** What failed: reach. The well-connected core was about 40,000 to 50,000 keys out of under 4 million published ([dataset](https://networks.skewed.de/net/pgp_strong); [history article](https://www.cryptologie.net/posts/a-history-of-end-to-end-encryption-and-the-death-of-pgp/)) [measured, graph data]. A security engineer reported he "never ever ever successfully used" it to validate a key ([Valsorda, via Schneier](https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2016/12/giving_up_on_pg.html)) [opinion]. In 2019 attackers attached close to 150,000 signatures to one person's key, because anyone could attach a statement to anyone's key and nothing was ever deleted ([LWN](https://lwn.net/Articles/792509/)) [opinion, news]. What worked: nothing for ordinary users.

**Nostr NIP-05 and web-of-trust scoring.** NIP-05 lets a profile claim a name at a web domain. The specification says it "is not intended to *verify* a user, but only to *identify* them", and that clients "must always follow public keys" ([NIP-05](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/05.md), read in full) [opinion, spec]. What failed: clients drew a check mark beside it, and look-alike domains pass the check ([community write-up](https://stacker.news/items/173439); [guide](https://nostr.co.uk/learn/profile-verification/)) [opinion]. Scoring from follow lists is the current answer. NIP-85 lets outside providers publish signed scores, and "each provider can offer their own ways to calculate such values" ([NIP-85](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/85.md), read in full) [opinion, draft spec]. No usability study of any Nostr identity signal was found. A newcomer follows nobody, so a personal score has nothing to compute from; that is this session's inference.

For DubWeb: these are suggestions. Do not ask a newcomer to compare keys. Let the app remember which identity the reader already knows under a name, and say so when a different identity uses it. Expect that to fail at first contact, as it did in 2005, which is where the test-round newcomer was. Avoid any glyph that reads as "verified" unless its meaning is one a reader can state. A help-desk mark matters most here: an honest warner and a false desk can use the same words, so the difference must come from something the app can check and not from the text of the post.

## 3. Impostors and name collisions on decentralized social networks

**Bluesky.** A handle proves control of a web domain and nothing more. The specification itself warns that "handles which look similar to a well-known domain present security and impersonation challenges", with `paypa1.com` as its example ([atproto handle spec](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bluesky-social/atproto-website/main/src/app/[locale]/specs/handle/en.mdx), read in full) [opinion, spec]. One hand count in November 2024 found at least one less-followed copy for 44% of the top 100 accounts ([Indicator](https://indicator.media/p/44-of-the-top-100-bluesky-accounts-have-a-duplicate)) [measured, one person, one date]. Bluesky then tightened removal, required parody accounts to say so in both name and bio ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/11/30/bluesky-promises-more-verification-and-an-aggressive-approach-to-impersonation)) [opinion, news], and in April 2025 added a centrally issued blue check ([Bluesky](https://bsky.social/about/blog/04-21-2025-verification)) [opinion, documentation]. Its labelling design defines "impersonation" as "accounts which falsely assert some identity" and shows only labels from sources the reader chose ([proposal 0002](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bluesky-social/proposals/main/0002-labeling-and-moderation-controls/README.md), read in full) [opinion]. A label set on the profile does not show on that person's posts ([Bluesky docs](https://www.docs.bsky.app/docs/advanced-guides/moderation)) [opinion]. Freed old handles could be taken by anyone until Bluesky reserved them ([issue](https://github.com/bluesky-social/atproto/issues/3111); [TechRadar](https://techradar.com/computing/social-media/bluesky-just-made-it-harder-for-someone-to-steal-your-name-but-verification-is-still-a-challenge)) [opinion, news].

**Mastodon.** The same username can exist on any server. The one built-in signal is a green check on a profile link, which shows that one party controls both the web page and the account ([Mastodon](https://joinmastodon.org/verification)) [opinion, documentation]. Impostors of the US cyber agency appeared in 2022 ([Cybersecurity Dive](https://www.cybersecuritydive.com/news/mastodon-infosec-cisa-impersonators/637292/)) [opinion, news]. No measured rate was found.

**Nostr.** Names are free text over keys, as in MOR. The protocol offers a report type, "impersonation: someone pretending to be someone else", and warns that reports "can be easily gamed"; it suggests clients use reports from the reader's own friends ([NIP-56](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nostr-protocol/nips/master/56.md), read in full) [opinion, spec]. What individual clients show could not be verified.

**What is measured about marks.** People read a check mark as "this is the real one" ([Vaidya et al., CHI 2019](https://seclab.cs.georgetown.edu/papers/twitter-credibility-chi2019.pdf)) [measured]. In a survey of 299 US adults, more than half misunderstood what Twitter's blue check certified and over 80% misunderstood the gold and grey ones ([Xiao et al., USENIX Security 2023](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity23/presentation/xiao-madelyne)) [measured, survey]. Most impersonation on Twitter cloned ordinary users, not celebrities: 16,572 account pairs were labelled as attacks ([Goga et al., IMC 2015](https://www.lix.polytechnique.fr/~goga/papers/impersonators_IMC2015.pdf)) [measured]. Signal answered fake "Signal Support" accounts in 2026 with plain words, "Name not verified" ([Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/05/13/signal-phishing-social-engineering-protections/)) [opinion, news]. Discord dropped its four-digit name suffix because more than 40% of users did not remember theirs ([Discord](https://discord.com/blog/usernames)) [opinion, company statement]. Bluesky's policy names the trick that defeats age and follower counts: gain followers under one identity, then switch names ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/11/30/bluesky-promises-more-verification-and-an-aggressive-approach-to-impersonation)) [opinion, news].

**The gap.** No study was found that shows account age, first-seen order or "followed by people you follow" to users and measures whether they are fooled less.

For DubWeb: these are suggestions. Compare names in a folded form that maps look-alike letters together, as the Unicode "skeleton" method does ([description](https://github.com/mtibben/confusables)) [opinion], or one swapped letter defeats "same name". A mark shown on both identities raises a question without answering it. MOR orders signed acts, so "used this name first" is a fact the app can state; a name change would have to reset it or the name-switch trick wins. Put the mark on the posts, where newcomers meet the impostor. Protect ordinary members, since they are the ones cloned. Treat reports of impersonation as information from people the reader has chosen, never as a count.

## 4. Social recovery and guardians

**The design.** One key signs day to day. A set of guardians, at least three, can together replace that key and do nothing else. Its best-known advocate names the two risks as guardians losing their keys and guardians colluding or being coerced, and advises guardians who do not know each other ([Vitalik Buterin's 2021 essay, via CryptoPotato](https://cryptopotato.com/vitalik-buterin-social-recovery-wallets/); [2023 advice, via Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/diversified-set-of-guardians-required-for-safe-self-custody-vitalik-buterin)) [opinion; the originals were not opened]. He keeps the bulk of his own funds in a different arrangement ([CryptoSlate](https://cryptoslate.com/ethereum-founder-urges-self-custody-recommends-use-of-multi-sig-social-recovery-wallets/)) [opinion].

**What products did.** Argent offers people, devices or a company-run guardian checked by email and phone ([Argent](https://support.argent.xyz/hc/en-us/articles/360008013258-How-to-add-a-guardian-)) [opinion, documentation]. A 2020 flaw affected wallets with no guardians at all, which had been the default ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/33009/bug-discovered-in-argent-ethereum-wallet?amp=1)) [opinion, news]. Optional set-up gets skipped. Shamir-style splitting, which cuts a secret into shares so that a set number rebuild it, appears in Trezor's backup; losing too many shares makes the wallet unrecoverable ([Trezor](https://trezor.io/learn/advanced/standards-proposals/what-is-shamir-backup)) [opinion, documentation]. Facebook ended its three-to-five-friend recovery in 2022 without a public reason ([WTOP](https://wtop.com/tech/2022/05/data-doctors-what-to-do-about-the-end-of-facebook-trusted-contacts)) [opinion, news]. When Ledger offered to split a key among three companies, users who had been told the key never leaves the device reacted with fury ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/230992/ledger-defends-crypto-wallet-recovery-tool)) [opinion, news].

**The one controlled test of attacking helpers** ([Schechter, Egelman and Reeder, CHI 2009](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/paper1459-schechter.pdf), read in full) [measured, three small experiments]. Recovery needed codes from three of four named helpers. It worked for 17 of 19 users. But 13 of 18 needed to be told who their helpers were. A forged email got a code from 4% of helpers. A phone call from someone who knew the victim got a code or the email from 19 of 42 helpers, 45%, although the emails were headed "FOR YOU ONLY". The authors call that an upper bound. Their proposed fix, briefing helpers when they agree to serve, was not tested.

**What newcomers misunderstand.** Of 643 crypto users surveyed, 43.4% could recognise a picture of recovery words; about 58% thought they could choose them like a password (the base of that figure is unclear) ([Eleshin et al., CHI 2025](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3706598.3713209)) [measured, survey]. Interviews with 29 users found that wrong ideas about keys are the most damaging, and tools do not correct them ([Mai et al., SOUPS 2020](https://usenix.org/conference/soups2020/presentation/mai)) [measured, interviews]. In 24 interviews, people preferred their own second device to a person as guardian ([Yu et al., CHI 2024](https://sauvikdas.com/papers/52/serve)) [measured, interviews]. A design team found that the people who most need social recovery are the least able to operate it ([write-up](https://ethresear.ch/t/what-the-ethereum-community-would-like-to-see-in-a-social-recovery-system/12773)) [opinion]. No study was found that measures the belief "support can restore my account".

**Attacks.** Hijacked accounts told friends "you are one of my Trusted Contacts" and asked for a code that was really the friend's own reset code ([Access Now](https://www.accessnow.org/public-security-alert-new-facebook-attack/)) [opinion, incident report]. A simulation showed captured accounts being used as helpers to capture more ([Gong and Wang 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1402.2699v4)) [measured, simulation]. Fake "recovery services" are a second fraud on people already robbed: 10,516 complaints and about $1.4 billion reported lost to the FBI in 2025 ([ASIS summary](https://www.asisonline.org/security-management-magazine/latest-news/today-in-security/2026/april/cryptocurrency-scams/)) [measured, complaint data via secondary coverage]. The UK regulator had 4,465 reports of scammers posing as it in six months, and 480 people paid ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fake-fca-scams-reported-6-months-2025)) [measured, helpline reports]. Regulators agree nobody legitimate asks an upfront fee ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/recovery-room-scams); [CFTC](https://cftc.gov/node/231206)) [opinion, guidance].

**"We will never ask for your recovery words."** Every vendor says it ([MetaMask](https://support.metamask.io/privacy-and-security/staying-safe-in-web3/spoofing-scams)) [opinion]. No measurement of its effect was found. A victim was reported after MetaMask's own public alert ([Finance Magnates](https://www.financemagnates.com/cryptocurrency/news/new-metamask-phishing-scam-preys-on-users-seeking-support/)) [opinion, news].

For DubWeb: these are suggestions. MOR holds no one's keys, so a stronger sentence than the vendors' is available: there is no staff, and nobody can restore a key. It is untested. Judge "recovery service" posts by what they ask (a fee, a key file, recovery words), so that a member who warns "there is no recovery service" is not marked. The hour after a loss is when the "recovery" offer arrives, so that is when a plain note is worth most. If guardians are ever offered, brief them when they agree, remind owners who they are, and expect the weak point to be a persuasive request to a helper, not a broken code.

## 5. Scam patterns that target newcomers, and what reduces losses

**Scale.** People in the US reported $15.9 billion lost to fraud in 2025 ([FTC data, via Spokesman-Review](https://www.spokesman.com/stories/2026/apr/03/americans-lost-a-record-159-billion-to-scams-in-20)) [measured, self-reports]. Impostor scams were nearly one report in three, with $3.5 billion lost ([FTC](https://ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/06/ftc-data-show-people-reported-losing-3-point-5-billion-imposter-scams-2025)) [measured]. Nearly 30% of people who lost money said it began on social media ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/04/new-ftc-data-show-people-have-lost-billions-social-media-scams)) [measured]. No regulator reports by "newcomer". No source measures victims by account age.

**The patterns seen in the test round are the measured ones.** Researchers posted 25,000 automated "I need wallet help" messages and drew over 9,000 fake-support scammers. They came in two kinds: those who ask for the secret words and those who ask for a payment ([Acharya et al., IEEE S&P 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09824?context=cs)) [measured, field experiment]. The trigger is a public call for help. Recovery fraud is an advance fee taken from someone already robbed; lists of victims are sold ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/refund-and-recovery-scams?r=seo)) [opinion, guidance]. On bare payment requests, India's payment system stopped person-to-person "collect" requests from 1 October 2025 to curb fraud and kept requests from registered merchants ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/personal-finance/banking/story/npci-to-tighten-upi-rules-peer-to-peer-collect-feature-to-end-in-october-489244-2025-08-13)) [opinion, news of a regulator's act; no before-and-after number found]. Structure alone is not safety: scammers send requests through PayPal's real invoice system so that they look official ([Intego](https://www.intego.com/mac-security-blog/money-request-and-invoice-scams-via-paypal-venmo-and-docusign/)) [opinion].

**What is shown to reduce losses.**

- Changing who bears the loss. After the UK made banks refund victims, an independent evaluation estimated about 21% lower losses on that payment system, about £73 million a year; the authors call it short-term evidence ([PSR](https://www.psr.org.uk/news-and-updates/latest-news/news/payment-fraud-falls-by-73m-following-psr-reimbursement-scheme/)) [measured, evaluation]. MOR moves no money, so this lever is not available to it.
- Showing a fact the asker cannot fake. Australia's recipient name check led to about 10,000 abandoned payments to accounts already flagged ([Australian Payments Plus](https://www.auspayplus.com.au/banks-hit-major-milestones-as-scam-fighting-technology-stops-thousands-of-risky-transfers)) [measured, industry-reported].
- Redesigning the action, not the warning. In a simulated bank app, changing the action buttons cut fraud success far more than standard warnings, whose effect decayed with repetition ([Nottingham CeDEx 2023](https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/cedex/documents/papers/cedex-discussion-paper-2023-08.pdf)) [measured, online experiment; rates not verified].
- Short education, repeated. A randomised test with about 2,600 people found it reduced susceptibility, faded without reminders, and helped the more financially literate most ([Burke et al. 2022](https://finrafoundation.org/fraud-educational-intervention)) [measured, randomised].
- Someone to ask. In a survey of 1,408 scam reporters, people with no one to talk the offer over with were far more likely to lose money ([FINRA Foundation 2019](https://www.finra.org/media-center/newsreleases/2019/new-fraud-research-low-financial-literacy-levels-and-social)) [measured, self-selected survey].
- Being told directly. Of 3,780 people the FBI told they were in a scam, 78% had not known ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/397134/fbi-says-crypto-related-fraud-losses-hit-record-11-4-billion-in-2025-with-seniors-bearing-the-brunt)) [measured, agency data].

**What does not work.** A Danish bank's one-off warning to clients over 40 showed no significant effect on fraud ([Jensen et al. 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10610-024-09573-1)) [measured, quasi-experiment]. Scammers coach victims to ignore or lie to the bank ([Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/scammers-pose-as-the-national-crime-agency-to-steal-30000-from-lloyds-customer-aQmfh5p9159J)) [opinion, cases], and pose as the protector itself, including a regulator's refund office ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/refund-and-recovery-scams?r=seo)) [opinion].

For DubWeb: these are suggestions. The strongest evidence favours changing what the app lets a stranger do over adding words. For a bare payment request with no terms, show facts the asker cannot fake (first message from this identity; how long it has existed) as facts and not as a verdict. For help, the fix suggested by the fake-support study is about the channel: a newcomer who asks for help in the open is a target, so where real help comes from should be a place impostors cannot display. Any "official" desk becomes the thing copied, as the "Welcome Desk" was. Give newcomers an easy way to ask another member "is this real?"; that makes honest warners valuable, which is one more reason not to mark them.

## 6. Shared warning registers

**Who may write.** The designs range widely. Staff only: Spamhaus ([FAQ](https://www.spamhaus.org/faq/section/Spamhaus%20SBL)) [opinion]. One legal authority making one narrow claim: the UK regulator's list of unauthorised firms, which says absence from the list means nothing ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/scamsmart/about-fca-warning-list)) [opinion]. Outsiders propose, reviewers merge, every change is in a public log: MetaMask's phishing list ([README](https://github.com/MetaMask/eth-phishing-detect), read in full) [opinion]. Anyone reports, staff mark each report "checked" or "not verified": Chainabuse ([docs](https://docs.trmlabs.com/guides/chainabuse/verifying-the-accuracy-of-reported-information)) [opinion]. Any key reports, with no retraction defined: Nostr ([NIP-56](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/56.md), read in full) [opinion]. Anyone may label, readers choose whose labels they see, and a label can be withdrawn by a later one: Bluesky ([docs](https://www.docs.bsky.app/docs/advanced-guides/moderation)) [opinion].

**How errors happen and get corrected.**

- Entries outlive the wrong. Of 151 public address blocklists, 53% to 60% held addresses since reused; one listing could affect up to 78 legitimate users for up to 44 days ([Ramanathan et al., IMC 2020](https://www.isi.edu/results/publications/16296/quantifying-the-impact-of-blocklisting-in-the-age-of-address-reuse/)) [measured].
- Entries are too wide. One flagged site put a warning on a whole UK government domain ([GOV.UK report](https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2022/11/24/incident-report-some-gov-uk-urls-blocked-by-deceptive-site-warning/)) [opinion, post-mortem].
- Crowds are mostly right, and wrong about prominent honest names. Across 176,366 crowd-voted reports, 39 honest sites were voted phishing, among them ebay.com ([Moore and Clayton 2008, via Virus Bulletin](https://www.virusbulletin.com/virusbulletin/2008/04/how-wise-are-crowds-when-assessing-phishing-websites/)) [measured].
- A known-good check helps. Testing 157 lists against known-legitimate senders gave up to 99% specificity, meaning few honest parties caught ([BLAG, NDSS 2020](https://ndss-symposium.org/ndss-paper/blag-improving-the-accuracy-of-blacklists)) [measured]. MetaMask tests additions against lists of well-known sites, and a contributor loses write access on a third false positive ([policy](https://github.com/MetaMask/eth-phishing-detect/blob/main/doc/fp-policy.md), read in full) [opinion, no outcome data].
- Strict agreement is accurate and late. Community Notes on X were about 97% accurate on one topic ([Rochester](https://www.rochester.edu/newscenter/crowdsourcing-fact-checking-community-notes-social-media-676142/)) [measured], with a median lag over 15 hours ([ISD](https://www.isdglobal.org/digital-dispatch/the-trust-consensus-paradox-why-decentralized-fact-checking-faces-challenges-on-polarizing-topics/)) [measured, NGO analysis]. Of notes shown, 30.2% were later withdrawn ([arXiv 2601.14002](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14002)) [measured]. The average phishing campaign lasts 21 hours ([Oest et al. 2020](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity20/presentation/oest-sunrise)) [measured].
- Removal should be free. Spamhaus says there is never a fee ([FAQ](https://check.spamhaus.org/faqs/spamhaus-blocklist/)); a list that sells fast removal is widely called extortionate ([Suped](https://www.suped.com/learn/blocklists/how-to-deal-with-uceprotect-listings-and-their-aggressive-practices)) [opinion].

**How registers are abused.** The impostor reports the real person as the fake: Meta removed a network that did exactly this ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2021/12/metas-adversarial-threat-report/)) [opinion, platform report]. False impersonation complaints were sold at about $60 per banned account, with paid "restoration" after ([Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/instagram-ban-restore-service-scam)) [opinion, news]. People were blocklisted for whom they followed, not for anything they posted ([Jhaver et al. 2018](https://medium.com/acm-cscw/online-harassment-and-content-moderation-the-case-of-twitter-blocklists-25f3ab588a4b)) [measured, interviews]. A bug listed the very communities a fediverse list was built to protect ([commentary](https://2024.thenexus.today/?p=4285)) [opinion]. Lists also die when funding ends ([The Register on SORBS](https://www.theregister.com/2024/06/07/sorbs_closed); [IFTAS](https://about.iftas.org/2025/03/03/iftas-service-shutdowns/)) [opinion, news]. No register publishes a wrongful-listing rate that an outsider can check.

For DubWeb: these are suggestions. List the act (this post asks for a key file), not the person, the name or the words. That is the register form of the use/mention rule: an honest warner shares the scammer's vocabulary, as a follower shares an association, and both were caught by lists built on surface signs. Let entries lapse, never act on a raw count of reports, keep a visible record of who wrote and who withdrew each entry, make withdrawal free, and tell the marked identity. Expect a "same name" report to be filed by the impostor against the original.

## Design rules to test first

1. **Judge the request, not the words.** Evidence: counter-speech misflagged by word-keyed models ([HateCheck](https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-long.4.pdf)) [measured, in hate speech; transfer is inference]. Test: plant honest warnings that quote scam wording beside real scams; count marks on each.
2. **Reserve the interrupting step for the act of sending a key file or recovery words.** Evidence: active warnings heeded by 79%, passive ones by few (exact figure to check) ([Egelman et al.](https://css.csail.mit.edu/6.566/2018/readings/youve-been-warned.pdf)); warning at the risky element beats a banner ([Petelka et al.](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3290605.3300748)) [measured]. Test: run a false help desk in the round; compare key files sent with and without the step.
3. **Keep marks few and visibly different by severity.** Evidence: habituation within days ([Vance et al.](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/6495)); probability matching ([Bliss et al.](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus1990/1980)) [measured]. Test: count marks shown per player per day and how many were right.
4. **Say that unmarked does not mean checked.** Evidence: implied truth effect ([Pennycook et al.](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/130380)) [measured]. Test: include a scam the rules miss; ask players afterwards what no mark meant to them.
5. **Tell original from copy with a stated fact, in words.** Evidence: new-identity attacks defeat continuity ([Garfinkel and Miller](https://cups.cs.cmu.edu/soups/2005/2005proceedings/p13-garfinkel.pdf)) [measured]; badges are misread ([Xiao et al.](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity23/presentation/xiao-madelyne)) [measured]; the display itself is untested. Test: have a player copy a name; compare a symmetric mark with "used this name first".
6. **Never show a "verified" glyph for a claim that only identifies.** Evidence: NIP-05 check marks ([NIP-05](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/05.md), read in full; [write-up](https://stacker.news/items/173439)) [opinion]. Test: ask players to state what each mark means.
7. **Say plainly that nobody can restore a key and no recovery service exists.** Evidence: recovery fraud figures ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fake-fca-scams-reported-6-months-2025)) [measured]; effect of such messages is unmeasured. Test: offer a fee-based "recovery" to players who lost an identity; compare take-up with and without the sentence.
8. **Give bare payment requests context that cannot be faked.** Evidence: India's removal of such requests ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/personal-finance/banking/story/npci-to-tighten-upi-rules-peer-to-peer-collect-feature-to-end-in-october-489244-2025-08-13)) [opinion]; name checks ([Australian Payments Plus](https://www.auspayplus.com.au/banks-hit-major-milestones-as-scam-fighting-technology-stops-thousands-of-risky-transfers)) [measured, industry-reported]. Test: send term-less requests from new identities; compare payment rates.
9. **In any shared register, list acts, let entries lapse, and ignore raw counts.** Evidence: stale and wide entries ([IMC 2020](https://www.isi.edu/results/publications/16296/quantifying-the-impact-of-blocklisting-in-the-age-of-address-reuse/)) [measured]; reports "easily gamed" ([NIP-56](https://github.com/nostr-protocol/nips/blob/master/56.md), read in full) [opinion]. Test: have an impostor mass-report the original; see which identity ends up marked.

## Open questions

Gaps the research found:

- No study measures whether showing account age, first-seen order or "followed by people you follow" stops people being fooled.
- No measured effect of "we will never ask for your keys" messaging, or of a warning shown when recovery words are revealed, exported or pasted.
- No evidence that newcomers are disproportionately victims. No source reports victims by account age.
- No register publishes a wrongful-listing rate with a checkable method.
- A "same name" mark is symmetric. How should it tell original from copy, and what happens when the older identity is the one that changed its name?
- No study of scam warnings, as opposed to hate speech, being misflagged. No study of harm to honest users from over-warning.
- No controlled study of a deployed social-recovery wallet. No study of newcomers to key-based social apps; all measured work is on money wallets.
- No reliable rate for how often a scam victim is targeted again.
