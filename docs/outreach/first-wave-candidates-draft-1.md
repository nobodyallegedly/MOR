# First wave: candidate doors, draft 1

*13 October 2026. A research list for the first wave of the universities (roadmap, "The way to the freeze, and the first wave"). Compiled from public information only. Nothing has been sent to anyone: Nobody, allegedly, approves the list first, and only then is anything sent, one honest message per door. The first wave reviews a frozen MOR 1 and is asked to break it.*

147 doors in 36 countries. A door is a research group, lab, centre or department, never a private person: each row gives the group's own public page and its public contact route, and no individual's name, personal address or phone number.

## Method

**What was asked.** About 150 doors meeting both criteria:

1. **They live code:** open-source work published under the group's or lab's own name, as a public code organisation or as released tools on the group's site. A professor's personal account alone did not count.
2. **Their published work is close to MOR's ethos:** cryptography, security and privacy; decentralised and peer-to-peer systems; the digital commons; cooperative or platform economics with a technical side; digital rights. Where a group is funded by or builds for the platforms MOR routes around, or is tied to one crypto company, the state or the military, the row says so in its note. Such doors are marked as weaker; none was dropped silently.

**How they were found.** The world was split into six regions, each searched by a separate research pass: the United States and Canada; the UK, Ireland and Benelux; Germany, Switzerland and Austria; southern and northern Europe; Asia-Pacific and the Middle East; Latin America, Africa, central and eastern Europe and Turkey. Each pass started from groups known for the fields above, then widened by web search (in English, Portuguese and Spanish where useful) and by searching GitHub for organisations by university name, place and keyword. For every row, the code organisation's own page was opened and its repositories read; only then was the row kept. The ethos line names one concrete published tool, paper or theme, and says where it was seen.

**Limits of the check.** The research environment could not open most university websites (the address lookup failed), although GitHub pages opened normally. So:

- "org checked" means the code organisation page was opened and read on 13 October 2026.
- "(search)" means a page or detail was seen only in search results, not opened.
- "not checked" means a detail that could not be confirmed; verify it before anything is sent.
- Where a row gives a public page with no source mark, it is the site the group's own code organisation links to.

Contact routes are the thinnest part of the list. A general group address or contact page was found for about a third of the doors; for the rest the route is the group's site or its people page. Before sending, each route should be confirmed on the live page.

**Spread.** No country passes about a tenth: the United States has 15 (10%), the United Kingdom 13, Germany 10, the Netherlands and Canada 9 each. The list leans to Europe (83 doors, counting Turkey). Latin America has 7 doors, Africa 3, and New Zealand and Norway none. These gaps are not for want of looking: many strong groups there publish papers but no code under their own name (see "Set aside"). Schools of thought, roughly: applied cryptography and secure computation; formal verification of protocols and systems; systems and hardware security; network security, Internet measurement and censorship; privacy engineering and usable privacy; decentralised, peer-to-peer and Byzantine-tolerant systems; local-first and replicated data; decentralised identity and data sovereignty (Solid, verifiable credentials); the free-software and civic commons, community networks and digital rights.

**Weaker doors.** About twenty rows carry a note: funding by or building for big platforms or a single crypto company (for example MIT DCI, IC3, Berkeley Hydro, Edinburgh Blockchain Technology Lab, Aarhus COBRA, C4DT, Maribor Blockchain Lab); ties to a state or defence (NICT, TII, Tsinghua, CMU-Africa's government digital-ID work); dual-use attack tools; or a fit only on one side of the ethos (free-software or open-data groups with no security work, and security groups with little decentralised work). They stay on the list for the author to decide.
