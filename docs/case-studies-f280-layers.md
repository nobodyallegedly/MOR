## In plain words

**The rule.** Anyone who is not an owner (a helper named in the plan, a role such as a relay or a referrer, a pool paid by plays or reading, a platform's or a service's cut) is paid first, off the top, as overhead. What is left goes to the owners alone, each by their share. A plan that puts a non-owner among the owners, in the rest, is invalid. "Shared ownership means shared fruits."

**What the case studies wrote, and what they now write.** Several studies wrote one flat list: the members' shares, a reposter's share, a tools share, the service's cut, side by side, as if they were all owners. Each such list is now written in layers: *whose envelope it is, what comes off the top and to whom, what is left to whom.* Where money passes from one envelope to the next (a platform paying a work, a pool paying a library), it reaches the pointer the next work's agreement designates, and that agreement divides it among its own owners. So a streaming service's subscriptions are the service's own envelope: the pool for the works comes off the top, as its cost of content, divided by viewing; what is left is the service's owners'; and each work's agreement divides what reaches it by ownership. This is F283's answer to "How can it collide with a publishing envelope deal?": it does not, layer by layer.

**What did not change.** Nothing else in any study: no style edits, no other fixes. Each study keeps its voice and length; most changes are a clause or a sentence. Two studies (15 and 16) needed no rewrite: every share in them already pays a non-owner who is nowhere set beside an owner, so they read off the top as written; their new drafts differ from the previous only by the note saying so. If a draft that differs only by its note is not wanted, those two can be dropped.

**Where there are no owners.** Case 12 (a worker, a client and a platform) has no stake anywhere, nor do case 04's budgets or case 19's pooled payment and bounty. No owner was invented: each is written as F280 reads it today, every share off the top and nothing left for stakes, and the first question below asks what that should mean.

**The draft notes.** Each new draft opens with a note in the style of the earlier ones: "Draft N rewritten in layers under F280 and F283 (`docs/case-studies-f280-layers.md`), nothing else changed: …", then the earlier notes unchanged.

## Questions for Nobody, allegedly

One at a time, the first asked now; each names its area. Each was checked against the working rule (F265) first: they ask what F280 means where it has nothing to divide, or what a study says, not a participant's choice. None is decided in the texts: where a question is open, the text says only what F280 as written already says.

**Q1. A deal with no owners: what does F280 mean when nobody holds a stake?** *(area: core business, Agreements splits; F280, F283's "the no-stake deals (12) looked at again then")* *(asked now)*

*In plain words:* a cleaner books a job through a platform. The client pays; the worker is paid; the platform takes its fee. Nobody owns anything in that deal: the job's agreement writes no stake. Under F280 as written, everyone is paid off the top and nothing is left over. So the "overhead" is the whole payment, and the worker gets none of the protection an owner gets (the platform raising its cut dilutes nobody, because nobody is in the rest). The same shape appears in case 04 (a budget split among projects by a vote), in case 19 (a company's one monthly payment for its whole dependency tree; a bounty paying a contributor and a reviewer), in case 14 (the hiring agreement paying a recruiter), in case 11 if the funders' pool is read as an envelope, and in the unwritten layer's cooperative and members' budget.

- **(a) As written, a stated cost.** A plan with no stake is all off the top; F280 and F270 do not reach it. *This is how the texts now stand.*
- **(b) The worker holds the stake in the job.** Agreements already lets an agreement write a stake in a vow (rule 14, F247). The job's agreement would write the worker's stake in her vow; the platform's fee and the helpers come off the top; what is left is the worker's by ownership, with F280's and F270's protection. In delivery, the restaurant (the meal's vow) would be a stake too. It changes what case 12 says about every trade.
- **(c) Plans with no stakes are exempt from F280.** Stated cost: the subtle dilution F280 stopped returns in those plans. (F283 did not take this option for pools.)
- *A fourth reading, for budgets only:* a budget is money to spend, not income to share (case 21's own open question 8). The association or commune pays each project by its own payment, with a receipt, and case 04 stops calling it a split plan. Case 19's pooled payment could be read the same way, or as a share off the top of the company's own app envelope, the rest to the company's owners.

*My lean:* (b) for trades (12), the fourth reading for budgets (04, and 19's pool); (a) for a bounty until then. But (b) changes what the case study claims about every gig, so it is yours.

**Q2 (next). A seller selling under her own vow, or reselling what she bought: is she the stake, so that "the rest" is hers?** *(area: core business, Agreements stakes; F280, rule 14)*

If you choose (b), case 13's resale sentence changes back to "all of it off the top".
