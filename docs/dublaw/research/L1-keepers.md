## 1. In plain words

1. **The frame I brought holds only in part.** It holds where equals contract with no one in the middle: a fixed text, blanks to fill, and a new version published beside the old. It breaks in four places, listed next.
2. **Old versions almost never retire.** In 2024, 39 of every 100 ISDA master agreements were still on the 1992 form, 22 years after the 2002 form. One tanker form went 48 years without revision. Keepers stop offering a text; they cannot make people leave it.
3. **Where there is a hub, keepers amend in place.** Card networks, exchanges, arbitration bodies and websites bind people to "the rules as they stand from time to time". Of 500 popular US websites studied, 476 kept the right to change their terms. MOR cannot do this, by design.
4. **Free text never went away.** Every successful form has a place where parties write their own words: ISDA's Schedule, shipping riders, the "Modifications" box on a cover page. The best keepers do not forbid it. They put it in one visible place.
5. **To change many live contracts at once, the working tool is a signed adherence.** One letter, signed once, changes every covered contract with every other party that signed. ISDA's interest-rate protocol passed 16,000 parties.
6. **Competing forms are thinned by naming and sorting, not by banning.** A short name for each text, a list that says which texts are current, and a chooser that offers only the recommended ones did more than any prohibition.
7. **Testing with real readers before release is rare among private keepers and strong among public ones.** A US mortgage form was tested on 858 people before release. After release the tests are courts, misuse and trolls, and keepers answer with guidance and optional fixes.
8. **A keeper's real powers are small: a name, a list and a place of publication.** Almost all of them disclaim liability for the text.

## 2. Case by case

### 2.0 Does the evidence break the frame? (asked to be said first)

The frame: successful keepers hold a small fixed master text, push variation into named parameters and short confirmations, and change things by a new version that parties adhere to, not by amending in place.

| Part of the frame | Verdict | The evidence that decides it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A fixed text that nobody edits | **Holds** | ISDA's printed master is altered only to insert names [I1, s; traced]. Common Paper's cover page points to standard terms at a versioned address [P1, r; traced]. The SAFE has both sides state they changed nothing but the blanks [Y2, s; traced]. |
| Variation lives in named parameters | **Holds in part** | Parameters exist everywhere, but so does a free-text box: ISDA Schedule Part 5 (background), Common Paper's "MNDA Modifications" [P1, r], Bonterms' "Additional Terms" [P6, r], shipping riders [X9, s]. ISDA reviewed thousands of agreements to find clauses that said the same thing in different words [I25, s; traced]. |
| A small frame | **Weak** | ISDA's master grew from 18 to 27 pages [I3, s; counted]. GPL version 3 is 5,643 words against 2,910 for version 2 [O13, r; counted, my count]. FIDIC's 2017 forms are about 60% longer than 1999's [X5, s; counted]. None lost its place, though old short forms lingered. |
| Change by new version, not amendment in place | **Breaks where there is a hub** | 476 of 500 websites reserve a right to change terms [X1, s; counted]. Arbitration rules apply as they stand when the case starts [X4, s; traced]. The bank message for a letter of credit can say "UCP latest version" [T13, s; traced]. Bonterms edited its Version 1.0 text in January 2024 without a new number [P7, r; traced]. |
| The new version replaces the old | **Breaks** | 1992 ISDA form: 39% in 2024 [I4, s; counted]. ASBATANKVOY 1977 stood unrevised until 2025 [X8, s; traced]. Creative Commons 1.0 to 3.0 all remain valid [C4, s; traced]. |

**My reading (inferred):** the pattern that wins depends on whether there is a hub. With a hub and continuing membership, amending in place wins, and the check is notice, a regulator, a vote or easy exit. Among peers with no hub, the keeper never touches a live contract; change is by a new edition or a signed adherence. MOR is the second world by construction.

### 2.1 ISDA

**Fixed and parameter.** The printed master agreement is fixed. Choices, additions and amendments go in the Schedule; the money terms of each trade go in a Confirmation; all of them together form one agreement [I1, I2, s; traced]. The credit support annex repeats the pattern: printed paragraphs, then one negotiated paragraph [I2, s; traced].

**Version naming and choice.** Forms are named by year: 1987, 1992, 2002 [I1, s; traced]. The parties choose a form when they sign; nothing forces the newer one (background). Take-up of 2002 was slow at first [I5, s; traced].

**Retirement.** None. A 2024 ISDA survey of 42 institutions reports 56% of master agreements on the 2002 form, 39% on the 1992 form, and 7% on 1992 forms upgraded to 2002 terms [I4, s; counted]. Those add to 102% as summarised, so the PDF needs checking.

**The definitions booklets: the most useful lesson in the case.** The 2006 Definitions were changed by numbered supplements. By 2021 there were between 70 and nearly 90, depending on the source and the date of counting [I7, I8, s; counted, sources differ]. A reader had to stack them by hand. The 2021 Definitions dropped that: every change republishes the whole booklet as a numbered, dated version, with a comparison against the one before [I7, s; traced]. A confirmation takes the version in force on the trade date unless it names another [I9, s; traced, summary speaks of the matrices]. The keeper moved from patches to whole restated versions. At least twelve versions existed by mid-2026 [I10, s; inferred from numbered mapping tables].

**Protocols.** A party signs one adherence letter and uploads it; ISDA posts it on a public list; the letter changes every covered agreement between that party and every other adherent [I11, s; traced]. Some protocols need matching questionnaires as well [I11, s]. Adherence to the interest-rate fallbacks protocol cannot be withdrawn, except to stop it applying to later joiners [I12, s; traced]. Counts, all from ISDA's own pages as summarised:

| Protocol | Adhering parties | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2020 IBOR Fallbacks, at launch (23 October 2020) | 257 | [I13, s; counted] |
| the same, on its effective date (25 January 2021) | more than 12,000 | [I14, s; counted] |
| the same, page updated 12 February 2026 | 16,355 | [I15, s; counted] |
| August 2012 Dodd-Frank | 34,106 | [I16, s; counted] |
| 2018 US Resolution Stay | 33,007 | [I17, s; counted] |
| 2018 Benchmarks Supplement | 897 | [I18, s; counted] |

The last row matters: a protocol nobody is pushed to sign gathers few signatures. The large counts all had a regulator or a hard deadline behind them (inferred).

**Proliferation.** German, French and European master agreements coexist with ISDA's; none was found to have died [I27, s; traced]. ISDA itself added French-law and Irish-law masters in 2018 [I27, s]. For the free text inside its own form, ISDA launched a Clause Library in 2020: standard wordings for the most-negotiated clauses [I25, s; traced].

**Testing and attack.** Before release: consultations. The 2018 fallbacks consultation drew answers from 164 entities, summarised by an outside firm [I20, s; counted, the summary was garbled]. After release: courts. English and US courts read one section (when a party may withhold payment) in opposite ways [I23, s; traced]. ISDA's answer in 2014 was an optional amendment form, not a new master [I24, s; traced].

**Who keeps it, and what they answer for.** A member association since 1985, over 1,000 members in 79 countries [I19, s; counted]. It holds copyright and sells the forms [I22, s; traced]. It commissions legal opinions on whether netting holds, for about 90 jurisdictions [I21, s; counted]. It offers its content as-is (s, from a bank's copy of the licence terms).

**Figures of use.** Over-the-counter derivatives stood at 846 trillion US dollars notional in mid-2025 [I28, s; counted]. The share under an ISDA master: not found.

### 2.2 Creative Commons

**Fixed and parameter.** Four elements (attribution, share-alike, non-commercial, no derivatives) combine into six licences; the legal text of each is fixed (background). A licensor chooses elements and version, nothing else.

**Version naming.** Numbered: 1.0 (December 2002), 2.0 (May 2004), 2.5 (June 2005), 3.0 (February 2007), 4.0 (25 November 2013) [C1, C3, s; traced]. Version 4.0 took more than two years, with four public drafts between April 2012 and September 2013, discussed on a mailing list and a wiki [C2, C3, s; traced].

**Retirement.** Old versions stay valid and enforceable; the keeper recommends 4.0 [C4, s; traced]. A licence can be "retired": in 2007 the Developing Nations licence and one Sampling licence were retired because almost nobody used them. Retirement means the page stays, existing works keep the licence, and the chooser stops offering it [C5, s; traced].

**Proliferation.** Under 3.0 and earlier, the licences were rewritten ("ported") for more than 50 countries [C6, s; counted, sources give 50, 55 and 60]. Version 4.0 stopped that: one international text, with official translations that are not separate licences [C7, s; traced]. There were 30 translations by March 2023 [C8, s; counted]. The keeper cut dozens of national variants down to one text.

**Compatibility.** The share-alike licence lets an adaptation go under the same licence "this version or later", or under a licence on a list the keeper maintains [C17, r; traced]. In 2015 the keeper put GPL version 3 on that list, one way only [C12, s; traced].

**The Wikipedia move: amendment in place by a moving pointer.** Wikipedia's text was under a different licence "version 1.2 or any later version". In November 2008 that licence's keeper published version 1.3, with a new section letting large wikis republish under a Creative Commons licence, inside a time limit [C13, s; traced]. Wikipedia's community voted, 75.8% in favour, and the change took effect in June 2009 [C14, s; counted]. Works marked "1.2 only" could not be reached. A keeper changed the terms of millions of existing works by publishing a successor, because the works had named "or later".

**Testing.** A funded study of what "non-commercial" means to creators and users was published in 2009 [C9, s; traced]. A 2018 usability study ran 81 interviews and found people could not explain the differences between the licences [C10, s; counted]. The licence chooser was rebuilt with rounds of usability testing [C11, s; traced]. A formal comprehension test of the short deeds: not found.

**Attack after.** Courts in several countries have enforced the licences [C15, s; traced]. The real attack came from inside: licences before 4.0 end automatically on any breach, and some licensors used small attribution slips to demand money [C16, s; traced]. Version 4.0 restores the licence if the breach is cured within 30 days of discovery [C17, r; traced]. The old versions cannot be fixed; the keeper can only urge people to move.

**Who keeps it.** A non-profit. The 4.0 text says the keeper is not a party to its licences [C17, r; traced].

**Figures.** 1.4 billion works in 2017, about 2.5 billion in 2022, by the keeper's own count [C18, s; counted].

### 2.3 Open-source licences

**Fixed and parameter.** The licence text is fixed; the parameters are a copyright line and, for some, a version choice. The MIT licence is 169 words [O13, r; counted, my count].

**Who approves.** The Open Source Initiative checks a licence against a fixed ten-point definition (background). Review aims to finish within about 60 days [O1, s; traced]. Since 2023 a new licence must fill a gap, and old ("legacy") licences are approved or refused as written, with no text changes [O1, s; traced]. The keeper never withdraws approval; a licence is retired at its own steward's request [O1, s; background for the examples].

**Proliferation, and what reduced it.** A committee reported in 2006. It did not remove licences; it sorted them into groups such as popular, redundant, superseded and voluntarily retired [O2, s; traced]. One researcher counted 205 licence names listed since 2000 [O4, s; counted]. What concentrated use in practice was a default: in 2015, MIT covered 44.69% of licensed GitHub projects, with a jump in licensing in mid-2013, when GitHub launched a simple chooser [O3, s; counted; the causal link is inferred].

**SPDX identifiers.** A project under the Linux Foundation gives each licence text a short identifier. Its list held 743 entries on 18 November 2026, 32 of them deprecated, plus 86 exceptions [O9, r; counted, my count from the published data]. Four things in it are worth a keeper's attention:

- Once given, an identifier never changes [O10, r; traced].
- A licence is added only when its text is final and public [O10, r; traced].
- Matching rules say which differences still count as the same licence: spacing, capital letters, the copyright line, and marked replaceable text. Added clauses mean no match [O10, s; traced].
- Deprecated identifiers stay listed and stay valid for old documents. The bare identifier for GPL version 2 was deprecated for two clearer ones, "only" and "or later" [O9, r; traced].

Sixteen identifiers begin with "MIT" and forty-three with "BSD" [O9, r; counted]. One distribution treated 33 MIT variants as one [O14, s; counted]. Free-text variants thrived, and a naming body made them tolerable.

**GPL version 3: public drafting.** Announced November 2005; four discussion drafts from January 2006; final on 29 June 2007 [O5, s; traced]. Three ways in: a website for comments on any sentence, international conferences, and four committees of about 130 people sorted by their role [O5, O6, s; traced]. One author decided [O5, s]. The count of comments: not found.

**"Or any later version".** GPL version 2 lets a program name "any later version", and then the recipient may choose which to follow [O13, r; traced]. The Linux kernel names version 2 only [O7, s; traced]. Its lead developer's stated reason was that "or later" means trusting whoever runs the keeper, for good [O15, s; traced].

**Compatibility.** Two licences can each be sound and still not combine: Apache 2.0 code may go into a GPL version 3 work but not a version 2 work, and not back [O11, s; traced]. Incompatibility is a cost that falls on users of both texts, long after drafting.

**Attack after.** When owners wanted different terms they did not break the old texts; they left for new ones, and the keeper's only weapon was to refuse the name "open source" [O12, s; inferred].

### 2.4 Common Paper, Bonterms and oneNDA

**Common Paper.** The signed cover page says the agreement is the cover page plus the standard terms of a named version, identical to those at a named address [P1, r; traced]. The cover page holds the variables (purpose, dates, term, governing law) and a box for modifications [P1, r]. The mutual NDA terms run to about 1,050 words, the cloud agreement to about 5,100 [P1, P2, r; counted, my count, markup included]. Versions are numbered: the cloud agreement has 1.0, 1.1, 2.0, 2.1 and 3.0, from June 2022 to autumn 2026 [P2, r; traced]. The keeper promises each version stays unchanged at its own address [P2, r; traced]. Version 2.1 changed only a cross-reference and still got a new number [P3, s; traced]. Signed agreements stay on their version [P4, s; traced]. Drafted by a committee of over 40 lawyers; free to use and modify [P2, r; traced]. More than 10,000 companies have signed on its platform, by its own count [P5, s; counted].

**Bonterms.** The same pattern: a cover page incorporates the terms, sets key terms, and may carry "Additional Terms", which override the standard text [P6, r; traced]. The cloud terms are about 4,950 words [P6, r; counted, my count]. Six major drafts, three sub-committees, seven months [P6, r; traced]. Only Version 1.0 exists, and its text was corrected in January 2024 under the same number [P7, r; traced]. For MOR's purposes this is the plainest example of what a version label without a hash permits.

**oneNDA.** A stricter rule: change only the variables. Change the body and the document may no longer use the name [P9, s; traced]. Version 2 came in March 2022 to fix reported problems; either version may be used [P9, s; traced]. The strict rule produced companion documents instead of edits [P10, s; inferred]. A public directory listed 369 adopters in October 2021 [P11, s; counted]. A drafting critic called the text mediocre though harmless [P12, s; traced]. The keeper was bought twice within a year [P10, s; traced]: a standard's steward can change hands.

**Proliferation.** The three coexist and adopters mix them [P8, s; traced]. No merger found.

**Testing.** Committee review only; no test with readers was found for any of the three.

### 2.5 The SAFE (Y Combinator)

**Fixed and parameter.** One self-contained document with blanks: amount, valuation cap or discount, names, date [Y2, s; traced]. Since 2018 both sides state that neither changed the form except to fill blanks [Y1, Y2, s; traced].

**The move from pre-money to post-money (2018).** Founders had begun using the form for whole rounds, and nobody could tell how much of the company had been promised until a later priced round. The new form measures an investor's share after all such money is counted [Y1, Y3, s; traced]. The economics changed (the founders now carry the dilution from later SAFEs) under the same name [Y7, s; traced].

**Version naming.** File names and headers carry v1.0, v1.1, v1.2 [Y2, s; traced]. No public list of changes between them was found.

**Retirement.** By removal. The keeper took the old forms off its page, and in 2021 dropped the variant with both a cap and a discount [Y4, s; traced]. Signed ones remain ordinary contracts (background), and the dropped variant still appears in deals [Y4, s].

**Proliferation.** A rival form launched in 2014 did not spread as widely [Y8, s; traced, from commentary]. On one large platform, SAFEs were 93% of the earliest rounds in mid-2026, and 91% of those were post-money [Y5, s; counted, one platform only].

**Testing and attack.** No public drafting process: written in-house. Attack after: a bankruptcy court had to decide where SAFE holders rank, and one dispute arose from a blank filled in wrongly [Y6, s; traced]. A parameter filled in by hand is itself a place where things fail.

### 2.6 ICC Incoterms

**Fixed and parameter.** A three-letter rule is fixed. The parameters are the named place and the edition year; the recommended wording carries all three [T2, s; traced].

**Version naming and choice.** Editions are named by year: 2000, 2010, 2020, every ten years since 1980 [T1, s; traced]. The rules bind only when a contract names them [T2, s; traced]. If no year is named, the keeper warns of trouble [T2, s].

**Retirement.** None. A contract naming the 2010 edition is still read under it [T3, s; traced].

**Proliferation.** The keeper prunes its own list: thirteen rules became eleven in 2010 [T1, s; (background) for thirteen]. Parties still alter rules ("FOB stowed"), and the keeper gives no meaning for such variants [T5, s; traced].

**Testing and attack.** For 2020, a drafting group of eight or nine experts weighed more than 3,000 comments from national committees over about three years [T4, s; counted]. Misuse persists: in one survey, 49% of freight forwarders had used a sea-only rule for container freight [T6, s; counted, from a slide deck citing the study].

**Who keeps it.** The International Chamber of Commerce, which holds the trademark and copyright and sells the book [T7, s; traced].

### 2.7 ICC UCP 600

**Fixed and parameter.** 39 articles (background). A letter of credit is under the rules only if it says so, and it may change or exclude any article [T9, s; traced].

**Version naming.** A number: UCP 500 (1993), UCP 600 (2007) [T8, s; traced].

**The keeper chose not to revise.** No new edition in 19 years. National committees voted against a revision in 2017 and again recently, preferring stability [T11, s; traced]. Change goes around the text instead: a practice guide revised in 2013 and 2023, a versioned electronic supplement (now 2.1), and official opinions [T12, s; traced]. The cost is admitted complexity [T11, s].

**A moving pointer.** The bank message that issues a credit has a field for the applicable rules, with codes such as "UCP latest version" [T13, s; traced, from a 2008 copy of the standard].

**Testing and attack.** The 2007 text drew over 5,000 comments and passed 94 to 0 [T10, s; counted]. After release: by the keeper's own figure about 70% of documents were refused at first presentation, though other sources give lower numbers [T14, s; counted, sources differ]. The keeper runs an opinion service and an expert dispute panel [T15, s; traced].

### 2.8 Model laws: UNCITRAL and the Uniform Law Commission

**UNCITRAL.** Working groups of states draft; the Commission adopts; decisions are by consensus where possible [U1, s; traced]. Seventy member states [U2, s; counted]. A model law is published with a guide to enacting it (s). Versions are carried in the title, "with amendments as adopted in 2006", and since July 2026 a further set [U3, U5, s; traced]. The arbitration rules exist as 1976, 2010, 2013 and 2021 texts; none is retired; parties are presumed to mean the rules in force when the arbitration starts [U4, s; traced]. Law based on the arbitration model stands in 93 states [U3, s; counted]; "based on or influenced by" is a loose test. Court decisions are collected in a public case-law system [U6, s; traced].

**Uniform Law Commission.** A study committee first, for at least a year; then a drafting committee with a reporter; then every act is read line by line at no fewer than two annual meetings, and passes by a vote of the states [L1, L2, L4, s; traced]. Drafting meetings are open to observers [L2, s]. A separate style committee reviews drafts [L3, s]. Acts are named with their year and "last amended" date (s). Acts are withdrawn or declared obsolete (s). Two failures teach more than the successes:

- A full rewrite of the sales article of the Uniform Commercial Code, approved in 2003, was enacted by no state and withdrawn in 2011 [L5, s; traced].
- A software-contracts act lost its partner body over the quality of its drafting, was enacted in two states, and was abandoned in 2003 [L6, s; traced].

Against those, the narrower 2022 amendments were enacted in 33 states within about three years [L7, s; counted]. Narrow changes to a settled text travel; rewrites do not (inferred).

### 2.9 The craft of drafting

**Adams' categories.** The current edition (the fifth, 2023) sorts the verbs of a contract into categories, each with its own wording [D1, s; traced]:

| Category | What it does | His wording |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agreement | the parties agree, once, at the top | "agree" |
| Performance | something is done by signing | "hereby" and a present-tense verb |
| Obligation | a duty on the party who is the subject | "shall" |
| Discretion | a party is free to act | "may" |
| Prohibition | a party must not act | "shall not" |
| Policy | a rule of the contract that is nobody's duty | present tense, or "will" |
| Condition (handled beside the categories) | something must be so for a result to follow | "must" inside the condition |
| Declaration | a statement of fact | "states" |
| Intention, recommendation | what the parties expect or point out | plain statements |

Sources: [D1, D2, s; traced]. Two notes. The list in the brief for this report (which included recital and statement of fact as categories) does not match what was found: statements of fact are his "declaration", and recitals are not in the list of categories as summarised. He also dropped one category ("belief") between editions [D3, s; traced], so even a careful vocabulary is revised. Another authority rejects "shall" altogether [D4, s; traced].

**ISO 24495-1 (2023).** Plain language is defined by outcome: readers can find what they need, understand it and use it [D5, s; traced]. Four principles: relevant, findable, understandable, usable. Under the fourth, the standard asks the author to evaluate the document with readers during and after drafting [D5, s; traced]. It measures by what readers do, not by a readability score (s). A second part on legal writing was published in August 2025 [D6, s; traced].

**Does plain language work?** One aviation business reported negotiation time down 60% after rewriting its contracts [D7, s; counted, from a business magazine, with a critic noting most of the gain came from fairer terms]. A 2022 study found that sentence structure, not technical vocabulary, is what makes legal text hard [D8, s; traced]. An older study found plain wording helped, but understanding stayed low [D9, s; traced]. Plain words are necessary and not enough.

### 2.10 Testing legal texts

**As code.** A 2020 OECD paper proposes an official machine-readable version of rules beside the written one [R1, s; traced]. One open system writes each test as a period, inputs and expected outputs [R3, s; traced]. Catala, a language built for statutes, places the legal text and its code side by side, written by a lawyer and a programmer together; its authors report finding an error in an existing implementation of French family benefits [R4, s; traced; sources differ on which implementation]. A later housing-benefit model exposed rounding and date ambiguities [R5, s; traced]. Writing a rule so a machine can run it is itself a review of the rule.

**With readers.** The strongest case found: a US mortgage disclosure went through ten rounds of one-to-one testing in 2011 and 2012, then a trial with 858 consumers in 20 places, where the new forms produced more correct answers [F1, F2, F3, s; counted]. A UK study tested 18 ways of presenting terms in six online experiments: a question-and-answer layout raised understanding by 36%; a summary table raised understanding of what it listed and lowered it for what it left out [F4, s; counted, from a secondary copy]. A summary helps and harms at once.

### 2.11 Cases added because they test the frame

**Rulebooks with a hub.** Card networks publish dated editions of their rules about twice a year [X3, s; traced]. Members are bound as the rules change.

**Shipping forms (BIMCO).** More than 350 standard contracts and clauses, named by code and year, adopted by a documentary committee, each published with notes explaining the changes [X7, s; traced]. Its editing tool keeps every change to the standard wording visible, and a standard clause has the issuing party warrant that the template is authentic [X7, s; traced]. Old forms persist for decades under layers of rider clauses [X8, X9, s; traced]. The 2022 edition of one form was made longer on purpose, to absorb the riders [X7, s; traced].

**Construction forms.** In a 2022 UK survey of over 950 people, 71% had used one family of forms in the past year [X10, s; counted]. These are amended heavily in practice; FIDIC's 2019 "Golden Principles" say a contract that breaks the form's balance should not carry its name [X5, s; traced]. Within a named version, one keeper issued amendments in 2019, 2020 and 2023 [X6, s; traced].

**What the research on boilerplate says.** Standard terms gain value as more people use them, which makes them sticky even when flawed [A3, s; traced]. One clause in government bonds went unrepaired for years after a court read it dangerously, in a study of over a thousand contracts [A1, A2, s; counted]. Standard clauses work as modules because a reader need not look inside them [A4, s; traced]. Trade bodies are one of the few suppliers of new terms, since nobody owns a clause [A5, s; traced].

## 3. What a keeper of agreement cMIPs could copy

*This whole section is my reading (inferred). It proposes nothing as decided.*

What MOR already fixes, from the Development rules as drafted: a cMIP is named by the hash of its content and never changes; many cMIPs may compete for one task; an agreement names at most one per task; adoption sorts them [R7, r; traced].

### 3.1 Could copy

1. **Whole restated versions, never patches.** ISDA abandoned supplements because nobody could stack 70 to 90 of them. MOR's "published again" is already the 2021 design. What the keeper adds is ISDA's other half: a comparison against the version before, and a plain note of why it changed. BIMCO does the same with explanatory notes.
2. **A new number for every change, even a typo.** Common Paper renumbered for a cross-reference. In MOR the hash already changes; the human-readable number should never lag it. Bonterms shows what happens when it does.
3. **The cover-page pattern.** Fixed text named by version, plus a short signed page of parameters. It is the closest existing form to "Terms name a cMIP, and the parameters sit in the Terms".
4. **One visible place for free text.** Every case has one. A keeper that pretends otherwise gets hidden edits; BIMCO's tool and the Golden Principles are what that looks like once it has happened. The question for a cMIP is where the free words go and what the client shows.
5. **Adherence as the tool for change across many agreements.** One signed act, a public list of who signed, effect only between those who both signed. It fits "nothing is updated, it is signed again". The lesson from the 897 is that the tool does not create the will to use it.
6. **Retirement as a status, not a deletion.** Creative Commons' "retired", SPDX's "deprecated", OSI's "superseded" and "voluntarily retired" all mean: still valid for what was made under it, no longer offered to newcomers. In MOR a text cannot be deleted anyway, so a keeper's status list is the only retirement there is.
7. **Sorting instead of banning.** OSI's groups and a chooser that offers few options did the work. A keeper could publish, and sign, its own list: current, superseded, redundant with another, not recommended, and why.
8. **Entry rules for the list.** SPDX's two are cheap and sharp: the text is final, and it does not match one already listed.
9. **Compatibility as a published fact.** Open source learned late that two good texts may not combine. A keeper of cMIPs could state, for each text, which others it has been tested beside.
10. **Tests published with the text.** Test cases in the manner of the rules-as-code work (inputs, expected outputs) are what a competing author, a client builder and a reader can all check. The Development rules already ask a client to show the numbers on an example before a user signs; a keeper's test cases are the natural source of honest examples.
11. **Drafting in a pair.** Catala's lawyer-and-programmer pair, the Uniform Law Commission's separate style committee, and its line-by-line reading are three forms of the same thing: a second kind of eye before release.
12. **Testing with readers before release.** ISO 24495-1 asks for it; the mortgage form shows the method; private keepers mostly skip it. This is where a new keeper could be better than the ones studied.
13. **A vocabulary of verbs.** Adams' categories fit a cMIP vocabulary well at four points and badly at one. Obligation, discretion, prohibition and condition map to what a rule can state and a verifier can sometimes check. "Performance" maps to what a signature itself does. "Policy" is what most of a cMIP is: a rule that is nobody's duty. The poor fit is "declaration": bytes can prove that a party said something, never that it is true. Intention and recommendation look like companion material, not rules.
14. **Narrow changes travel; rewrites stall.** The 2003 sales rewrite got no enactments; the 2002 ISDA form was slow; the narrow 2022 amendments moved fast.
15. **Explain the versions people are still on.** Since old versions never retire, a keeper's duty to people on old texts does not end at the new release. Creative Commons' trolls live in the old versions.

### 3.2 Must not copy, because MOR differs

1. **The moving pointer.** "Latest version", "as amended from time to time", "or any later version". It is the dominant tool wherever there is a hub, and the Wikipedia case shows its power is real. In MOR an agreement names a hash, and nobody amends in place. A keeper must not rebuild the pointer at another layer, for instance a client that quietly resolves a name to the newest hash. Whether parties may *choose* something like "or later" in their own Terms is a question, not a finding (see section 4).
2. **Matching rules that call different texts the same.** SPDX can say two texts with different spacing are one licence. The core judges only bytes: one changed byte is another text. Any "these are equivalent" statement would have to be a signed opinion of the keeper, outside the core, and labelled as opinion.
3. **Control through the name.** oneNDA, Incoterms, FIDIC and "open source" all police a name. On MOR anyone may publish a competing text, and the Development rules say adoption decides. A keeper can hold a reputation; it should not need a trademark.
4. **Retirement by removal.** The SAFE's keeper retires a form by taking it off its page. Nothing on MOR is taken off anything.
5. **Editing under a kept version label.** Bonterms and NEC4. Impossible at the hash, but still possible in a keeper's own documents and lists, which is where the discipline has to live.
6. **The silent in-house rewrite.** The SAFE changed who bears dilution under the same name with no public draft. A keeper that answers to nobody can do that; one that wants competing authors to trust its list cannot.
7. **Leaning on courts to finish the text.** ISDA and Creative Commons were completed by judgments. A cMIP's rule is what a verifier runs; where it is silent there is no court behind it. What cannot be checked from bytes has to be said plainly to be a record, not a rule.
8. **Membership as the source of authority.** ISDA, ICC and BIMCO speak for members who pay. A keeper of cMIPs has no members to speak for, only texts and a record of being right.

## 4. Questions

*None of these is answered here. Each is tagged with where it belongs.*

1. **Development layer.** Should a cMIP for agreements carry a published set of test cases as part of what the keeper releases, and should a text without them be listed differently?
2. **Core business.** May Terms say, in the parties' own words, that a named successor version will apply when some condition is met ("or later" by the parties' choice)? Or does "nothing is updated, it is signed again" rule that out? The research shows this clause is powerful and dangerous; the spec was not checked for it here.
3. **Core business.** Is there, or should there be, one signed act by which a party adheres to a new version across many of its agreements at once, taking effect only with others who signed the same? This session did not check whether the grammar already allows it.
4. **Development layer.** Where does free text go in an agreement built on cMIPs: one named field, as on a cover page, or nowhere?
5. **Presentation.** What does a client show when an agreement carries words outside any cMIP, so that they are as visible as BIMCO's marked changes?
6. **Development layer.** Should the keeper publish a signed status list of texts (current, superseded, redundant, not recommended), and may that list ever say two different texts are equivalent in effect?
7. **Presentation.** Should a new version always be shown with a comparison against the one before and the reason for the change?
8. **Core business.** What does DubLaw answer for? Every keeper studied disclaims liability. Is that DubLaw's position too, or does it stand behind something narrower, such as its test cases?
9. **Development layer.** Is Adams' set of categories, or a reduced form of it, a sound starting vocabulary for agreement cMIPs, with "declaration" treated as a record and not a rule?
10. **Presentation.** Will agreement texts be tested with ordinary readers before release, in the way ISO 24495-1 asks, and who would do it?
11. **Development layer.** When a cMIP is superseded, what does the keeper owe the people whose agreements still name the old one?
12. **Core business.** Does DubLaw draft alone, in a pair, or in the open with public drafts, and who has the last word on a text DubLaw publishes?
