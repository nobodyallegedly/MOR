## 1. In plain words

**First, where the evidence breaks the frame.**

1. **Delivery: the frame breaks.** Most practice does not wait for the receiver's receipt. Trade rules treat delivery as done when goods reach a carrier [E1][E3]. Payment platforms accept a carrier's "delivered" scan as proof [E25][E27]. Escrow pays out when the buyer stays silent after that scan [E31][E32]. Only consumer sales law and freight receipts fit the frame [E6][E34]. What holds everywhere is narrower: *the sender's own word is never enough; a record the sender cannot make alone is.* (traced)
2. **Deposits: the frame bends.** In England the landlord may keep the money under an insured scheme, and on the published figures this works about as well as a held deposit: about 1% of deposits reach adjudication overall [C7]. The one like-for-like figure found shows more disputes in the insured model, 1.14% against 0.49% [C10]. What the working systems share is not "held by neither" but "no one can take it unchallenged". (counted, then inferred)
3. **Bundled assent holds up in contract law, and fails in data-protection law.** One click can bind a person to a whole linked document, arbitration clause included [A6]. Under GDPR, consent folded into other matters does not bind [B1]. Both are settled. (traced)
4. **Consent to be recorded: the frame is one regime among several.** GDPR consent is specific and withdrawable forward only [B1]. But photo practice often avoids consent as its ground, because it can be withdrawn, and uses a release, which is a contract [B9][B11]. And in many places a party to a conversation may record it with nobody's consent [B18]. (traced)

**Then, what held.**

5. **Assent as a chosen act holds, and courts ask for more than the act.** The side relying on the assent must show what the person saw and which version, as it was on that day [A14][A15]. A signed act that names the exact text by hash answers that. It does not answer whether the text was put in front of the person. (traced, then inferred)
6. **Who must prove is settled the same way in all four kinds: the one who wants the money or the benefit.** The landlord proves the damage [C12]. The seller proves the parcel arrived [E11]. The publisher proves the consent [B1]. The dealer accounts for the sale [D3]. (traced)
7. **Every working system has a rule for silence, with a clock.** Fourteen days, then the claimed deposit is paid [C14]. Two days after the scan, then the seller is paid [E31]. One year, then the Swiss tenant can take the deposit back [C25]. (counted)
8. **The measured failures sit where one side records its own performance.** About 7% of sampled postal delivery scans were made in the wrong place [E16]. A dealer sold consigned works and kept the money, with restitution of $120 million ordered [D33]. No source measures how often a delivery photo is false. (counted)

## 2. Kind by kind

### 2.0 Assent online (it runs under all four kinds)

How people read terms is R4's ground and is not repeated here. This part is only about *proof*.

**What courts require to be shown.**

- Two things: notice of the terms that a reasonable person would see, and an act that plainly shows agreement [A2][A9]. (traced)
- A button pressed without notice of terms is not assent. In Specht the licence sat below the download button, out of sight [A1][A3]. (traced)
- A link at the foot of every page, with no act tied to it, is not enough (Nguyen) [A3][A4]. (traced)
- A line beside the button saying that signing up means agreeing to the linked terms can be enough (Meyer). The person is bound without opening the link [A5][A6]. (traced)
- The same design can fail on looks: a link that does not look like a link (Cullinane) [A7][A8]; small or pale type, or no statement that the click means agreement (Berman) [A9][A10]. (traced)
- The line is still moving. In 2024 and 2025 one appeals court upheld one sign-up screen and rejected two others [A11][A12][A13]. (traced)

**What the business must produce.** A dated chain: the exact screens and the exact version of the terms for that person's sign-up. Sample screens without dates failed [A14][A15]. (traced)

**Changed terms.** Posting a new version on a website is not notice of it [A16]. A vague statement that an email was sent, with no copy and no proof of receipt, left the older terms in force [A17]. (traced)

**How often each form holds.** A vendor's survey of 2020 cases put success at 70% for click-to-agree, 64% for sign-in notices and 14% for browse-only terms [A18]. The method and sample were not seen; treat as indicative. (counted, weak source)

**How few read.** One or two shoppers in a thousand opened the licence in one study [A20]. In another, 399 of 543 students joined a made-up service without reading its policy [A21]. This is R4's ground; the two figures are here only because they bear on what "assent" proves. (counted)

**Electronic signatures.**

- United States: a signature or record is not denied effect only for being electronic [A22]. Some matters are excluded, among them wills and family law [A23]. A signature belongs to a person if it was that person's act, shown in any manner [A24]. (traced)
- When the signer denies signing, the one relying on the signature must show it was the signer's act. A bare statement failed [A25]; a signing service's marks alone failed too [A26]. (traced)
- European Union: no signature is refused only for being electronic; a qualified signature equals a handwritten one [A27]. An advanced signature must be uniquely linked to the signer and able to identify them [A28]; under the signer's sole control, with later changes detectable *(background)*. The 2024 revision asks that wallets let people make qualified signatures free for private use [A29]. (traced)
- Switzerland: most contracts need no form at all [A30]. Where the law demands writing, only a qualified signature with a qualified timestamp equals a handwritten one [A31][A32]. If authenticity is contested with stated grounds, the party relying on the document proves it [A33][A34]. (traced)

**What this leaves for the four kinds.** Law mostly asks for *an act attributable to the person, plus notice of the terms*. It does not ask that the full text be in view. The frame's "exact text in view" is stricter than the cases. (inferred)

### 2.1 To be recorded or photographed

**Minimum content.**

- A stock agency's adult release asks for: the model's name, an address, date of birth, signature and the date signed; for a minor, a parent or guardian's name and signature; the release date must match the shoot [B15]. (traced)
- A release says whether the permission can be revoked [B11]. Scope of uses, media, territory, term and payment are standard clauses *(background)*.
- Under GDPR, consent must be given per purpose and set apart from other matters [B1]; freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous *(background for the definition's wording)*.
- Commercial use of a likeness needs prior consent in California, and written consent in New York [B13][B14]. (traced)

**How assent is proven, and what makes it fail.**

- GDPR: the one using the recording must be able to show the consent [B1], by active behaviour of the person [B6]. A pre-ticked box is not consent [B5]. Silence is not consent [B2]. (traced)
- Agencies: a signed release. Some require a handwritten signature, in ink or on a touchscreen [B15]. Witness rules differ: one agency dropped the witness in 2023 [B15]; one never needed it for adults [B16]; one requires a witness who saw the signing and is neither model nor photographer [B17]. Photo identity is asked for in some cases [B15][B17]. (traced)
- Spoken recording: United States federal law lets a party record, or one party consent [B18]. About eleven states ask for everyone's consent; lists differ [B19][B20]. Germany and Switzerland make it an offence for a participant to record a private conversation without the others' permission [B21][B22]. In the United Kingdom a party may record for their own use; sharing brings data-protection law in [B23]. (traced)
- Failure on record: releases read narrowly. Models' releases were held not to cover use of their images by clubs they had no dealings with [B34]. All-in-one acceptance drew a €50 million fine [B7]. (traced, counted)

**Who proves performance, and with what.** Performance here is *staying inside the scope*. The person recorded cannot see every use. Proof is the published use itself, found by them or by others. A model's payment, where there is one, is an ordinary payment. (inferred)

**Default when one side is silent.** No consent. Under GDPR silence and inactivity are not consent [B2]. Where one-party recording is lawful, the default runs the other way: the recording may be made [B18]. (traced)

**How it ends; what if one side disappears.**

- GDPR consent can be withdrawn at any time, as easily as given; what was done before stays lawful [B1]. After withdrawal, stored data is erased unless another ground exists, and others it was passed to are told where that is reasonable [B35]. So withdrawal reaches *held copies* going forward; it does not unmake past use. (traced)
- Consent has no fixed expiry; refreshing it now and then is recommended [B4]. (traced)
- A release as a contract can be written as irrevocable [B11]. A New York court refused to let a model undo, once adult, the consent her parent gave for her as a child [B12]. That a paid release cannot be revoked is common teaching *(background; no source found stating it squarely)*.
- Research practice sets levels of leaving: no more contact; no more access; no more use, with stored material destroyed [B28]. Samples already tested or shared cannot be pulled back [B29], and data already collected may be kept and analysed in United States guidance [B30]. (traced)
- "Dynamic consent" (2015) is an interface where participants change their choices over time [B25]. In one Australian trial 91 people registered, and no difference showed in trust, regret or knowledge [B26]. Critics name burden and fatigue [B27]. No figure was found for how many people later change a choice. (counted)
- Because consent can be withdrawn, one university's guidance prefers another ground for photographs where it is valid [B9]; a German law firm advises consent for individuals and small groups [B10]. Practice is split. (traced)
- If the photographer disappears, the copies remain. Nothing found says who then answers. (inferred)

**Failures and frauds on record.**

- 14,678 deepfake videos counted in 2019, 96% pornographic [B31]. (counted)
- One in twelve of over 3,000 United States adults reported intimate images shared without consent [B32]; 22.6% of over 16,000 people in ten countries reported image-based abuse [B33]. (counted)
- A United States law of 2025 makes platforms remove such images within 48 hours of a valid request, from May 2026 [B36][B37]. (traced)
- Forged releases: no figure found.

**Against the frame.** "Specific, and withdrawable forward only" is a fair summary of GDPR consent. It is not what the release trade settled, and it is not needed at all where one-party recording is lawful. Bundled consent fails under GDPR and holds in a release, if the release's words cover the use. (inferred)

### 2.2 To let a room and hold a deposit

**Minimum content.**

- England, from 1 May 2026: written key terms, among them the landlord's name and address, the rent and when it is due, and the deposit [C21][C22]. (traced)
- A lodger in the landlord's own home: guidance suggests the rent, the notice period, whether the term is fixed, bills, and a receipt for the deposit [C19][C20]. Guidance, not a statutory list. (traced)
- For the deposit itself: the amount, where it is held, and the scheme's details served within 30 days [C1][C2]. (traced)
- Caps: five weeks' rent in England for most lets [C4]; three months in Switzerland [C23] and in Germany [C31]. (counted)

**How assent is proven, and what makes it fail.** By the signed agreement. Without a written agreement a lodger still has a let, on "reasonable notice" terms [C19]. Assent to the *condition of the room* is a separate act: the check-in report, which both sides rely on later [C12][C13]. (traced)

**Who proves performance, and with what.**

- The deposit stays the tenant's money until the landlord justifies a deduction. The landlord carries the burden [C12]. (traced)
- Evidence accepted: the agreement, check-in and check-out reports [C12][C13]; dated photos and invoices *(background)*.
- The dispute service is free to both sides *(background for "free"; the schemes' adjudication was seen described, the price was not)*.

**Default when one side is silent.**

- England, held scheme: if the other side does not answer within 14 days, the claimant makes a sworn, witnessed statement; the scheme writes to the other side; after 14 more days of silence the claimed sum is paid [C14]. (traced)
- Switzerland: the bank pays out only with both signatures, a final payment order or a final judgment. If the landlord has brought no claim within a year of the lease ending, the tenant can demand the money [C24][C25]. (traced)
- Short lets: the host must claim within 14 days of checkout [C36][C37][C38]. On one platform the guest then has 24 hours before the platform decides [C36]; on another, an unclaimed deposit goes back by itself [C37]. (traced)

**How it ends; what if one side disappears.** By return of the deposit, agreed or adjudicated. A vanished landlord in the held model is answered by the silence rules above. In the insured model the landlord or agent has the money; agents have collapsed holding it, and the landlord stayed liable to the tenant [C15][C16]. A lodger's only route is a court [C18]. (traced)

**Held by whom: the evidence.**

| Model | Where | Who has the money | Figures found |
|---|---|---|---|
| Held by a scheme | England (custodial), Scotland | The scheme | England: 54.41% of deposits by number [C7]. Scotland: 2.44% reach dispute [C7] |
| Insured | England | Landlord or agent | 54.65% of deposit value [C7]. 90% of tenants got all or part back, one scheme's own figures [C9] |
| Blocked account | Switzerland | A bank, in the tenant's name | No dispute figure for deposits alone. All tenancy conciliation, second half of 2025: 19,240 new cases, 57.9% of closed cases agreed [C29][C30] |
| State authority | Australian states | The authority | Victoria 2023-24: 64% of bonds repaid wholly to renters [C33]. Queensland 2024-25: 57.6% [C34] |
| Landlord, kept apart | Germany | Landlord, separate from own assets | None found [C31] |
| Guarantee, no money held | Switzerland | Nobody; an insurer stands behind | About 5% of the sum a year [C26]; ten years on CHF 6,000 cost over CHF 3,268 [C27]; the insurer reclaims payouts from the tenant [C28] |
| Nothing | Lodgers; scams | Whoever took it | See frauds below |

- England and Wales, March 2025: about 4.71 million deposits protected [C6]; average deposit £1,175 [C7]; about 1% reach formal adjudication [C7]. (counted)
- Held against insured, like for like: 0.49% against 1.14% disputes in 2018, one scheme's figures [C10]. A minister's answer put tenants' wins at 57% of held disputes and 50% of insured, undated [C11], and noted that insured schemes leave no official record of how fast money is returned without a dispute [C5]. (counted, weak sources)
- No independent study comparing the models was found. The scheme figures are the schemes' own.

**Failures and frauds on record.**

- England, 2022-23 survey: of renters who paid a deposit, 81% said it was protected, 6% said not, 13% did not know [C17]. (counted)
- Penalty for not protecting: one to three times the deposit [C3]. (traced)
- Rental fraud, United Kingdom: 18,645 reports and £22.1 million lost, April 2014 to March 2018 [C39]; 4,092 reports and £12.8 million in 2025/26 [C40]. (counted)
- United States: about 65,000 rental scam reports and about $65 million lost, January 2020 to June 2025; median loss $1,000 [C41]. (counted)

**Against the frame.** Bent, not broken. An insured deposit is held by one party and works on the published figures, *because a scheme stands behind it, the burden is on the landlord and the dispute service binds both*. Where one party holds the money with nothing behind it (lodgers, scams), the failures are the ones on record. (inferred)

### 2.3 To leave goods with someone to sell (consignment)

**Minimum content.**

- Ownership, the period, the split, when payment follows a sale, who may set or cut the price, who insures, and what happens to unsold work [D1]. (traced)
- An inventory list with each delivery, signed by the receiver and returned [D2]. (traced)
- A dealer who discounts without authority may owe the owner's share on the agreed price [D1]. (traced)

**Typical splits.**

- Art galleries: commission usually 40 to 50% [D7]; an Australian source puts the traditional figure at a third or less [D8]. (counted)
- Second-hand shops: 40 to 60% to the owner [D9]. (counted, trade source)
- One online reseller pays by price band, from 20% under $100 to 60% at $300 to $749 (seen 20 November 2026) [D10]. (counted)
- One auction house fixed the seller's commission at 10% of the first $500,000 in May 2024, then went back to negotiating in December 2024 [D12]. (counted)

**How assent is proven, and what makes it fail.** By the signed agreement and the signed inventory. In New York and California the law supplies the agreement where art goes from artist to dealer: the dealer is the artist's agent, and the work and its proceeds are held in trust [D3][D5]. In California that cannot be waived [D5]. (traced)

**Who proves performance, and with what.**

- The seller's own records. The owner usually cannot see a sale happen. (inferred)
- New York gives the artist a right to an accounting with the price and the buyer's name [D3]. One model contract gives inspection of the books on five days' notice [D30]. (traced)
- The audit problem is on record: a gallery that would not open its books until a court ordered it [D3]. (traced)
- Payment deadlines after the dealer is paid: 30 days in four United States states, 90 in one, monthly in another [D29]. (counted)

**Default when one side is silent.**

- The owner does not collect: shop contracts make the goods the shop's after 5 days in one example and 30 in another [D14]. One online reseller holds for 365 days, then returns or donates as the owner chose [D11]. (counted)
- Shop periods of 60 or 90 days are common, with the price cut at 30 and 60 days [D13]. (counted, trade source)

**How it ends; what if one side disappears.**

- In Switzerland either side can end a commission at any time [D32]. (traced)
- **Title is the centre.** The owner keeps title, but against the seller's *creditors* that may not help.
  - United States: for consignments within the commercial code's definition (each delivery worth $1,000 or more; a merchant not generally known to sell others' goods), the seller is treated as owning the goods as far as its creditors are concerned, unless the owner filed a public notice [D15][D16][D17]. (traced)
  - Counted: when a sports retailer failed in 2016, about 170 suppliers had about $85 million of goods there on consignment, and more than 160 court proceedings followed [D19]. One supplier who had not filed won because the lender actually knew [D36]; another lost, its goods being 14% of stock [D20][D21]. Unfiled consignors lost in an earlier case [D22]. (counted, traced)
  - Art in New York: since 2012, trust property is not behind the dealer's creditors [D4]. Before that, one owner took about seven years to get a painting back [D23][D24]. (traced)
  - England: a selling agent in possession can pass good title to a buyer in good faith [D25]. Proceeds can be followed only if kept apart or held on trust [D26]; that consigned goods are not the failed seller's assets is *(background)*.
  - Switzerland: the agent sells in its own name for the owner's account [D27]. In the agent's bankruptcy the owner may claim the goods, subject to the agent's lien, and claims pass to the owner once the owner has met its own duties [D28]. (traced)
- Shops close and owners go unpaid: cases in 2026 and earlier, with no totals seen [D31][D35]. (traced)

**Failures and frauds on record.**

- A New York dealer admitted selling consigned works and keeping the money: 6 to 18 years, $120 million restitution ordered (2010) [D33]. (counted)
- An art dealer sentenced in 2022 to 84 months, with $86.7 million forfeited [D34]; that the scheme sold the same works more than once is *(background)*.

**Against the frame.** The frame had no line on consignment. The evidence adds one: the seller holds both the goods and the proceeds, and is the only witness to the sale. This is the kind where one party holds everything, and the frauds on record are the largest. (inferred)

### 2.4 To have something delivered

**Minimum content.** What is sent, to whom and where; who chooses and pays the carrier; and *the point where risk passes*. The trade rules exist to fix that point [E1][E2]. For consumer sales the law fixes it and the parties cannot move it against the consumer *(background for "cannot")*. (traced)

**Where risk passes.**

- Trade rules (2020 edition): under the carriage-paid rules, when the seller hands the goods to the carrier [E1]. Under the delivered rules, at the destination, ready for unloading or unloaded [E2]. Shipboard rules: on board the vessel *(background)*. (traced)
- In every rule the seller owes the "usual proof" of delivery and the buyer must accept it [E3]. Under one rule the buyer can tell the carrier to issue the seller an on-board document [E4]. (traced)
- Consumer sales in the European Union and the United Kingdom: when the consumer, or someone they named, takes possession. If the consumer hired their own carrier, on handover to that carrier [E6][E7]. (traced)
- United States sales law: on handover to the carrier in a shipment contract; at the destination in a destination contract; otherwise on receipt, if the seller is a merchant [E8]. (traced)
- Switzerland: at the making of the contract unless agreed otherwise [E9]; for goods to be sent, once set aside and handed over for dispatch [E10]. (traced)

**How assent is proven, and what makes it fail.** As in 2.0. The one thing peculiar to delivery: naming a safe place or a neighbour is itself an assent that moves the risk. A parcel left where the buyer said is the buyer's [E12]. (traced)

**Who proves performance, and with what.**

- Consumer sales: the seller. A "delivered" status alone is said to prove nothing [E11]. (traced)
- Parcels: a photo of the place or the person, with a map position [E13][E14][E15]. The highest service of one carrier cannot be left with a neighbour [E15]. (traced)
- Payment platforms: a carrier's record showing "delivered", a date and a matching address [E25]. A signature was required from $750 on two platforms [E27]; one platform's help pages say it stopped being mandatory from 26 January 2026, seen on regional pages only [E26]. (traced)
- One marketplace denies a claim where a signature matches the buyer's name [E28]; another lets a buyer open a case even when tracking says delivered [E29]. (traced)
- Road freight: taking delivery without written reservations is first evidence that the goods arrived as described. Hidden damage must be notified within seven days [E34]. (traced)
- United States freight: the sender shows good condition at origin, damage at destination and the amount; then the carrier must answer [E35]. (traced)

**Default when one side is silent.** Silence favours the sender once a carrier has marked delivery.

- Two days after the scan, the seller is paid [E31].
- An inspection period of 1 to 30 days; if the buyer does nothing, the seller is paid [E32].
- Fourteen days on a German classifieds service; sources differ on when the clock starts [E33].
- Freight: no reservation, no easy claim [E34]. (counted, traced)

**How it ends; what if one side disappears.** With receipt, or with the clock running out. A buyer who vanishes loses by silence. A seller who vanishes after sending a false tracking number may already have been paid; see frauds. (inferred)

**Failures and frauds on record.**

- United States postal audit, 2018: of 25.5 million delivery scans, about 1.9 million (7%) were made at the depot, not at the door; a third carried no location [E16]. (counted)
- **No source measures how often a delivery photo is false.** Single cases exist: a courier filmed taking a parcel back after the photo [E22]; a driver removed for a generated photo (2026) [E23]; a $2.59 million scheme of deliveries that never happened (2025) [E24]. (traced)
- United Kingdom: 37% of people had a problem with their latest parcel; a quarter of those problems were parcels left somewhere insecure [E17]. (counted; year to check)
- Parcel theft in the United States: surveys by vendors give 104 million parcels in a year [E19], or $7.7 billion [E20], or $12.8 billion [E21]. They do not agree. (counted, weak sources)
- The same-postcode trick: a real tracking number for another address in the buyer's area shows "delivered", and the claim closes for the seller [E36]. No figure found. (traced)
- Buyers' false claims: a card network puts first-party misuse at about one dispute in five [E30]. (counted, industry source)

**Against the frame.** Broken as a general statement. Receipt by the receiver is the proof in consumer law and in freight. Elsewhere a third party's record, or that record plus silence, is taken as enough. The receiver's own act is asked for only as the value rises, and one large platform appears to have stopped asking. (traced, then inferred)

## 3. What a cMIP must state (this session's reading)

*Everything in this section is inferred. It is a reading offered to DubLaw, not a rule and not a proposal for one. Nothing here was checked against the MOR texts beyond what the session's brief said of them.*

### 3.1 What a signed act already gives, and what it does not

- An assent that names the Terms by hash answers the question courts press hardest: *which version did this person agree to?* [A14][A17] A stranger can check it from the text alone.
- It does not show that the text was put before the person [A9]. That is what the person saw, which is DubWeb's. **Seam: DubWeb.**
- It does not show who held the key. Courts ask the relying side to show the act was the person's [A24][A25][A26]. That is Identity's ground, not Agreements'.
- It proves agreement and never performance. "Buyer by assent" has this flaw for payment; the same flaw appears in all four kinds below, as delivery, as return of a deposit, as a sale, as staying in scope.

### 3.2 For each kind

**To be recorded or photographed.** A stranger could check from the text alone:

- who is recorded and who records; the session, by date or by the hash of the recording;
- each use allowed, listed one by one, so that an assent to one use is not an assent to another [B1][B34];
- whether the permission can be withdrawn, and what withdrawal reaches: no new uses; held copies; nothing already published [B1][B35][B28];
- whether money is owed for it. **Seam: DubPay.**
- that a later act of withdrawal is itself a signed act naming the same Terms. In MOR's words nothing is updated; a withdrawal is signed again.

*Only a witness can give:* that the person recorded is the person who signed [B15][B17]; that a minor's guardian is the guardian; that a use in the world stayed inside the list. *Not checkable by anyone:* that copies were destroyed.

**To let a room and hold a deposit.** From the text alone:

- the parties, the room, the rent and its dates, the notice period [C19][C21];
- the deposit: the amount, who holds it, and the rule for paying it out. **Seam: DubPay** for the holding itself;
- who must prove a deduction, and by when [C12];
- what silence means, with its clock, for each side [C14][C25];
- the hash of a check-in record, assented to by both, and later of a check-out record.

*Only a witness can give:* the state of the room at the start and at the end [C12][C13]; whether damage happened; a judgment between two accounts of it. Disputes are DubLaw's own other ground and are not developed here.

**To leave goods with someone to sell.** From the text alone:

- the list of items, each with its price and the lowest price the seller may take [D1][D2];
- the split, the period, and the day payment is due after a sale [D29];
- whose the goods are until sold, and whose the proceeds are when sold [D3][D5];
- what happens to an unsold item, and after how long an uncollected one changes hands [D14];
- the seller's signed receipt of the list.

*Only a witness can give:* that a sale happened, when, and at what price. Today that witness is the seller alone [D3]. A buyer's own signed act would be a second witness. *What no text can give:* priority against the seller's creditors, which outside law decides [D15][D25]. **Seam: DubPay**, if proceeds were ever held apart from the seller.

**To have something delivered.** From the text alone:

- what is sent, from whom, to whom;
- the point where risk passes, stated as an event: handed to a carrier, left at a named place, or received [E1][E6][E12];
- which proof counts for that event: the receiver's signed receipt, a carrier's record, or a code only the receiver holds;
- what silence means and for how long [E31][E32];
- whether the proof asked for rises with the value [E27].

*Only a witness can give:* the handover itself. A receiver's signed receipt is the receiver's own word and a stranger can check the signature. A carrier's scan or photo is a third party's word and can be false [E16][E22]. The condition of the goods is a witness's matter too [E34]. **Seam: DubPay** for money released on that proof. **Seam: DubWeb** for how the receiver is asked to confirm.

### 3.3 Three things that recur

- **A text can state who must prove.** In all four kinds practice puts it on the one who gains [B1][C12][D3][E11].
- **A text can state what silence means.** Every working system does [C14][C25][E31][D14]. Where it does not, the stronger party's silence wins.
- **A text cannot make a fact.** A receipt, a photo, an inspection, an account of a sale: each is somebody's word. A text can only name whose word counts and what may be said against it.

## 4. Questions

*Each is for Nobody, allegedly, to take or leave. None is answered here.*

1. **(core business)** Is an assent meant to say only "I agree to these Terms", with receipt, payment and delivery always being separate signed acts by whoever is in a position to witness them? The evidence treats agreement and performance as two proofs in every kind.
2. **(development layer)** For "Buyer by assent": should the text count a buyer by an act that proves payment or receipt, and name which, in place of the assent alone? Delivery practice suggests the receiver's receipt; payment practice suggests the payee's.
3. **(development layer)** Should a cMIP that depends on a fact in the world be required to name whose word establishes that fact, and what counts against it?
4. **(development layer)** Should silence be something a cMIP must state, for each party, with a time? Practice always has such a rule. Left unsaid, what answer does the core give?
5. **(development layer)** Withdrawal of consent to be recorded: is it one more signed act that a cMIP reads, and what does such a text say about copies already made and uses already published?
6. **(development layer)** Can an assent be to one listed use among several in the same Terms, or only to the Terms whole? Data-protection practice wants the first; contract practice accepts the second.
7. **(core business)** Is DubLaw meant to write texts for agreements where one side is under 18, or where a guardian signs? Release practice treats it as a separate case.
8. **(development layer, seam to DubPay)** For a deposit: is "who holds it and on what rule it is paid out" DubLaw's to state and DubPay's to carry out? Where is the line?
9. **(presentation, seam to DubWeb)** What must a person have been shown before an assent counts as theirs in DubLaw's eyes? Courts ask for notice; the core proves only the signature.
10. **(core business)** Consignment and deposits meet outside law that a text cannot change: creditors' priority, deposit statutes. Does DubLaw's constitution say plainly that its texts state what the parties agreed and never what a court will do?
