# DubLaw research L3 of 5: disputes settled without a state's court

*A record of research by a DubLaw session, 20 November 2026. It informs DubLaw's constitution and way of working. It decides nothing. Nothing here is a decision of Nobody, allegedly; his decisions are only in `docs/decisions.md`.*

**The wish** (Nobody, allegedly, 20 November 2026): "First, I want you to work on your own constitution and workflow. For that, you can run research sessions first to inform you. **The question.** How have disputes been settled without a state's court behind them: at what scale, with what failure modes, and what does a decision need to carry, and to rest on, to be followed?

## 1. In plain words

**The evidence breaks the frame in four places, so that comes first.**

1. **The part that holds: held money, a deadline, a default and a short menu.** Every system that reached millions of cases a year had all four (eBay and PayPal, card chargebacks) [P7] [C8]. The one that had none of them, the EU's platform, sent about 200 cases a year to a decider out of millions of visits, and was shut in 2025 [G1] [G3]. *Counted.*
2. **Break one: "a decider named before the dispute" is too strong.** What working systems fix beforehand is the way a decider is appointed, not the person. Commercial arbitration and domain-name panels name the person afterwards and run thousands of cases a year [A22] [G12]. The recorded bias sits where one side keeps choosing the forum [G16] [A30] [A33]. *Counted and traced.*
3. **Break two: systems did work with no money held, but never on nothing.** Diamonds, cotton and grain rest on a closed membership worth more than one award, the naming of defaulters, and a court able to confirm the award [O1] [O6] [A17]. They fail when one default pays more than staying in: after the 2011 cotton price swing, about 185 firms ignored awards [O11]. *Counted.*
4. **Break three: appeals and published precedent did not follow volume.** The largest systems publish nothing. Domain-name panels have had no appeal in 25 years, and British Columbia's tribunal removed a review route in 2022 as it grew [G8] [G13]. What grew with volume was automation, coded reasons and statistical penalties for those disputed too often [P7] [P13] [C7]. *Traced; the generalisation is inferred.*
5. **Crowd juries are not shown to be reliable or hard to capture.** Most answers are never contested, so the default does the work. In the contested minority, one study puts overturned outcomes at up to 12.4%, voting power is concentrated, and the operator has overridden the jury [B21] [B22] [B24]. *Counted; the verdict is inferred.*
6. **The decider in the big systems is rarely neutral.** It is the party holding the money: the platform, or the cardholder's own bank. *Traced.*
7. **Silence is treated in two opposite ways.** Platforms and card schemes let silence lose [P4] [P9]. Arbitration rules make the claimant still prove the case [A3]. The one forum that ran default-wins at volume for one industry ended in scandal [A33] [A34]. *Traced.*
8. **The best compliance on record came with no decider at all.** In a Maine small-claims study, 70.6% of mediated settlements were paid in full, against 33.8% of judgments [M9]. *Counted, one study, 1981.*

## 2. Case by case

### 2.1 eBay and PayPal

- **Who decides.** The two parties first, in a structured exchange. Then the platform's staff or software. The user agreement fixes this before any dispute [P1].
- **History.** A 1999 pilot; then an outside mediator, SquareTrade, where about half of respondents took part and 90% of those settled (2002, its own figures); eBay took the work in-house in 2003 [P5] [P6].
- **Question and outcomes.** Item not received, or not as described. Refund in full or part, return, or denial [P1].
- **Deadlines and silence.**
  - eBay: report within 30 days of delivery; the seller has 3 business days; then either side may ask eBay to step in [P1].
  - PayPal: 180 days to open; the buyer must escalate within 20 days or the case closes, so silence also cuts against the buyer [P2]. The seller generally has 10 days; a silent seller loses once the buyer escalates [P3] [P4].
- **Evidence.** The seller shows tracking or delivery [P1].
- **Review.** eBay: within 30 days of the decision [P1]. PayPal: within 10 days of closing [P4]. Both go back to the same body.
- **What makes it followed.** Money held: PayPal places the disputed payment on hold when a case opens, and keeps reserves [P3]. eBay recovers the refund from the seller [P1]. Sellers with a dispute ratio of 1.5% or more pay a higher dispute fee (third-party record of the agreement) [P13].
- **Published or cited.** Nothing found. *(background)*
- **Scale.** Over 60 million disputes a year, 90% resolved by software alone. The figure is the designer's own, from an undated slide deck, and dates from his time at eBay, 2003 to 2011 [P7] [P8]. A typical dispute was worth about $70 to $100 [P9]. No current figure found.
- **A designer's finding.** Users who filed a dispute became more active afterwards than those who did not; only those whose case ran long traded less. The extract was garbled; verify before use [P10].
- **Crowd jury tried.** eBay India's Community Court (2008 to about 2011): 21 jurors drawn at random, for feedback removal only, never money [P11]. Users recorded cases short of votes and serial filers [P12].
- **Abuse.** eBay keeps an abusive-buyer policy [P14].

### 2.2 Amazon's A-to-z Guarantee

- **Who decides.** Amazon, fixed beforehand by the seller agreement. Amazon calls its review an investigation [Z1].
- **Question and outcomes.** Non-delivery, not as described, or refund not given; grant or deny. *(background)*
- **Deadlines and silence.** The buyer first contacts the seller and waits 48 hours [Z2]. The seller's window is 72 hours in most third-party guides; no official figure found [Z3]. A silent seller has the claim granted and the amount debited (third-party sources) [Z4].
- **Review.** 30 days after the debit, on new information, to Amazon [Z1].
- **What makes it followed.** A debit from the seller's account [Z1], and reputation: a target of under 1% of orders with a defect, counting claims, chargebacks and bad feedback [Z5].
- **Published.** No. *(background)* **Volume.** Not found.
- **Bribery on record.** In a suit filed December 2023, Amazon named a refund-fraud group and seven former staff said to have taken bribes to approve over $500,000 of false refunds [Z6] [Z7].

### 2.3 Airbnb's Resolution Centre

- **Who decides.** The parties first; then Airbnb's support staff, under the Terms [R1].
- **Deadlines and silence.** A host asks for reimbursement within 14 days of checkout; if the guest refuses, part-pays or is silent, the host may escalate to Airbnb [R1]. Travel problems must be reported within 72 hours [R2].
- **Evidence.** Photos and receipts from the host [R1].
- **Review.** By contacting support. Beyond that the Terms carry an arbitration agreement for US claims [R3].
- **What makes it followed.** Partly money held: the host's payout is released about a day after check-in [R4]. After that, the Terms let Airbnb charge the guest's payment method [R3]. So its power reaches money not yet held.
- **Published.** No. *(background)* **Volume.** Not found.
- **Failure on record.** August 2025: a host claimed about £12,000 with photos said to be altered by software. Airbnb upheld the claim, then reversed it on appeal and apologised [R5].

### 2.4 Card chargebacks (Visa, Mastercard)

- **Who decides.** First the cardholder's own bank; then the merchant's bank and the merchant answer; last, the card network as arbitrator. All are fixed by scheme rules; the merchant never contracts with the decider. *(background)*
- **Question.** Bounded by reason codes. Visa has four families: fraud, authorisation, processing errors, consumer disputes [C1]. The code decides what evidence counts [C6].
- **Deadlines.** Cardholder: generally 120 days [C2]. Merchant: 30 days at network level, often 5 to 20 through the acquirer [C3] [C4].
- **Silence.** A silent merchant loses, and the funds are pulled back when the dispute is filed. *(background; no extract confirmed it)*
- **Burden.** On the merchant to rebut. Since April 2023 a merchant can shift liability back in one fraud category by showing two earlier undisputed purchases matching on device or address data [C6].
- **Review.** A paid higher tier: Visa's arbitration fee rose from $500 to $600 in April 2025 [C5].
- **What makes it followed.** Money pulled back through the banks; and monitoring programmes that fine merchants disputed too often (Visa: 220 basis points, falling to 150 from April 2026) [C7].
- **A state behind it, in part.** US and UK statutes give credit-card holders rights against the issuer [C12] [C13]. The chargeback itself is contract [C14].
- **Published.** No precedent. *(background)*
- **Scale.** 263 million chargebacks forecast for 2025, rising to 334 million by 2028 (Mastercard, 2026 edition) [C9]. Value $33.79 billion in 2025 [C8].
- **Failure modes.** Each chargeback costs a merchant about $128 [C8]. About 45% of merchants' chargeback volume is judged fraudulent, first-party fraud included [C8]. Merchant win rates are self-reported and range from 20% to 54% [C10] [C11].

### 2.5 Commercial arbitration

**The UNCITRAL Arbitration Rules**

- **Who decides.** A tribunal formed after the dispute. If the parties cannot agree who appoints, the Secretary-General of the Permanent Court of Arbitration names an appointing authority [A1].
- **Challenge.** For justifiable doubts about independence *(background)*; a contested challenge is decided by the appointing authority, sought within 30 days [A1] [A2].
- **Silence.** If the respondent files nothing, the case continues; silence is not an admission [A3].
- **Burden.** Each party proves the facts it relies on [A4].
- **Costs.** In principle the loser pays; the tribunal may apportion [A5].
- **Appeal.** None; the award is final. *(background)* **Interim measures.** Provided for. *(background)*
- **Expedited Rules (2021).** One arbitrator; defence within 15 days of the tribunal forming; hearings may be dropped; award within six months [A6] [A7].

**The Technical Notes on Online Dispute Resolution (2016)**

- Three stages: negotiation, facilitated settlement, and a final stage left open [A8] [A9].
- They are notes, not rules, because states split on whether a consumer can be bound, before any dispute, to arbitrate. A two-track draft failed; the final stage was left undescribed [A10] [A11].

**Expedited and documents-only procedures**

- **ICC expedited.** 865 cases from 2017 to 2024; of 461 final awards, 63% came on or near the six-month limit; late arbitrators had fees cut in 33 cases [A12]. 169 cases in 2025 [A13].
- **London maritime small claims.** Ceiling $100,000; documents only; fixed fee of £5,000; costs capped; appeal to court waived [A15] [A16]. London maritime arbitration as a whole: 2,015 references in 2025 [A14].
- **Grain trade (GAFTA).** Two tiers. Year to September 2025: 314 new cases, 170 first-tier awards, about 20% appealed, 71% of appeals upheld the first tier [A17]. Those who do not pay are named to the whole trade: 39 firms posted in 2025, 2 of whom then settled [A18].

**Why awards travel: the New York Convention (1958)**

- 172 states are parties [A19].
- Reported court decisions grant enforcement in almost 90% of cases; the sample is of reported cases only [A20].
- In a 2008 survey, 84% of counsel said losers complied without a court in over three quarters of their cases [A21].
- It needs an agreement in writing; an exchange of plain emails has been accepted [A23]. Courts may refuse for narrow listed reasons, and do not rehear the merits. *(background)*

**Choosing and challenging the decider**

- Challenges rarely succeed: ICC accepted 2 of 38 in 2025 [A13]; the LCIA rejected all 10 in 2024 [A25]. The LCIA publishes its challenge decisions [A24].
- The 2024 conflict-of-interest guidelines of the International Bar Association keep graded lists and say an arbitrator must decline where justifiable doubts exist [A26].
- Emergency arbitrators (ICC, since 2012): over 250 applications; 17 in 2024, of which 5 were granted in whole or part [A27].

**Repeat players**

- US employment cases: employees won 21.4% of 1,213 awards, and did worse where the same employer and arbitrator met again [A28] [A29].
- US consumer cases: firms that arbitrate often win more and pay less; a later paper puts much of this down to resources and counsel [A30].
- A counter-finding: a study of 301 consumer awards from 2007 [A31]. That it found a weaker effect is *(background)*.

**What makes it followed.** A court in 172 states, high voluntary compliance, and in closed trades the naming of defaulters. No money is held. *Inferred from the above.*

**Published.** ICC awards since 2019 may be published after two years unless a party objects [A32]. Trade awards are private. *(background)*

**Scale.** ICC 841 new cases in 2024, median dispute $5 million [A22]. SIAC 886 in 2025 [A36]. AAA 11,553 business cases in 2023 [A37].

**Capture and fraud on record.**

- The National Arbitration Forum: 94% of over 19,000 California cases went to the business, nearly all debt collection [A33]. Minnesota's Attorney General sued in July 2009 over hidden ties to that industry; the forum left consumer work that month [A34].
- A French award of €403 million (2008) was annulled in 2015 for an arbitrator's concealed links to a party [A38].
- An $11 billion award against Nigeria was set aside by the English High Court in 2023 for bribery that the tribunal had not detected [A39].
- A counter-tactic: thousands of individual claims filed at once, turning the firm's own fee rules against it [A35].

### 2.6 Private ordering

**The New York Diamond Dealers Club (Bernstein, 1992)**

- **Who decides.** A Floor Committee and a Board of Arbitrators, members elected for two-year terms, so named before any dispute [O2]. Arbitration is a condition of membership [O3].
- **Question.** Trade disputes between members only. The club may decline claims that are outside the trade, complicated or burdensome [O4].
- **Deadline.** An award unpaid after 10 working days may be reported to the membership [O1].
- **Evidence and reasons.** Arbitrators need not find facts and write no reasons [O1].
- **Appeal.** On written request and a $100 fee [O1]. Courts review only procedure [O4].
- **What makes it followed.** No money held. Paid awards stay secret; unpaid ones are published to members [O1]. The sanction bites only on someone who wants to go on trading diamonds [O2].
- **Published.** No, on purpose [O1].
- **Scale.** About 2,000 members and about 150 arbitrations a year, around 1990 [O2].
- **Failure and capture.** Membership fell to about 1,200; trust in the arbitration eroded; a club president was accused of rigging decisions (extract cut short; verify) [O5].

**Cotton (Bernstein, 2001)**

- **Who decides.** Standing trade tribunals: a joint Board of Appeals for merchant and mill disputes, the Memphis Cotton Exchange for merchants, and Liverpool rules (now the International Cotton Association) for international sales [O6].
- **Question.** Contract disputes under detailed written trade rules, applied strictly: 18 opinions from 1978 to 1990 never mention good faith [O7].
- **Appeal.** Memphis allows one only when the first panel was split [O6]. Liverpool had appeal tiers and barred lawyers from hearings [O8].
- **What makes it followed.** Membership and expulsion [O6] [O9]. Courts matter more for international awards [O10].
- **Published.** Yes: written opinions, cited later [O6]. At low volume: 28 cases in 21 years at the Board of Appeals [O7].
- **Failure, with figures.** After the price reached $2.27 a pound in March 2011 and fell, contracts worth nearly $1 billion were in default or at risk [O12]. Arbitration applications reached 242 in 2011 and 247 in 2012 [O13]. About 185 firms from almost 50 countries ignored rulings over two years; the list of unpaid awards passed 500 names [O11]. The association then barred members from trading with listed firms [O14].
- **Not found.** Bernstein's own compliance count.

**The law merchant and the Champagne fairs**

- **The claim (Milgrom, North and Weingast, 1990).** Private judges kept records of defaulters, and merchants avoided those named; reputation alone carried judgments [O15].
- **The reply (Edwards and Ogilvie, 2012).** Enforcement at the fairs was by public authorities. A public fair court sat throughout; its wardens were the ruler's officials; no private judges are recorded. An appeal in 1310 went to a higher public court [O16] [O17].
- **On the law merchant itself.** Kadens (2012) and Sachs (2006) find local custom and statute, not a merchant-made law that travelled [O18] [O19].
- **Reading.** Among historians the private-judge account is not supported. *Inferred from the critics' abstracts only.*

**The Maghribi traders**

- **The claim (Greif, 1989 and 1993).** A coalition in which shared information and joint punishment made reputation binding, without legal contracts [O20].
- **The reply (Edwards and Ogilvie, 2012).** No clear case of group punishment; the traders also used legal means [O21]. Greif answers that each of their claims is wrong [O22].
- **A count (Goldberg, 2012).** About 900 documents; legal action appears in about 5% of the letters (via a secondary source) [O23].
- **Reading.** Unresolved. The middle position: reputation for daily dealing, law for serious disputes. *Inferred.*

**Two shorter cases**

- **Neighbours in Shasta County (Ellickson, 1991).** The legal rule on straying cattle did not change behaviour; norms and gossip did. The thesis is limited to close-knit groups [O24].
- **A darknet market.** The operators held buyers' coins in escrow, then left with about $12 million in March 2015 [O25]. Holding the money does not help if the holder is the one not to be trusted. *Inferred.*

### 2.7 Blockchain courts

**Kleros**

- **Who decides.** Jurors drawn at random, weighted by tokens staked, after the dispute is created [B1]. The court, not the jurors, is fixed beforehand by the contract. *(background)*
- **Question.** One of a preset list of rulings. *(background)* The contract holds the funds and carries out the ruling. *(background)*
- **Deadlines and silence.** Evidence, vote and appeal periods, set per court [B2]. A juror who misses a step is penalised [B3]. If only one side funds an appeal, that side wins without a vote [B4].
- **Appeal.** Each round has twice the jurors plus one, so fees grow fast; the loser of the last round pays more [B4] [B5].
- **Design.** Jurors are paid for voting with the majority, a Schelling-point scheme. A 2015 analysis shows a credible bribe could capture such a vote at no cost [B6]. The answer offered is appeal, and in the last resort a split of the token; a public thread argues a majority holder would win the split too [B7].
- **What makes it followed.** Money held by the contract. Outside the chain, one known case: a Mexican court in May 2021 enforced an arbitrator's award that had incorporated a three-juror decision [B8]. The court enforced the arbitrator, not the jury. *Inferred.*
- **Published.** Rulings are public; there is no binding precedent and no reasons. *(background)*
- **Scale.** Sources conflict: over a thousand cases (the project's own FAQ, undated); 1,200 (July 2026); 1,500 since 2018 (September 2026) [B9] [B10] [B11]. In 2019, 54 of 300 disputes had been appealed [B12].
- **Concentration.** 9,024 token holders, the largest with 11.6% (a 2023 paper) [B13]. The project reports about 90% agreement among jurors in two courts [B14]; herding would produce the same figure. *Inferred.*
- **Contested rulings.** Case 302 (a market on reported deaths, July 2020), decided amid contradictory data [B15]. A 2022 split in a community that relied on the court, with one side accusing the other of suppressing dissent [B16].
- **Proven bribery.** None found.
- **Now.** Version 2 has run in beta since November 2024, with about 100 cases by September 2025; version 1 was still live in September 2026 [B17] [B18].

**Aragon Court**

- **Design.** Like Kleros: staked jurors, at most five rounds [B19].
- **Life.** Live in February 2020 with no real dispute; early cases were mock disputes, the first voided [B20] [B26].
- **Disputes heard.** Not found.
- **End.** Its sponsor announced its own dissolution on 2 November 2023 after a conflict with investors [B27]. The court was shut down on 1 December 2024 [B28].
- **Reading.** It ended from disuse and its sponsor's collapse, not from a bad ruling. *Inferred.*

**UMA's optimistic oracle**

- **Who decides.** Nobody, if no one objects. Otherwise holders of the token who vote: a standing body, self-selected by buying in. *(background)*
- **Deadlines and default.** A proposer posts an answer with a bond; unchallenged, it stands after about two hours; disputed, it takes four to six days [B29]. Votes are cast sealed, then revealed, over 48 hours [B30].
- **Appeal.** None. *(background)*
- **What makes it followed.** The contract pays on the oracle's answer.
- **Scale.** In 2024, 11,093 markets settled for one large user, 217 disputed, about 2% [B31]. Over 1,150 disputed markets in the first five months of 2026 [B32].
- **Accuracy.** Of 233 disputed markets, the vote overturned the audited winner in 12.4%, given as an upper bound [B21].
- **Concentration and conflict (press reports, May and June 2026).** At least 60% of active voters could be linked to betting accounts; about one dispute in five had a voter with a stake; the top ten wallets cast over half the votes in most disputes. The developer says it has seen no credible evidence of manipulation [B22] [B23].
- **Contested resolutions, dated.**
  - June 2024, a market on a token's backers: the vote said No; the operator called it wrong and refunded [B24].
  - March 2025, a market on a minerals deal, about $7 million: resolved Yes with no deal signed; one holder reportedly cast about 25% of the votes. Alleged attack; no refunds [B33].
  - July 2025, a market on what a head of state wore, about $237 million traded: first Yes, then No; collusion alleged and denied [B34].
  - June 2026, a market on a firm's bitcoin sale: No upheld by 98.6% of voting power after the operator issued a late clarification [B35] [B32].
- **Response.** From August 2025 only listed proposers, 37 at first, may post answers [B25]. Price markets moved to another oracle in September 2025 [B36].

**Reality.eth**

- **Who decides.** By default the last person to post an answer with a bond. Otherwise an arbitrator named when the question is created [B37].
- **Deadline and default.** Each new answer needs at least double the bond and restarts the clock [B38]. When the clock runs out, the last answer stands. *(background)*
- **Review.** Anyone may pay the named arbitrator's fee to call it in [B37].
- **Scale.** Not found.

**Reading of the four.** Held money makes rulings carry themselves out. The crowd is the weak part. *Inferred.*

### 2.8 Mediation

- **Who decides.** The parties. The mediator is chosen after the dispute. There is no default: a no-show means no outcome. *(background)*
- **Settlement rates.**
  - UK: about 21,000 commercial mediations in 2023-24; 70% settle on the day, 87% in all. The figures are mediators' own reports [M1].
  - Singapore Mediation Centre, 2017 to 2019: 65% to 69% [M4].
  - US employment regulator, 2024: 71.2% of mediated charges resolved, but that is under 10% of charges filed [M3].
  - Italy, where a first session is required: about 162,000 mediations in 2024; 59% went past the first meeting and 54% of those agreed [M6].
- **Use.** A 2014 European Parliament study found mediation in under 1% of cases across the EU [M5].
- **Compliance.** Maine small claims: 70.6% of mediated settlements paid in full, against 33.8% of judgments [M9]. The follow-up study found defendants nearly twice as likely to comply [M10].
- **When it fails.** One side will not come [G1] [M3]. The wrong people in the room, poor preparation [M7]. Power imbalance and cost above the stake: *(background)*.
- **Enforcement.** The Singapore Convention (2019) had 23 parties by August 2026 [M8].
- **Published.** No. *(background)*

### 2.9 Public online dispute resolution

**The EU's ODR platform (2016 to 2025)**

- **Who decides.** Nobody on the platform. It passed a complaint to an outside body only if the trader agreed. *(background)*
- **Deadline and default.** The trader had 30 days to agree. Silence closed the case, so the default favoured the silent trader [G1].
- **Figures.** In 2020, 89% of complaints closed automatically and about 1% reached a deciding body [G1]. In later years, two to three million visits a year and about 200 cases reaching a body [G3].
- **A side effect.** In the first year, 40% of consumers whose case closed said the trader had then contacted them directly (secondary source) [G2].
- **End.** Complaints stopped on 20 March 2025; the regulation was repealed on 20 July 2025. The stated reason was cost against performance [G3] [G4].
- **What made outcomes followed.** Nothing. *Inferred.*

**British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal**

- **Who decides.** A tribunal member appointed by the state, assigned after filing. The forum is fixed by statute. *(background)*
- **Question.** Strata disputes (2016), small claims to CAD 5,000 (2017), some vehicle claims (2019) [G5].
- **Stages.** Guided self-help, negotiation, facilitation, then a decision [G6].
- **Deadline and default.** 14 days to respond; if the respondent is silent the claimant may ask for a default decision, liability assumed [G7].
- **Review.** An objection route for small claims was abolished from July 2022; only judicial review remains [G8] [G9].
- **What makes it followed.** A court: a filed order has the force of a court order [G9].
- **Published.** Yes, in a public database [G10].
- **Scale (cumulative, undated).** 334,702 self-help sessions; 59,740 applications; of 50,332 closed, about 45% withdrawn or by consent, 23% default decisions, 24% final decisions (the percentages are this session's arithmetic) [G10].
- **Failure mode.** Delay: average time to resolve rose from 208 to 277 days in 2024-25 [G11].

**Domain-name disputes (the UDRP), a case added because it teaches most**

- **Who decides.** One or three panelists appointed by a provider after the complaint. The complainant picks the provider. The registrant agreed to the procedure when registering. *(background)* [G17]
- **Question.** A three-part test. Outcomes: transfer, cancel or deny. No money. *(background)*
- **Deadline and silence.** 20 days to respond. On silence the panel still decides, and the complainant keeps the burden [G18].
- **Review.** No appeal. The registrar acts after 10 business days unless the registrant shows a court filing [G13].
- **What makes it followed.** The registrar is bound by contract to move an asset it already controls. *(background)*
- **Published and cited.** All decisions are published; an overview by the largest provider gathers panel views and works as precedent [G14].
- **Scale.** 6,282 cases at the largest provider in 2025; over 80,000 in all [G12]. From 2015 to 2025, about 92% of 42,306 cases had no response [G15].
- **Bias on record.** In 2001, complainants won about 83% before one panelist and 60% before three; at one provider six panelists decided 53% of single-panel cases [G16]. A 2026 paper finds nearly a third of filings consistent with forum shopping [G15].

**The UK Financial Ombudsman, as a contrast**

- 305,726 new complaints in 2024-25; 34% upheld [G19]. Firms must join by regulation; a decision binds the firm if the consumer accepts. *(background)*

## 3. Against the arbiter draft (this session's reading)

*Everything in this section is inferred. It compares the findings above with "An arbiter's award", draft 2, as summarised in the assignment. This session did not read the draft's full text, so a gap named here may already be filled there.*

### 3.1 Where the draft already stands with the systems that scaled

- **Money held, a deadline, a default, a short menu of outcomes.** These are the four things every million-case system has (2.1, 2.4).
- **A split in millionths.** Finer than the yes-or-no of crowd courts and domain panels; it matches the partial refunds of the platforms.
- **Delivery receipts as evidence.** Tracking is the deciding evidence at eBay and PayPal [P1].
- **A named place.** The New York Convention ties an award to a place. *(background)*

### 3.2 What the successful systems have that the draft lacks

1. **A stage before the decider.** eBay, the BC tribunal and the UNCITRAL notes all put negotiation first [P1] [G6] [A8]. Most cases end there: about 45% at the BC tribunal [G10]. Settled outcomes are also the best complied with [M9]. The draft goes from claim to answer to award.
2. **A rule for the silent party.** The draft's default covers the arbiter's silence. The summary does not say what follows when the respondent does not answer. The systems split in two: silence loses (platforms, cards), or the claimant must still prove (arbitration, domain panels). The second is slower; the first is the pattern of the 2009 scandal when one side files in bulk [A33].
3. **A way back from a wrong award.** Platforms reopen on new information within 10 to 30 days [P1] [P4] [Z1]. Grain has a second tier used in about 20% of awards [A17]. Courts set aside awards won by forgery or bribery [A38] [A39]. The Airbnb case shows forged evidence caught only on review [R5]. In the draft a second award is a fork and a warning; there is no way to correct the first.
4. **Disclosure, challenge and recusal.** The draft's arbiter is accepted before the dispute, which answers most of this. It does not cover a conflict that arises later, such as the arbiter coming to trade with one party. Arbitration has graded disclosure lists and a third party who rules on a challenge [A26] [A1].
5. **A substitute.** Arbitration fixes a way of appointing, with a fallback when it fails [A1]. The draft names one person. If that person is gone, the default pays the payer, whoever was in the right.
6. **A clean right to decline.** The diamond club may refuse a burdensome claim [O4]; arbitrators must decline when in doubt [A26]. In the draft the arbiter's only way to decline is silence, and silence refunds the payer. An arbiter can therefore decide for the payer by doing nothing, and sign nothing.
7. **Fee shifting.** In arbitration the loser pays in principle [A5]; card networks charge the losing side for the top tier [C5]; maritime small claims cap costs [A15]. The summary gives the arbiter's fee but not who bears it by outcome.
8. **Coded reasons for a claim.** Card schemes and domain panels limit not only the outcomes but the grounds, and the ground fixes the evidence that counts [C1] [C6] [G14]. The draft limits outcomes; the summary does not show a list of grounds.
9. **A watch on repeat players.** Platforms and card schemes penalise parties disputed too often [P13] [Z5] [C7]. Research found bias where one side meets the same decider again and again [A28] [A29] [G16]. A seller who writes the same arbiter into every Terms is that pattern.
10. **Reach beyond the money held.** Arbitration can award damages and have a court collect them in 172 states [A19]. The draft's award cannot. This is a limit the draft chose, and it is also what frees it from needing a court.
11. **Small-claims tracks.** Fixed fee, documents only, no hearing [A15]. The draft already looks like one. What it lacks is the other track, for the dispute too large or tangled for one award on papers.
12. **Interim measures.** PayPal freezes the payment when a case opens [P3]. The summary does not say whether a claim stops other timed releases of the held money.

### 3.3 What the draft has that they lack

- **The decider's own signature before any dispute.** Fee, windows and consent are fixed in advance. Arbitration fixes only the way of appointing; platforms impose themselves.
- **A default for the decider's silence.** Almost no system has one. The nearest are a cut in late arbitrators' fees and a party's right to leave the expedited track [A12] [A6].
- **A decider who is not the money holder's own staff, as far as the summary shows.** In every million-case system the decider is the interested intermediary (finding 6). The darknet case shows the risk of a holder who answers to no one [O25].
- **Fork detection.** A second award is visible to anyone as a warning. Other systems need a registry or a court to say which award is the real one.
- **Time references in order.** Platforms have their own clock and need none. The crowd courts take their chain's clock. Arbitration argues about dates.
- **Citable awards from the start.** Cotton published at 28 cases in 21 years and was cited; diamonds at 150 a year chose secrecy [O7] [O1]. Published decisions are also what let outsiders count bias, as was done for domain panels [G16].
- **No operator who can override.** The oracle's operator refunded against one vote and steered another with a late clarification [B24] [B35]. In the draft nobody stands above the award. The other face of this is gap 3.

### 3.4 Which gaps a cMIP could fill, and which need a person

**A cMIP could carry, because it is a published text that returns an answer from the record:**

- a list of claim grounds, each with the evidence that counts (gap 8);
- the rule for a silent party, either way, and its window (gap 2);
- the fee split by outcome (gap 7);
- substitutes named in order, as the time references are (gap 5);
- a decline, signed by the arbiter, that passes the claim to the next name without pointing at either party (gap 6);
- one review by a second named arbiter, within a window, on stated grounds, with the first award marked as superseded and not as a fork (gap 3);
- a negotiation step: a split proposed by one party and assented to by the other, before or instead of an award (gap 1);
- a hold on timed releases while a claim is open (gap 12);
- counts from published awards: how often this arbiter has sat for this party, and with what results (gap 9).

**A person is needed:**

- to judge whether evidence is true: the altered photos, the bribed staff [R5] [Z6];
- to say what unclear Terms mean. The oracle's worst disputes were about the wording of the question, not the facts [B34] [B35];
- to decide whether a doubt about the arbiter is justified, and to step aside (gap 4);
- to mediate;
- to decide whether to accept Terms that arrive with the other side's arbiter already written in;
- to answer the questions of state law: whether a consumer can be bound before a dispute, which is the point UNCITRAL's states could not agree on [A10]; and whether a court would treat an award as an arbitral award, as the Mexican court did with an arbitrator's award, not the jury's [B8].

## 4. Questions

Nothing here is decided. Each question is tagged.

1. When the respondent does not answer within the answer window, does the claim succeed, or must the arbiter still decide on the claimant's evidence? *Development layer.*
2. Should an award be correctable at all: one review, by whom, on what grounds, within what window? Or is finality the point, as in mainstream arbitration? *Development layer; it touches core business if a superseded award must be told apart from a fork.*
3. Is the refund to the payer the right default for an arbiter's silence, given that it lets an arbiter rule for the payer by doing nothing? Would named substitutes come first? *Development layer.*
4. May an arbiter decline in the open, and what follows? *Development layer.*
5. Who bears the arbiter's fee by outcome? *Development layer.*
6. Does DubLaw want a negotiation step before the award, given that settled outcomes are the best followed? *Development layer.*
7. Should claims carry a ground from a short list, with the evidence each ground needs? *Development layer.*
8. Are awards public to all, to the parties, or to those who later deal with the arbiter? The evidence pulls both ways: secrecy in diamonds, publication in cotton and domain names. *Core business for what the record shows; presentation for how it is shown.*
9. How is a reader of Terms shown that the arbiter has sat for the other party many times before? *Presentation, drawing on a development-layer count.*
10. Does DubLaw take a position on Terms between a business and a consumer that name the business's arbiter in advance? Many states do not let such a clause bind the consumer. This sits with R4, which this session could not read. *Core business for consent; a person's call on the law.*
11. Is the draft meant to stay inside money held for good, or should a later text describe how an award could be carried to a court for more? *Core business.*
12. While a claim is open, do other timed releases of the held money stop? *Development layer.*
13. The frame said precedent comes with volume. The evidence says it does not come on its own. Does DubLaw want cited awards to weigh anything, or only to inform? *Development layer; presentation.*
