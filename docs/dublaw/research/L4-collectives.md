## 1. In plain words

1. **The frame breaks in three places.** The evidence says so first, as asked.
   - Hard-to-change rules did not last longer. Rules nobody could change at all died.
   - Cheap exit at a known price emptied groups whose membership could be bought for less than the exit paid.
   - Fixing the division early was not enough. The money still stopped where one side measured the income, or where a contributor had no address.
2. **What survives is a layered version.** Everyday rules are easy to change by the people who use them. The rule about changing rules is harder. Nothing is impossible.
3. **Silence is never left as a permanent "no" by any legal system studied.** Each one has a way out. The mildest is to stop counting the silent person after notice and a wait.
4. **Silence is allowed to mean "yes" only for small matters, or with a watcher.** Money terms are carved out. Where that carve-out eroded, abuse followed.
5. **Orphaned money goes to the biggest known holders unless a rule says otherwise.** In United States music that is the statute. About $161 million of the 2021 pool is still unpaid after five years.
6. **Every working "silence" design has the same five parts:** a notice, a clock, a veto, a carve-out, and a place for the share to wait.
7. **Rights to leave go unused unless someone builds the road.** 13% of Europeans said they had moved their data. Under 10% of those who know about legacy tools set one.
8. **The largest recorded blockage came from people who said "no" loudly, not from silence.** Argentina's holdouts were 7% and active.

### 2.1 Commons (Ostrom)

- **Changing a rule.** Most people affected by the working rules can take part in changing them (principle 3) [excerpt][C1]. No number is given (inferred). Ostrom names three levels: working rules, rules for making working rules, and rules for making those [excerpt][C2] (traced). That deeper levels are costlier to change is (background).
- **Silence.** Not addressed (inferred).
- **Division.** Benefits in proportion to contributions (principle 2). A study of 69 cases found this mattered in every resource type [excerpt][C4] (counted).
- **Leaving.** Handled by boundaries: who is in. No exit price is a design principle (inferred).
- **Disappearance or death.** Not addressed. Rights usually attach to a household or land (background).
- **The record.** A review of 91 studies found the principles broadly supported, and read them as likelihoods, not guarantees [excerpt][C5] (counted). Ostrom found no fixed set of rules that lasts, only shared principles [excerpt][C3] (traced).

### 2.2 Cooperatives

**The International Co-operative Alliance's principles.** Open and voluntary membership; one member, one vote; capital partly common with a limited return (background; the Alliance's site was unreachable). Its own Statement changes by half plus one of votes cast [excerpt][C7] (traced).

**Exit and the return of capital.**

- Accounting treats a member share that the member can demand back as a debt, unless the co-op may refuse [excerpt][C8] (traced). So a co-op that wants stable capital makes exit discretionary (inferred).
- United States farm co-ops: of 792 surveyed, the most used method (44%) repays old capital in rotation; the average wait was 16 years in 1991 and at least 18 later for grain and supply co-ops [excerpt][C9][C10] (counted).
- Price of leaving: face value, no growth, paid after many years or at death, at the board's discretion (inferred).

**Mondragon.**

- **Changing a rule.** Each co-op's assembly, one member one vote (background). A single co-op's assembly can leave the group by its own vote [excerpt][C14] (traced).
- **Silence.** Not found.
- **Division.** Older accounts: up to 70% of profit to members' capital accounts, at least 20% to reserves, at least 10% to community purposes [excerpt][C12] (traced). Fixed before the year's result.
- **Leaving.** Capital stays until the member leaves or retires [excerpt][C12]. Basque law: repaid within five years at most; deductions capped at 20% for unjustified withdrawal and 30% for expulsion [excerpt][C13] (traced). The price is known; payment is slow.
- **Disappearance or death.** Heirs are repaid within one year. A leaver who does not claim within five years loses the sum to the reserve [excerpt][C13] (traced).
- **The record.**
  - Fagor Electrodomésticos, 2013: debt reported between about €800 million and €1.1 billion; about 5,600 jobs, some 1,700 to 2,000 of them members; about €185 million of subordinated contributions sold to up to 10,000 savers [excerpt][C15][C16] (counted; sources disagree).
  - Members asked for about €20 million of voluntary contributions back from 2012 and say they were blocked, despite a one-month rule [excerpt][C17]. The known price failed when it was needed (inferred).
  - Ulma and Orona left the group on 16 December 2022, by 80.52% and about 70%. Mondragon lost about 15% of its industrial sales. No exit fee was found [excerpt][C14] (counted).

**Stocksy United** (a photographers' platform co-op). What held.

- **Changing a rule.** Members propose; 10% of artist members is quorum at the first stage; the board decides what goes to a formal vote [excerpt][C18] (traced). Silent members do not block (inferred).
- **Division.** The artist gets 50% to 75% of each licence. Surplus: 5% to founders, 5% to staff, 90% to artists by that year's royalties [excerpt][C19] (traced). Fixed in advance.
- **Leaving, death.** Not found.
- **The record.** Members voted in 2018 to lift the 1,000-member cap [excerpt][C20].

**Resonate** (a music streaming co-op). What failed. It ran from 2017 to 2024; its own site says it is dissolved, with former board members settling the finances [excerpt][C22] (traced). Why it closed was not found.

**Turning a mutual into a company.**

- Halifax, 1997: about 75% of roughly 7 million members voted, and 97% of those backed conversion, which paid them shares [excerpt][C23] (counted). Outsiders opened small accounts to get the payout [excerpt][C23].
- None of the converted societies stayed independent [excerpt][C24, a press piece].
- The Co-operative Bank, 2013: a £1.5 billion shortfall; control passed to bondholders [excerpt][C25] (counted).

### 2.3 DAOs

**Moloch (the "rage quit").**

- **Changing a rule.** The core settings are fixed when the contract is made; no function changes them [read][D1] (traced). To change the rules, members found a new group and move. Ordinary proposals pass by more yes than no.
- **Silence.** Counts as nothing. There is no quorum [read][D1][D2].
- **Division.** Each member's slice of the common fund, by share, worked out at the moment of leaving [read][D1].
- **Leaving.** A member burns shares and takes their slice of every asset. A member who voted yes on a pending proposal must wait until it is processed [read][D1]. So those who voted no, or did not vote, can leave before it takes effect. The price is book value, known in advance.
- **Disappearance.** Nothing reclaims a silent member's shares. A vote can strip the vote from them and pay the slice to the member's address, which may be a dead key [read][D2] (inferred for the last part).
- **The record.** A built-in bound fails a proposal if too many leave after the yes votes [read][D1]. No counted history of rage quits was found.

**The DAO, 2016.**

- **Silence.** Quorum was a fifth of all tokens, so tokens that never voted weighed against every proposal. The code had a function to halve the quorum, because the designers expected this [read][D4] (traced; the motive is inferred).
- **Leaving.** A holder could split off with their share after a wait [read][D4].
- **The record.** The exit function was the hole: it paid before updating balances, and roughly 3.6 million ether left [excerpt][D5]. Ethereum changed its own history to undo it [excerpt][D5].

**Nouns, 2023.**

- **Leaving.** When 20% of tokens asked, a fork took their share of the treasury to a new group with a simple quit [excerpt][D6].
- **The record.** 472 of 846 tokens (about 56%) left with about 16,757 ether (about $27 million). The tokens had traded below their share of the treasury [excerpt][D7][D8] (counted). The fork's own treasury fell from about 16,750 to 7,700 ether in three days [excerpt][D8]. The designers then proposed replacing forks [excerpt][D6].

**Rook and Aragon, 2023.** Neither had a written exit. Buyers bought tokens below treasury value to force one.

- Rook: a treasury of about $50 million, well above the market price; more than half was liquidated for holders [excerpt][D9].
- Aragon: the association called the buying an attack and cancelled a promised hand-over of about $200 million to token holders. It then dissolved. 86,343 ether (about $155 million) was redeemable until 2 November 2024; tokens not redeemed by then got nothing [excerpt][D10][D11] (counted).

**Compound.**

- **Changing a rule.** 400,000 yes votes are quorum; votes are counted as held when the proposal starts, so tokens bought later do not count; settings change by the same vote, within fixed bounds [read][D12] (traced).
- **Silence.** Blocks: only yes votes count to quorum [read][D12].
- **The record.** Proposal 289 (July 2024) passed 682,191 to 633,636 and sent about $24 million of tokens to a vault run by its proposers. It was undone by negotiation, not by a rule [excerpt][D13] (counted).

**Attacks.**

| Case | What happened | The missing rule |
|---|---|---|
| Beanstalk, April 2022 | Borrowed funds bought about 67% of the votes and the change ran in the same transaction. About $182 million drained; the attacker kept about $77 million [excerpt][D14][D15]. | No wait between gaining votes and using them. |
| Build Finance, February 2022 | A proposal passed unnoticed and gave control of the token and treasury; about $470,000 taken [excerpt][D16]. | Low threshold, no notice that reached anyone. |
| Tornado Cash, May 2023 | The code of an approved proposal was swapped after approval, giving 1.2 million false votes against about 700,000 real ones [excerpt][D17]. | Approval named an address, not a fixed text (inferred). |
| Mango, October 2022 | The exploiter voted with about 33 million taken tokens on his own settlement, keeping $47 million [excerpt][D18]. | Nothing separated taken weight from held weight. |

**Turnout and concentration.**

- In 17 of 21 groups studied, fewer than 10 participants held a deciding majority [excerpt][D19] (counted).
- Across ten groups, under 1% of holders held 90% of voting power [excerpt][D21] (counted).
- One 2025 paper gives average participation of 6.3% [excerpt][D23] (counted; one source).

**Quorum failure and silence as approval.**

- Uniswap's first proposal, to lower its own quorum, had over 95% support and failed for want of votes [excerpt][D24].
- Arbitrum cut its constitutional quorum from 5% to 4.5% in 2025 because it was becoming unreachable; delegates noted that a cheaper quorum makes an attack cheaper too [excerpt][D25].
- Lido built a track where routine motions pass unless enough holders object, because ordinary votes on routine matters failed for lack of attention [read][D26].
- Lido's design also lets 1% of stakers delay a decision and 10% trigger an exit with a long wait [read][D27; values marked as proposed].
- About 30,000 addresses never claimed their Uniswap tokens; at Safe almost two-thirds of the tokens went unclaimed [excerpt][D31].

### 2.4 Silent members and decisions

**Company written resolutions.**

- England: a written resolution needs a majority (or 75%) of *all* members entitled to vote, so silence is a "no". It lapses after 28 days. Removing a director or auditor cannot be done this way [excerpt][S1][S2] (traced).
- Delaware: written consent from holders of the votes that would carry it at a full meeting; a 60-day window; prompt notice to those who did not consent [excerpt][S3][S4].
- Informal agreement by everyone: all must agree, knowing what they agree to. Silence alone is not agreement [excerpt][S5].

**"Deemed consent" after notice.**

| Where | Silence means | Safeguards |
|---|---|---|
| English insolvency (since 2017) | Yes | Notice; 10% by value objecting stops it and forces the full procedure; cannot be used for the practitioner's pay; creditors can demand a meeting [excerpt][S7][S8] |
| English company documents by website | Yes | Asked one by one; 28 days; not asked again within 12 months; about the channel only [excerpt][S10] |
| Syndicated loans ("snooze you lose") | Left out of the count | Usually 10 working days; the original form carved out the money terms [excerpt][S11] |
| Opt-out class actions | Included and bound | Best possible notice; right to opt out; court approval [excerpt][S21] |
| Insurance business transfers | Bound | Notice; independent expert; regulator; court [excerpt][S45] |

- **Abuse on record.** Larger loan deals now apply "snooze you lose" to everything, and outlier drafting binds a lender to changes it never approved [excerpt][S11][S12] (traced).
- **Pattern.** Silence means "yes" only for small or procedural matters, or with a court or expert watching. The mildest fix is to leave the silent out of the count (inferred).

**Supermajorities.**

- Sovereign bonds: 75% thresholds, counted on all bonds outstanding, so silence is a "no" [excerpt][S14][S16].
- Argentina: 93% accepted; the rest held out and blocked market access until a settlement of about $4.65 billion in 2016 [excerpt][S17][S18] (counted).
- Greece, 2012: the majority-binding clause was added to existing bonds by statute, after the fact. Take-up rose from 83.5% to 95.7% [excerpt][S19][S20] (counted).
- Poland's one-member veto: of about 150 parliaments, 53 passed nothing [excerpt][S29, a secondary source] (counted).
- The first United States constitution needed all thirteen states to amend, and was never amended [excerpt][S30].
- National constitutions: only half last more than nineteen years; flexibility is among the features tied to lasting [excerpt][S31]. A follow-up found the written difficulty of amendment predicts little once habit is counted [excerpt][S32].
- Companies: a bundle of entrenching terms, supermajorities among them, goes with lower firm value [excerpt][S33].

**A partner who stops answering.**

- England (the 1890 Act's defaults): everyday matters by majority; changing the business or admitting a partner needs everyone; no expulsion unless agreed in advance; one partner's death or bankruptcy dissolves the firm for all, unless agreed otherwise [excerpt][S34][S35] (traced).
- If the others carry on without settling, the leaver or the estate chooses between the profits their share earned and 5% a year [excerpt][S36].
- United States (the revised uniform Act): leaving or dying no longer ends the firm. The firm must buy the share at the greater of break-up or going-concern value, with interest. If nothing is agreed in 120 days, it must pay its own estimate in cash, and the leaver may go to court [excerpt][S37][S38][S39] (traced).
- Limited companies of members: on death the estate keeps the money rights and loses the vote [excerpt][S40].
- Deadlock: a court may dissolve, but only for a real deadlock and after any agreed exit has been tried [excerpt][S41][S42].

**Lost shareholders.** English listed companies forfeit dividends unclaimed for 12 years; £2.1 billion of dormant assets of all kinds has gone to a national fund [excerpt][S25][S26]. United States states hold unclaimed property for the owner without limit: about $70 billion held, $4.49 billion returned in one year [excerpt][P41][S28] (counted).

### 2.5 Dividing income among contributors

**Music split sheets.**

- **Division.** A sheet records each writer's percentage. Guidance says to sign at the first session, because a collaborator can refuse later [excerpt][M11]. No survey of how often sheets go unsigned was found.
- **Silence.** United States: with no agreement, co-authors hold equal shares, whatever each contributed [excerpt][M13]. England: equal shares presumed, and all must consent to license [excerpt][M14].
- **The record.**
  - An organist won 40% of a 1967 song after 38 years of saying nothing [excerpt][M15].
  - Band members who claimed a spoken agreement to share lost [excerpt][M16].
  - Where claims on a work add up to more than 100%, the United States licensing collective holds *all* the money until the claimants or a court settle it [excerpt][M8] (traced).

**Collecting societies.**

- **Changing a rule.** In the European Union the members' assembly sets the distribution policy [excerpt][M9].
- **Silence.** Money that cannot be distributed after three years becomes "non-distributable"; the assembly decides its use [excerpt][M9]. The British society shares unidentified money among identified members in proportion [excerpt][M18]. So unregistered means: paid to the registered (inferred).
- **Leaving.** In the European Union, on at most six months' notice [excerpt][M9; article number to check]. In the United States, courts held large publishers could not withdraw only their digital rights [excerpt][M20].
- **Death.** A successor is admitted at the society's discretion (Britain) or on documents and a fee (one United States society) [excerpt][M18][M22].
- **The record.**
  - Spain: broadcasters played music they published themselves late at night and shared the royalties. One estimate puts the take at €400 to 600 million. The world federation expelled the society in 2019 [excerpt][M23]. The split was untouched; the usage record was captured (inferred).
  - Germany: publishers received a flat share of about €30 million a year until one author's lawsuit ended it in 2016 [excerpt][M24].

**The "black box": the United States Mechanical Licensing Collective.**

| Item | Figure | As of | Source |

| The same pool after rates were finally set | about $397 million | 2024 to 2026 | [excerpt][M2][M3] |

- **The rule for the orphan money.** Held at least three years, then paid to the *identified* owners by market share; owners must pass at least half to their writers [excerpt][M7][M27] (traced). The statute's own text was not read.
- **The brake.** The Copyright Office told the collective to "err on the side of holding unclaimed royalties longer" [excerpt][M10]. The first market-share payout is set for April 2027, starting with January 2021, where under $7 million remains [excerpt][M4][M5].
- **Who was not paid.** Independent writers who administer their own songs and are not registered [excerpt][M30]. Writers on works with conflicting claims [excerpt][M8]. Foreign-language writers and missing identifiers: (background), not evidenced.
- **Other pots.** One actors' union held about $76 million in residuals for performers or heirs it could not find [excerpt][M39] (counted).

**Film profit shares.**

- **Division.** The percentage is fixed by contract before filming. The amount it applies to is worked out afterwards by the studio that pays (background for the standard definitions).
- **Silence.** A typical term makes a statement final unless challenged in detail within 24 months [excerpt][M32].
- **The record.**
  - A film that took over $288 million showed no net profit; a court found the formula unconscionable [excerpt][M33].
  - A film with $939 million in receipts showed a $167 million loss [excerpt][M34].
  - A television dispute settled for a reported $200 million; the issue was the fee a network paid its own studio arm [excerpt][M36].
  - An arbitrator awarded $179 million in another, later cut to about $51 million [excerpt][M37].
- **The contrast.** Guild residuals: the formula is set by collective agreement and paid through the union (background). What fails there is finding people.

**Where the shower's water stops** (inferred from the above):

1. At the writer with no registered address. The money arrives and has nowhere to go.
2. At the unsettled split. One over-claim freezes the whole work.
3. At the publisher, who may lawfully keep half of redistributed orphan money.
4. At whoever measures the income.
5. At the missing person or heir.

### 2.6 Death, disappearance and succession

**Law on digital assets.**

- United States uniform law (2015): what the user set in the platform's own tool comes first; then a will; then the platform's terms [excerpt][P1][P4] (traced). So a silent user leaves the decision to the platform.
- The 2014 version gave heirs broad access and failed in the legislatures; the reversal took a year [excerpt][P2]. Adopted in 46 states [excerpt][P5, a secondary source].
- Germany's top court (2018) set the opposite default: an account passes to heirs [excerpt][P8].

**Platform tools.**

| Tool | What silence counts as | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Google's inactive account manager | 3 to 18 months of no activity, after a warning | Up to 10 contacts get the data, or it is deleted [excerpt][P10] |
| Google's general policy | 2 years of no use, after notices | The account may be deleted [excerpt][P11] |
| Apple's legacy contact | No clock; a key and a death certificate | Access for 3 years; purchases and saved passwords excluded [excerpt][P15] |
| A password manager's emergency access | The owner not answering within a set wait | Access granted; the owner can refuse before then [excerpt][P18] |

- **Use.** A British survey: 28% knew of legacy tools; under 10% of those had set one [excerpt][P14]. About 24% of United States adults have a will [excerpt][P12]. 23% of crypto holders had a written plan [excerpt][P35] (counted).

**Bitcoin.**

- Lost for good: estimates run from about 1.6 million to 3.8 million coins [excerpt][P31][P32] (counted; methods differ).
- A Canadian exchange whose founder died: the regulator's staff found fraud, with clients losing at least C$169 million. A death revealed it; lost keys did not cause it [excerpt][P33].
- Inheritance designs, by name only: a recovery key that becomes valid after a coin has not moved for a set time, the clock restarting when the owner moves it [excerpt][P36]; services where an heir asks, a six-month wait runs, and the owner may refuse [excerpt][P37]; guardians who can replace a lost key after a delay [excerpt][P38].
- With no plan, the share is lost to everyone (inferred).

**Missing people in general law.** England: presumed dead after 7 years; a guardian for property after 90 days [excerpt][P39][P40]. The general answer to an orphaned share is a custodian who holds it and an owner who can still claim (traced).

### 2.7 Carrying an agreement elsewhere

**Assignment and novation.**

- Assignment moves the benefit only. Replacing a party (novation) needs every party's consent [excerpt][S43] (traced).
- A term banning assignment works [excerpt][S43]; England voided such bans for small firms' receivables in 2018 [excerpt][S44].
- Employment contracts move automatically when a business is sold; a worker who objects does not move, and usually gets nothing [excerpt][S47].

**Data portability.**

- 13% of Europeans said they had used the right to move their data (2019) [excerpt][P22] (counted).
- Of 135 services that produced an export, 20 produced a complete one; most offer no import [excerpt][P26][P27] (counted).
- Where it is used, someone built a standard and paid for it: British open banking had 16.5 million connections by December 2025; about 1 million current accounts switch a year [excerpt][P29][P30] (counted).
- European cloud law caps switching charges until January 2027 and then bans them [excerpt][P44]. This is the clearest case of law fixing the exit price in advance.
- Moving between social servers: followers move, posts stay [excerpt][P47]. About 99.7% of active accounts on one portable network sat on the company's own servers [excerpt][P49; one personal analysis].

### 2.8 Theory: exit, voice and loyalty, as tested

- A review of the tests calls the results somewhat disappointing and says each of the three ideas must be split into kinds first [excerpt][C26; abstract only].
- In workplace studies, better alternatives raised both leaving and speaking up; so did having more invested [excerpt][C27].
- Unionised workers quit less; one later study finds the effect gone once pay is counted [excerpt][C28].
- East Germany, 1989: mass leaving set off protest instead of draining it [excerpt][C30].
- Phone number portability lowered prices where moving was fast [excerpt][P53].
- **Reading** (inferred): cheap exit does not reliably kill voice. It changes behaviour only where it is fast, standard and in fact used.

## 3. The silent signer, and what a group's rules must state

**Everything in this section is the session's reading (inferred). It decides nothing.**

### 3.1 What the evidence says about the silent-signer flaw

1. **The flaw is old and has a known shape.** A rule that needs every named person, with no way out, is the Polish veto and the first American constitution. Both ended. The English partnership default has the same shape and the United States replaced it.
2. **No studied system keeps unanimity without a way out.** The ways out, from mildest to strongest:
   - **Leave the silent out of the count**, after notice and a wait. (Loans; a deceased member's estate losing its vote.)
   - **Lower the bar** below everyone, with silence still a "no". (Written resolutions; bond clauses.)
   - **Buy the silent share out** at a formula. (The revised partnership Act; squeeze-outs.)
   - **Count silence as "yes"**, for small matters only, with an objection threshold. (Insolvency.)
   - **A named third party decides.** (Courts.)
3. **The core already holds the first of these.** Agreements draft 24 states the cost itself: one silent party blocks every change to a deal. It names the ways out: the abandonment clause, succession plans, and a collective. Under that clause a named authority declares a party absent, and the declaration removes the voice (rule 89, outcome 0) or moves the stake (outcome 1). *This was read in `spec/MIR-agreements-draft-24.md`; the "Named signers" text itself was not found.* So the open matter may be narrower than the flaw sounds: whether "Named signers" hands silence to that clause, and what a reader sees when it does not.
4. **Silence and refusal are different, and the evidence treats them differently.** A way out for silence that also removes an active "no" is the "snooze drag". A text should be checkable on this point: a signer who answers "no" in time is never treated as absent.
5. **Who starts the clock matters more than how long it runs.** Every working design gives notice to the person, then waits, then lets them stop it. Clocks that run without provable notice are the Build Finance case.
6. **The harm from a missing way out falls late.** The first versions pass. The block arrives years on, when someone has died or lost a key. Uptake figures say almost nobody plans for it unprompted. So a default matters more than an option.

### 3.2 The safeguards a condition-based rule would need

Each line names the failure it answers.

| Safeguard | The failure it answers |
|---|---|
| The condition names an exact version by its hash, never a name or an address | Tornado Cash: the approved thing was swapped |
| Whatever the condition counts (signers, shares, weight) is read at a point fixed before the proposal | Beanstalk; Compound's snapshot is the fix |
| A stated wait between "the condition is true" and "in force" | Beanstalk: same-moment execution |
| The person who gains from the answer does not alone supply the inputs | Film accounting; Spain's usage logs |
| A stated list of what can never come into force by condition alone | Loans: the carve-out for money terms, and its erosion |
| An objection in time stops it, and sends it to the stricter path; it is not merely counted | English insolvency |
| The same proposal cannot be re-sent at once after failing | The 12-month limit on asking again |
| Notice to each person bound, with a record that it was sent | Build Finance; class-action notice rules |
| A way to leave during the wait, at a stated price, for those who did not agree | Moloch's grace period |
| That exit price never exceeds what the place could be bought for | Nouns, Rook, Aragon, Halifax |
| The condition for changing the condition is stated, and is stricter | Arbitrum; the layered reading of Ostrom |
| The count drops voices that are provably gone, by a stated rule | The DAO's quorum; unreachable quorums |
| The text cannot be rewritten for versions already in force | Greece 2012 |

**Candidates for the "one more attack" the other team's text is waiting for.** Offered for that team to test, not as findings about a text this session did not read:

- **Borrowed weight.** Hold what the condition counts for one moment only.
- **Swap after approval.** Make the condition true for one text, then put another in its place.
- **The measured input.** The party who benefits supplies the fact the condition reads.
- **Wear-down.** Re-send until an objector misses one deadline.
- **Withheld notice.** An objection or a notice that exists but is kept from the place the condition looks. *R2 is said to show that order is harder for a group than for one person; if so, "no objection arrived in time" is exactly the kind of fact a group cannot prove without naming where to look. R2 was not read.*
- **The cheapened bar.** Lower the condition by the condition, in small steps.
- **Buy to leave.** If leaving pays more than joining costs, joiners come only to leave.

### 3.3 What a cMIP for a group's rules must state

For a stranger to check it from the text alone, the text would need to answer each of these. Where the answer is "nothing", it should say "nothing".

**Who and what.**

1. Who counts: named signers, or holders of a stated stake, and at what moment the list is read.
2. Which matters it covers, and which it never reaches.

**Changing.**

3. The rule for each layer: everyday matters, the constitution, and this rule itself.
4. The exact thing an assent names: a version's hash.
5. What silence counts as for each layer: "no", not counted, or "yes".
6. If silence is ever not a "no": the notice, the clock, where the clock is read, what stops it, and the list of matters excluded.
7. What an in-time "no" does.
8. How soon a failed proposal may be sent again.
9. The wait between passing and force.

**Dividing.**

10. The shares, fixed before income.
11. What the shares are *of*: who states the income, and from which record.
12. Where a share waits when its holder cannot be found, for how long, who may claim it, and where it goes after. Whether it ever goes to the other holders, and in what proportion.
13. What an over-claim freezes: the disputed part, or everything.

**Leaving.**

14. How a member leaves and what they take, as a formula a stranger can compute.
15. When it is paid, and what may delay it.
16. What the leaver keeps: a share without a voice, or nothing.
17. Whether a place can be transferred, and whether the exit price can exceed the cost of entering.

**Disappearing.**

18. Who may declare a member absent, on what proof, and after what wait.
19. What the declaration does: removes the voice, moves the stake, or both.
20. What reverses it if the member returns.
21. Who succeeds, and whether a successor has a voice.

**Carrying on.**

22. What happens to all of this if the group forks, closes, or moves.

Lines 11, 12 and 17 are the ones the frame did not have and the evidence added.
