# MOR Case Study: Paid for Results, Seen for What It Is

## Why this case study

Advertising pays for most of the free internet, and almost nobody in its chain can see the whole of it.

On the open web, an advertiser's money passes through an agency, a buying platform, an ad exchange, a selling platform, data brokers and verification firms before it reaches a publisher. Each ad slot is auctioned in milliseconds every time a page loads. A 2020 study for the UK advertisers' association ISBA, carried out by PwC, found that publishers received only about half of what advertisers spent, and that around 15% could not be traced at all. The largest platforms avoid the chain by doing everything themselves: they sell the ads, run the auctions, hold the data and report the results, marking their own homework.

The costs fall on everyone. Views are cheap to fake, so fraud thrives. Targeting depends on tracking people across the web. The viewer gets nothing for their attention or their data. Publishers are judged by clicks, which rewards outrage.

MOR was born from this problem. Its earliest design had an advertising layer, and its first law was written for it: **rewards attach to settlements, never to attention.** This case study starts with what that law makes possible, advertising paid only when it leads to a real sale, and then accommodates the world as it is, where much advertising buys attention, by making that legible too.

## 1. A sale share

**Ana roasts coffee** and sells it online. She has no advertising budget, so she publishes a standing offer: "10% of any sale you lead to."

**Anyone can promote her coffee.** Jules, who makes videos about cooking, mentions Ana's coffee and links to it. When a viewer buys, the viewer's own wallet signs the referral into its record of the payment: "I came from Jules's video." That signed referral is the evidence, and Ana's split service pays Jules's share against it, with its own receipt. Ana's side cannot invent a referrer of its own, and cannot quietly drop Jules, because the only referral that counts is the one the buyer signed. This is the same role share that pays the reposter of a song in the music case study, and the same rule: evidence for a share comes from the other side of the deal.

**Ana pays only for results.** If Jules's video sells nothing, it costs Ana nothing. If it sells a lot, both earn.

**Fraud stops being worth it.** A fake view costs nothing to produce; a fake sale costs the price of the product. There is no advantage in bots.

**Returns are part of the terms.** Ana's offer can hold the referrer's share until her return window closes, on a named time reference, so a refunded purchase pays no share.

**Attribution is a question for an attribution cMIP.** A buyer may have seen Ana's coffee in three videos before buying. Who earns the share? For example:

- **last referrer:** the share goes to whoever the buyer came from;
- **first referrer:** the share goes to whoever introduced the product;
- **shared:** the share is divided among every referrer the buyer's client recorded within a time window.

Whichever rule applies, the buyer's wallet is the one that records and signs the referrers it saw.

## 2. A direct sponsorship

**Ana likes Jules's work** and wants a longer relationship. They sign an agreement: a flat monthly fee, plus the sale share on everything Jules's viewers buy.

**The sponsorship is visible to viewers.** Many countries require sponsored content to be disclosed. On MOR, a sponsored video names its sponsorship agreement, so every client can show it clearly. Viewers see who is paid to recommend what.

**Nobody stands in between.** There is no network, no exchange and no broker. The creator and the brand deal directly, as podcast hosts and their sponsors often do today.

## 3. The viewer's side

**Targeting happens on the viewer's device.** The viewer's own client knows what they read, watch and buy. It chooses which offers to show from what it knows locally, and nothing leaves the device. Brave's browser and Google's Privacy Sandbox, since largely abandoned, tried versions of this idea.

**The viewer can share in the sale.** A brand's offer can include a share for the buyer: a discount or cashback on the purchase itself. The viewer benefits when an ad serves them, not merely when it reaches them.

**The viewer can refuse.** Clients can hide all offers, show only offers from creators the viewer follows, or only sponsorships the viewer agreed to see. The viewer's dial. And the viewer decides whether their wallet signs a referral at all: a buyer who wants no part in attribution can pay without one, and then nobody earns a share on that sale.

## 4. An ad network as a matcher

**Ana's business grows,** and finding creators by hand becomes impossible. An ad network appears: a matcher between brands with standing offers and creators looking for products that fit their audiences.

**The network earns a visible role share** in each sale it helped create, evidenced like any other: by the buyer's signed referral, or by the network's own signed record of the introduction it made. Brands and creators see its cut in every settlement.

**Networks compete** on matching quality, on the creators they bring, and on their fees. They have ratings, as matchers do in the gig economy case study. A brand can use several at once and compare them in real settlements.

## 5. The world as it is: attention

**A car maker does not sell cars from a video link.** Much advertising aims at awareness: being remembered when someone buys a car years later. No sale share can pay for that, and awareness is the main reason attention-based advertising exists. This is where MOR accommodates the status quo: it could be sale shares everywhere, but the world as it is demands attention deals too.

**Attention deals are allowed, and legible.** The car maker's agency buys views through intermediaries, on MOR, and everything the chain does is visible to those it concerns:

- **Every hop is a receipt, and every payer keeps a record.** Like the payment routes in the Finance MIP, each intermediary's cut is a line the advertiser can see, and the advertiser's own record of what it paid stands against any intermediary that reports less. Intermediaries stay; they show what they take. Nothing goes untraced.
- **Placements are signed by publishers.** The advertiser sees exactly where each ad ran, signed by the publisher's identity, with its ratings. Fake sites cannot hide behind an exchange.
- **Metrics come from named modules.** Views and clicks are claims signed by the metric module that measured them, never by the party being paid for them, as in the video case study. Competing verification services audit them, and the advertiser chooses which to trust.
- **Data brokers are visible.** If targeting data is bought, the purchase is a line in the settlement. What the data contained stays between the parties; that it was bought does not.

**The viewer can be paid for attention too,** if they agree: a share for viewing ads they opted into, paid to the viewer's pointer. This is an attention deal, allowed and visible for what it is.

**The subtext is an invitation.** Everything in this section exists because settlement-based advertising cannot yet answer awareness. That is not a verdict; it is a challenge. Builders who dislike the attention economy are invited to make the novel models good enough to compete with it. For example, a brand could hold a share for referrers that vests whenever a buyer's purchase, years later, names the video that first introduced the brand. Whoever solves awareness on settlements takes the last ground attention holds.

## 6. What brands, agencies and platforms keep

- **Walled gardens keep their systems.** A large platform can keep running its own auctions and its own data, and publish on MOR as a publisher like any other. Advertisers compare it with open channels in real settlements, not in the platform's own reports.
- **Brands keep every model they use today:** attention campaigns, sponsorships, performance marketing. They gain visibility into where their money goes, and a channel where fraud does not pay.
- **Agencies remain valuable** for strategy, creative work and media planning. On MOR they earn visible fees, and compete on the value of their advice rather than on hidden margins.
- **Publishers get paid for what they actually sell,** signed and visible, and can compare every buyer.

## 7. The network behind it

- **Brands:** publishing standing offers and sponsorships.
- **Creators and publishers:** earning sale shares, sponsorships and attention deals.
- **Ad networks:** matching brands and creators for a visible role share.
- **Agencies:** planning and buying, for visible fees.
- **Metric and verification services:** making signed claims and auditing each other.
- **Viewers:** choosing what they see, signing what they followed, and sharing in what they buy.
- **Developers:** building offer clients, on-device targeting, attribution logic and campaign tools.

## 8. What MOR provides, and what it needs

**From the core, nothing new.** Standing offers, role shares with third-party evidence, the payer's signed referral, splits and receipts, the payer's own payment record, time references, agreements and sponsorships, signed publications, payment route disclosure, metric claims as acts signed by their module, and payee pointers are already defined.

**Open for others to build (cMIPs and Modules):**

- **Sale-share cMIPs:** offers, referral records in the buyer's wallet, holds until the return window closes.
- **Attribution cMIPs:** last referrer, first referrer, shared.
- **Sponsorship cMIPs,** including disclosure to viewers.
- **On-device targeting modules** that choose offers without sending data anywhere.
- **Buyer-share cMIPs:** discounts and cashback for the viewer.
- **Metric modules and audit services** for attention deals.
- **Supply-chain disclosure** for multi-hop ad buying.
- **Awareness models:** settlement-based answers to brand advertising, the open challenge.

## 9. Honest limits

- **Attribution is imperfect.** Nobody knows for sure which video made someone buy. Every attribution rule is a convention.
- **A share needs the buyer's signature.** A buyer who pays through a wallet that records no referral earns Jules nothing on that sale. That is the price of evidence no seller can fake, and it is why wallets that record referrals well will matter to creators.
- **Sale shares favour products sold online.** Services, local shops and slow purchases fit less naturally.
- **Creators can become salespeople.** A creator paid on sales may recommend what pays best. Sponsorships and shares are visible, so viewers can weigh it; they are not prevented.
- **Privacy depends on clients.** On-device targeting protects the viewer only if the client really keeps data local.
- **Attention deals stay attention deals.** Legibility exposes fraud and hidden cuts; it does not make views honest.
- **Awareness is unsolved on settlements,** and may stay so for a long time.
- **Law comes first** on disclosure, consumer protection and data.

## 10. What it demonstrates

A coffee roaster paying a creator for sales, and a car maker buying millions of views through an agency, can use the same network. The novel model comes first: advertising paid only when it produces a real sale, evidenced by the buyer, with no tracking, no fraud worth doing and no hidden middlemen. The world as it is follows: attention deals, allowed and made legible, every hop a receipt, every placement signed, every metric signed by its measurer. Greed is not banned; it is made visible, and the better models are invited to win.
