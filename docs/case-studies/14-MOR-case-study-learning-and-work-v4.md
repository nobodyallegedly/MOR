## Why this case study

Education and the job market speak to each other through a thin, old channel: the diploma. A diploma says that an institution vouched for someone once. It does not say what they can do today, how good the teaching was, or whether anything they learned was ever used. Employers read diplomas, then test candidates anyway. Professional networks let people list their qualifications, and listing is all they do: anyone can claim anything.

Meanwhile, people learn everywhere: from online lessons, tutors, workshops, jobs, and years of practice that no diploma records. Recognition of prior learning exists, but it is slow, local and with very little lateral transfer.

This case study puts education and the job market side by side on the same rules. Its core is a **decentralised accreditation system**: instead of one authority granting accreditation, many signed streams of ratings are weighted and combined by a module of the user's choice into an accreditation score. On the other side, a person's profile no longer lists diplomas: it **shows** what they can do, each claim linked to the evidence behind it, a track record of proven or assessed skills and knowledge.

It starts with one teacher and one lesson, and ends with universities and employers. Under MOR everyone can keep their place, it is only what’s humanly possible that changes. 

## 1. The players

- **Students:** learn, pay, rate, and own their record.
- **Teachers:** make lessons, which are works.
- **Tutors:** support students, paid through named shares.
- **Aggregators:** curriculum designers, who compile lessons into modules, courses and curricula, as editors compile assets in the journalism case study.
- **Locations:** rooms, studios, workshops, paid through named shares.
- **Equipment rentals:** cameras, instruments, machines, paid through "tools used" named shares.
- **Industry experts:** review lessons, sometimes before they are published.
- **Assessors:** judge what students can actually do, as a service students buy.
- **Recognition of prior learning:** RPL advisors, who help people gather evidence of what they learned outside school, and RPL assessors, who judge that evidence.
- **Recruiters and HR specialists:** match people to jobs, and describe what jobs require.
- **Employers:** hire, and much later report how well what was learned held up at work.

## 2. One lesson

**Elena teaches colour grading.** She records a lesson: videos, exercises, and a project. The lesson is a work, bound to her. Each improved version is a new work naming the previous one.

**Experts review it before release.** Elena invites two industry colourists to review the lesson before it goes public. She pays them for their time. Their signed reviews are published with the lesson on release day. A pre-release review is a prediction: "this lesson will teach grading well". Later streams will show whether it was right.

**Students enrol and pay.** Enrolment is an agreement and a payment. The lesson's split pays Elena, a tutor who answers questions each week, the studio where the live session takes place, and the rental service that lends the grading panels, each with its own receipt.

**Students rate the lesson.** Each rating is a signed act pointing at the student's enrolment, using a rating cMIP. Other teachers rate it too, and so does an aggregator considering it for a curriculum.

## 3. Assessed

**Karim finishes the lesson** and wants his skill to count. He books an assessor randomly selected under the matching rules: an experienced colourist who assesses grading work, independent of Elena, with no direct stake in her lesson. The match happens between the knowledge and skills promised by Elena and the knowledge and skills assessed by the assessor. Karim pays for the assessment, as candidates pay for a driving test or a language exam today.

**The assessor measures progress, not only results.** If Karim takes a short test before the lesson and a project after it, the assessor can judge both, so the record shows what the lesson added, not just what Karim already knew. 

**The result belongs to Karim.** The assessor signs an attestation of attainment, bound to Karim's identity: what he can do, at what level, assessed by whom. It also feeds the lesson's accreditation, as the strongest stream it has.

**Strictness is what assessors sell.** A lenient assessor who passes everyone would attract students at first. But every assessor has a Trust Score (section 5), and an attestation from a lenient one carries little weight in any accreditation module or with any employer. Students who want their attainment to count seek strict, credible assessors.

## 4. Accreditation, computed

**Many streams, arriving over time:**

1. **before release:** industry experts' reviews;
2. **during and after the lesson:** students, other teachers, aggregators;
3. **after assessment:** attainment, judged by independent assessors, the anchor;
4. **years later:** employers, once former students have used what they learned at work.

**Nobody grants accreditation; modules compute it.** An accreditation module reads the streams, weighs each rating by its author's Trust Score and each stream by its reliability, and computes a score. A university, a trade association and an employer can each run a different module on the same public streams and get their own answer. Choosing a module is choosing a method.

**The score settles as evidence arrives.** It starts from predictions and opinions, and moves towards evidence as attainment and employer feedback come in.

**Granularity is free.** The unit of accreditation can be a single lesson, a module, a course, a curriculum, a teacher or a location. An aggregator compiles Elena's lesson with others into a course on post-production. The course names its lessons, and when it earns, lineage pays each lesson's teacher, if the course's agreement honours the lessons' offers; if it does not, its published split shows exactly that. The course has its own accreditation, and so does each lesson inside it.

**Everyone who rates can share in what the unit earns,** whatever their verdict: the lesson's plan carries a share for a named receiver, such as the raters' pool or a collective of raters. An expert who criticised the lesson holds the same kind of share as one who praised it. A share could quietly reward praise, since raters benefit when the unit sells. **How shares work is a question for an accreditation cMIP**, for example:

- **a flat share:** every rater holds the same share, whatever their verdict;
- **a trust-weighted share:** each rater's share follows their Trust Score at the time of payout, so the shares of past inflators shrink;
- **an accuracy-scaled share:** the share vests only once attainment and employer feedback arrive, and grows with how well the rating predicted them, a *proper scoring rule* under which telling the truth is always the best strategy;
- **simple payment:** a fee per rating, no share at all.

**Assigned raters.** If raters only choose what to rate, weak lessons may go unrated. An accreditation cMIP can let aggregators or accreditation modules assign some raters to each unit, like a jury.

## 5. The Trust Score

**Every participant has one:** students, teachers, experts, aggregators, assessors, employers.

**It measures coherence over time:** whether what someone says holds up against what later happens. An expert who predicted that a lesson would teach well, and whose prediction was borne out by attainment, gains trust. A teacher who rated a colleague's weak lesson highly, and was contradicted by the assessments, loses it. An assessor whose results agree with other independent assessors, and later with employers, gains it.

**Changing one's mind is not a contradiction.** A revision is a new signed act, resolved forward in time. A contradiction is two opposite claims standing at once. The Trust Score rewards learning, not rigidity.

**Calibrate against outcomes, not against the crowd.** A trust score that rewards agreeing with the majority, or with the assessor, becomes an agreement detector and breeds conformity. Anchoring trust in assessed attainment and employer outcomes keeps it a measure of being right, not of being agreeable.

**Trust scoring is a module too.** For example:

- **outcome calibration:** trust grows with how well past ratings predicted attainment and employer feedback;
- **peer agreement:** trust grows with agreement among independent raters of the same unit, useful where outcomes take years;
- **coherence over time:** trust follows the consistency between a participant's words and actions across everything they sign.

## 6. Learning that happened elsewhere

**Rosa has graded films for twelve years** without ever taking a course. She has no diploma in colour grading, and no way to show what she knows except her reel and her word.

**An RPL advisor helps her build her evidence.** Much of it is already on MOR: the films she graded are works naming her in their credits. Her agreements with production companies, her receipts and her gig ratings from clients are signed records. The advisor helps her gather them, adds outside evidence (letters, a reel), and maps it all against the learning outcomes of the course that Elena's lesson belongs to. Rosa pays the advisor for this service.

**An RPL assessor judges the evidence.** The assessor is independent, like any assessor. Where evidence is thin, the assessor sets a practical test. Rosa receives the same kind of attestation of attainment as Karim, and it counts in the same way.

**The doors are the same for everyone.** Whether someone learned from a lesson, on the job or alone, what counts is what they can show.

## 7. Shown, not listed

**Karim's and Rosa's profiles show what they can do.** A profile on MOR is not a list of claims. Each line links to its evidence:

- attestations of attainment, signed by assessors with visible Trust Scores;
- the accreditation of the lessons and courses behind them;
- works they made, with their credits;
- ratings from clients and employers, tied to real jobs;
- recognitions of prior learning.

Anyone reading the profile can follow each line to the signed record behind it. A professional network built on this does not need to ask anyone to trust a list.

**The record belongs to the learner.** It lives under the learner's identity, not on a school's server or a networking site. If a school closes or a platform shuts down, nothing is lost.

**The learner chooses what to show.** A profile shows what its owner decides to show. With credential modules, Karim can prove a fact about his record ("assessed at an advanced level in colour grading, within the last three years") without revealing everything behind it.

## 8. The industry layers

**Industry describes what it needs.** Industry experts and HR specialists publish competence frameworks: signed descriptions of what a job requires. Aggregators map their curricula to them, and assessors assess against them. A job advertisement can point to the competences it needs rather than to diplomas.

**Recruiters match.** A recruiter matches Karim to a post-production studio, reading his demonstrated competences instead of guessing from a CV. The recruiter's fee is a share in the hiring agreement, naming the recruiter as its receiver, and everyone involved has ratings of their own. 

**Employers close the loop.** A year after hiring Karim, the studio reports how well his grading skills held up at work. That feedback flows back: to the assessor who attested his level, to Elena's lesson, to the course, to the experts who predicted the lesson would teach well. Everyone's Trust Score adjusts. This loop takes years to fill; it is far away, and it is the most valuable stream of all.

**Employer feedback touches a person's working life,** so it needs their consent. **How it flows back is a question for a feedback cMIP**, for example:

- **with the employee's consent,** for each piece of feedback;
- **aggregated and anonymous:** many employers' feedback is combined, and proofs show it came from real employers of real graduates without revealing who;
- **only to the employee,** who decides whether to share it.

## 9. What universities and employers keep

- **Universities keep their strengths:** their teachers, their campuses, their curricula, their brand. On MOR, a university is an aggregator, an assessor and a location at once, and its degree is a bundle of accredited units, each one visible.
- **Their brand becomes evidence.** A university whose graduates perform well sees it in the streams, and every accreditation module weighs its assessments accordingly. Reputation stops being inherited and becomes demonstrated, which favours institutions that teach well.
- **Accreditation bodies become modules.** A national accreditation agency can publish its own accreditation module and keep its role, with its method visible.
- **Regulated professions stay regulated.** Medicine, law and other licensed professions still require licences from the state. A licence can be a signed credential on MOR; the state still issues it.
- **Employers hire with less risk.** They read demonstrated competences, and their own feedback improves the pipeline they hire from.

## 10. What MOR provides, and what it needs

**From the core, nothing new.** Identities, works and versions, agreements and payments, splits and role shares, stakes, signed attestations, the task for identity proofs, collectives and grants are already defined; lineage is built from these core pieces. A rating, a review, an attestation and a feedback report are all signed acts pointing at other acts.

**Open for others to build (cMIPs and Modules):**

- **Rating and review cMIPs** for lessons, courses, teachers and locations.
- **Accreditation cMIPs:** streams, weights, shares for raters, assigned raters.
- **Accreditation modules:** the computations, one per method.
- **Trust Score modules:** outcome calibration, peer agreement, coherence over time.
- **Assessment cMIPs:** independence rules, before-and-after assessment, attestations of attainment.
- **RPL cMIPs:** evidence portfolios, mapping to learning outcomes, RPL assessment.
- **Competence frameworks:** signed descriptions of what jobs require.
- **Profile clients:** showing demonstrated competences with their evidence.
- **Credential modules:** proving facts about a record without revealing all of it.
- **Feedback cMIPs:** employer feedback with consent or anonymity.
- **Recruiting and matching modules.**

## 11. Honest limits

- **What gets measured gets gamed.** Teachers may teach to the assessment. Independent assessors, progress measured before and after, and employer feedback make gaming harder, not impossible.
- **Not everything valuable is measurable.** Curiosity, judgement and character do not fit neatly into attainment.
- **The loop is slow.** Employer feedback takes years. For a long time, scores rest on predictions and assessments.
- **Newcomers start with nothing.** A new teacher or assessor has no track record. Modules must give them a fair start.
- **Paying for assessment is a barrier.** Those who cannot pay are left out unless assessments are funded by targets, pools, employers or the state.
- **Privacy is delicate.** A learning and working record is personal. Selective disclosure and consent help; they must be designed with care.
- **Legitimacy is earned slowly.** Employers and institutions will trust computed accreditation only after it has proved itself over years.
- **Law comes first** for regulated professions and for officially recognised diplomas.

## 12. What it demonstrates

A single lesson and a university degree, a first job and twelve years of experience, all use the same rules. Accreditation stops being a stamp from one authority and becomes a score anyone can compute from signed evidence, with every rater's record on the line. Learners own what they have shown, wherever and however they learned it. Employers read evidence instead of claims, and their feedback, years later, makes the whole system more honest. Decentralising an industry, it turns out, happens one role at a time: each role can be filled by anyone, each judgement is signed, and each claim can be followed to what stands behind it.
