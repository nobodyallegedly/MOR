## Why this case study

A journal does three jobs at once.

- **It carries.** It holds the work and serves it to readers.
- **It stamps.** It has the work reviewed and says: this version passed.
- **It lends a name.** A paper in a known journal borrows the journal's standing, and careers are counted in those names.

The three were bundled when carrying meant printing and posting. Carrying is now nearly free, and it is still what readers pay for. Reviewing is done by researchers, mostly unpaid. The name is what the fee really buys. The public often pays three times: for the research, for the salaries of those who review it, and for the right to read the result.

Others have already pulled these jobs apart. Overlay journals review papers that stay on a public preprint server. Peer Community In reviews and recommends preprints with no journal at all. eLife stopped deciding "accept or reject" and publishes reviewed preprints with a public assessment. Each shows that the stamp can be separated from the carrying. None of them ties in who is paid, who built on what, or what happens when the service closes.

On MOR the three jobs are three different things. Carrying is what a relay does. A stamp is a signed act. A name is the record of the identity that signed it. Each can be done by whoever has a reason to, and a university department turns out to have one.

This case study follows a researcher, Noor, fresh out of a postgraduate degree in hydrology, with no PhD yet. It starts small, with a six-page proposal that already carries a finding, and follows it through review, funding, a large project and a cooperation with industry. Along the way it follows the money, because where money comes from, and why, decides what gets researched.

MOR does not do research and does not judge whether a result is true. Laboratories, ethics boards and the judgement of peers stay where they are. MOR carries who wrote what, who built on it, who vouched for it, who paid, and why.

## 1. The small: a proposal

**Noor has an identity,** with a signing key on her laptop and a safety key on an offline device, both seeds backed up, at a home she chose.

**Her proposal carries a finding.** Her master's work suggested that the water reaching an aquifer can be estimated from how fast the soil dries after rain, with cheap moisture sensors, instead of from rainfall records. One sentence says it: *"Recharge is written in how fast a soil dries, not in how much rain fell."* The proposal asks for money to test it in the field.

**The proposal is a work,** text bound to her by a work claim. She publishes it openly: stored locked, like everything on MOR, with its key attached. She anchors it through an anchoring cMIP, so it provably existed by a point on a named time reference. Order is not evidence of authorship, but if two people ever claim the same finding, an anchor is where the claims can be compared.

**Her own sources are attributed as she writes.** Her writing tool has a MOR plug-in. When she pastes a passage from a work on MOR, the plug-in attaches a reference to that work. When she rewrites someone's idea in her own words, she marks the passage as a paraphrase and the plug-in asks for its source. Older literature outside MOR is referenced by its web address, with the hash of what was there. Attributing a paraphrase is already good academic practice; the plug-in makes the honest path the default. It is client software under a citation cMIP. It signs nothing: Noor signs her proposal, references included.

*What the core carries here is the reference from one work to another, whole. Marking which passage quotes which passage is the citation cMIP's job (open question 1).*

## 2. Carrying: the department's shelf

**Each willing department has a reason to run a relay,** old school style, carrying the work it chooses, as its library once held the journals it chose. A hydrology department's relay holds its members' work, the works they build on, and whatever else it judges worth keeping.

**Its reasons are its own.** What it carries says what it stands for. Its students read from a shelf chosen by people they know. Its own output no longer sits on someone else's servers. It is paid for from the department's budget, where journal subscriptions once were: public money or tuition, spent on what a department is for.

**Libraries do the same at their own scale:** a university library, a learned society, a national library, each a relay run by an identity, each choosing.

**Noor offers her proposal to the relays that fit:** her own department's, two hydrology departments' elsewhere, a national library's. Each decides by its own policy. Those that take it mirror it, and she adds them to her routes, so any client can find the copies.

**Nothing depends on one of them.** The proposal is the same signed act wherever it is held. If a department closes its relay, the others still serve it.

**Carried is not stamped.** A relay that accepts a text has made a small editorial choice. It has not reviewed it.

## 3. Stamping: an inbox and an outbox

**A department can do more than carry.** It is an identity, here a collective of its staff, and its routes say two things. Its **inbox** is where authors deliver work they would like it to review. Its **outbox** is where anyone finds what it has stamped.

**Noor chooses where to send.** Her own department would review her gladly, and its stamp on its own graduate would weigh little. She delivers the proposal to the inbox of a department abroad known for soil physics, the part of her method most likely to be wrong. She sends it openly; an author with an unpublished manuscript can send it as a private act sealed to the department, which no relay can read and whose sender no relay sees.

**The department assigns two reviewers,** one of its own staff and one outsider it pays a fee. Each review is a signed act pointing at the exact version reviewed, under the review cMIP the department has adopted. In each, the reviewer names the matters the judgement concerns.

**The outsider holds no academic post.** Ilse is a soil engineer who has spent twenty years on drainage and foundations in clay for a construction firm. She has no doctorate and has never published a paper. The department found her by her record, not her title: it looked for reviewers by matter, and on soil physics hers was among the best it could see.

- The staff reviewer, a professor, on field methods: the approach is sound and should hold across soil types.
- Ilse, on soil physics: it should hold in sandy soils; *"I expect the sensors to drift in clay."*

**A review is a prediction.** Section 6 shows which of these two held.

**The stamp.** The department's founding agreement gives stamping to an area held by its five-member review committee, any two of whom must sign. The collective signs a stamp: this version, reviewed under this review cMIP, by these reviews, found worth funding as a pilot. Two committee members sign it, and it appears in the department's outbox. The department may also publish the stamped version as its own publication.

**Each reviewer has a Trust Score on the matters reviewed.** The accreditation case study describes the Trust Score: a module's computation of how well what someone said held up against what later happened. Here it is kept by matter. Beside Ilse's review a reader sees: on soil physics, eleven past predictions, nine borne out; on sensor electronics, none. The eleven come from reviews she signed for two departments and a utility over four years, each later met by field results. Nothing in the computation asks where she works. The module shows the count, because a score built on three predictions is an anecdote. It is computed from public signed records, and a different module will compute it differently.

**Who pays for the stamp, and why.** The department, from its own budget: staff review as part of salaried work, outsiders for a flat fee, whatever their verdict. Its reason is standing. What it stamps says what it is good at, and a department that stamps carelessly has that on its record.

**Signed or blind is a choice,** not decided here. It belongs to the review cMIP a department, journal or funder adopts (section 11).

**A review lives on its reviewer's own chain,** so, as with ratings in the gig economy case study, it survives the reviewer's rotation only if kept: Noor can pin one with a witness act, which is public, or the department's agreement can name a keeper that records reviews as they arrive.

## 4. A sentence travels

**First, published.** Noor's sentence exists in her proposal, a work bound to her.

**Then, quoted.** A hydrologist in another country writes a survey of recharge methods and quotes the sentence. His plug-in attaches the reference to her proposal. His survey now names her work, the way a remix names its stems in the music case study.

**Then, paraphrased.** A journalist writing about drought reads the survey, not the proposal, and paraphrases the idea. She names what she actually read: the survey. A water agency's report later paraphrases the newspaper.

**Always traced.** Each work names its immediate source. A reader of the agency's report can walk back: report, article, survey, proposal. Today that chain usually breaks at the second step, and the survey gets the credit.

**Citations knot the chains together.** The survey names the proposal by its hash, and nobody can name a hash that does not exist yet. So the survey was made after the proposal, with no clock and nobody's date to trust. That gives order, not dates; for a date, authors anchor.

**Lineage can carry money, and here mostly does not.** Noor's offer is the usual academic one: free to quote, with attribution. If the survey were sold as a book chapter, its split could carry a share for the works it stands on, if its own agreement honoured their offers; if not, its published split would show exactly that.

## 5. Where the money comes from, and why

Every later step is shaped by who pays and what they want.

| Source | Where its money comes from | Why it pays | What it asks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A foundation | An endowment, given for farming under drought | Its founders' purpose | Work on farmland; a public result |
| A public research council | Taxes | Water is a public matter | Open results, reports, accountable spending |
| The university and its departments | Public funding, tuition | Standing, teaching, its share of awards | Overhead, its name on the work |
| Industry partners | Sales of their products | A usable method before competitors have it | First use, a period of secrecy, a share of what results earn |
| Readers and their libraries | Mostly the same institutions and taxes again | Access | Access that lasts |

**The foundation publishes a call:** signed terms saying what it funds and how proposals are judged. Its panel reads Noor's proposal, the department's stamp, the two reviews with their reviewers' records, and the trace of her sentence through other people's work. It awards $50,000 for a one-year pilot.

**The award is a deal** between the foundation, the university that hosts Noor, and Noor. It names two payments tied to milestones on a named time reference, a keeper, and what must be published. The university's overhead is a line in it, for example 15%, visible to all three. A deal changes only with every party's signature: the foundation cannot quietly change the second payment's terms, the university cannot raise its overhead, and Noor cannot move a milestone.

**The money's first bend.** The foundation funds farming. Its terms say the pilot runs on irrigated farmland. Farmland in that region is sandy loam, the soil where Ilse expected the method to work. The clay soils, where she expected it to fail, are outside what this money is for. Noor signs. The clause is in the deal, so anyone reading the pilot's results later can see why they cover the soils they cover.

**The pilot works,** on sandy loam. The research council funds the full project: $1.8 million over four years, paid to the university, which answers in law for public money. Its signed terms require results and data public by a named point, and yearly reports. Its remit is national, so this time the clay is in.

## 6. A large project, and a failure worth publishing

**The project becomes a collective:** a full identity with its own keys and a founding agreement every founder signs. Its members are Noor, a senior hydrologist and the university.

**Standing is written down, not hidden.** The council requires a principal investigator with a doctorate, so the senior hydrologist holds that role. Noor negotiates one thing before she signs: the area for the project's publications is held by her and the senior hydrologist together, both needed. Nothing is published in the project's name, and nothing is held back, without her. The university holds the Finance lane, since it answers for the money; its research officer signs under a grant from the university. Any two of the three members can rotate the collective's safety key.

**Each result is a work with its own claim:** papers, datasets and analysis code, each bound to the people who made it, with their roles. Stakes in a result are set when it exists.

**Helpers known in advance are paid by fee or by named share.** A field technician, three farmers who host stations, and a sensor supplier are known before any result exists. Each is paid a fee under their own agreement, with a receipt; or, where one prefers to bet on a result, through a share naming them as its receiver in that result's plan. Role shares are for helpers known only at payment time.

**Spending is on the record for those it concerns.** Each payment from the award has a receipt, and the yearly report points at them.

**In the second season the sensors drift in clay.** The method fails there, as Ilse wrote it would.

**The money's second bend.** A failure has no buyer. The journal the project had in mind publishes new methods, not their limits. The award has a line for one publication fee. The senior hydrologist would rather finish the main paper first and mention clay in a paragraph.

**Noor writes it up anyway.** Dataset version 2 is a new work naming version 1, and a short paper says where the method does not hold and by how much. Nothing is edited: the survey that quoted her still points at the exact text it quoted. She needs her co-holder's signature to publish in the project's name, and gets it after an argument that is theirs to have. She delivers the paper to the inbox of the department that stamped her proposal. A department strong in soil physics has a reason to stamp a careful negative result that a journal selling novelty does not. It reviews it, stamps it and carries it. No publication fee is paid.

**The record settles.** Ilse's count on soil physics is now twelve, ten borne out. The engineer was right and the professor was not, and the record says so without comment. The professor signs a short note revising the view, a new act, and a module that rewards coherence over time treats a revision differently from a contradiction left standing. The department's stamp on the proposal said "worth a pilot", and the pilot held; that is on its record too.

**A new way of taking part.** Ilse is paid a fee for each review. Two more departments now send her work on clay. She has not joined academia; her judgement has, on the one matter where it is among the best there is.

**A retraction would be the same kind of act:** signed by the authors, pointing at what it retracts. A publisher can withdraw its own publication; it cannot remove the authors' work, and nobody can make the works that cited it point elsewhere.

**Another group replicates** on its own soils and signs its report, naming the dataset and the method. Agreement and disagreement both count.

## 7. Lending a name: the journal

**The project still sends its main paper to a journal,** and for a plain reason: the hiring committee that will read Noor's file in three years counts journal names.

**A journal is one more identity with an inbox and an outbox.** Its acceptance is the same kind of act as the department's stamp: this version, reviewed under this review cMIP, accepted. Its editors act under grants. What sets it apart is what readers and committees have come to think of its signature.

**Its fee is legible.** The publishing deal between the project and the journal names a publication fee, for example $2,400, paid from the award, so from taxes. The settlement shows what the journal kept and what it paid its reviewers, if anything. A journal may charge any fee and pay reviewers nothing; every author and funder can see it, and one that does better can show the difference.

**The paper stays the authors'.** The journal publishes it as its own publication and may hold a stake in that publication, never in the paper. Many journals ask authors to hand over their rights. On MOR that would be a transfer of stakes in the work, signed by the authors: allowed, and visible for what it is.

**Readers.** The council's terms require open access, so the paper's key is attached. A journal that sells access does so through standing offers; the buyer is usually a library, paying with institutional money. A relay that delivers a paid copy can earn a role share where the owners offer one, on its signed delivery record, which counts only once the buyer's own payment record acknowledges it.

**Two stamps, side by side.** The main paper now carries the journal's acceptance. The clay paper carries a department's stamp. A reader sees both for what they are: who signed, under which review rules, with what record.

## 8. From theory to experiment to use

**A sensor manufacturer and a regional water utility approach the project.** The research moves from theoretical, to experimental, to applicable, and the cooperation gets more complex.

**A consortium is founded: a collective made of collectives.** Its members are the project, the manufacturer and the utility. Its founding agreement, signed by all three, gives each part of the work to those who answer for it: the project holds the area for publications; the manufacturer and the project together hold the area for the calibration software; who judges a dispute changes only with every member's signature.

**Why the companies pay.** The manufacturer puts in $400,000 and hardware, from its sales, because a sensor that also estimates recharge is a product. The utility gives access to its wells, because better estimates save it drilling.

**The money's third bend, and the largest.** Noor's finding was that cheap sensors are enough. The manufacturer sells expensive ones. Its first draft of the terms has the calibration built for its own sensor line only, and the method description kept private for three years.

**What she can refuse, and what she cannot.** The method description is a publication of the project, and the project's publications need her signature. The council's terms already require it public. So the negotiation, on the record, ends here:

- **A period of secrecy, six months.** New results stay private that long, so a patent can be filed. They are anchored the day they exist, so priority is not lost, and made public afterwards by publishing their key.
- **The calibration software for the manufacturer's sensors is the manufacturer's to license,** exclusively, for five years.
- **A share of what results earn.** The results do not exist yet, and a stake names an existing work by its hash. So the manufacturer holds a stake in the consortium itself, a share of its income; or the parties agree that a stake in each result is transferred to it once that result exists, by a transfer both sign.
- **Nobody funds the cheap-sensor calibration.** The manufacturer has no reason to, and the council's award is nearly spent. The gap is not hidden: the method is public, and the consortium's terms show what was paid for and what was not. Another group, or a utility in a poorer region, can see exactly what is missing.

**Public money and private money stay apart.** The council's award is paid to the university and spent under its receipts. The consortium receives what the results earn: licences, sales of calibrated data.

**Noor's part does not depend on her title.** She joined with a proposal and no doctorate. Her claim on the first work, her stake in each result she helped make, her signature on what the project published and the trace from every later use back to her sentence stay hers, whoever employs her next.

## 9. The network behind it

- **Researchers:** paid by salaries from awards, and by stakes in results where results earn.
- **Departments:** carrying the work they choose; receiving work in their inbox, having it reviewed, and publishing what they stamp in their outbox; paid from their own budgets.
- **Libraries:** carrying and preserving at a larger scale, paid by their institutions, and by delivery shares where owners offer them.
- **Reviewers:** academics and practitioners alike, paid by salary or by fee, building a record by matter.
- **Journals and publishers:** selecting, editing and lending their name, paid by publication fees, standing offers and publication stakes.
- **Funders and their panels:** publishing calls, signing awards, reading receipts.
- **Universities:** hosting, employing, answering for the money, paid by overhead.
- **Technicians, hosts and suppliers:** paid by fee or named share.
- **Replicators:** paid from awards that include replication.
- **Keepers and anchoring services:** recording awards, reviews and priority.
- **Developers:** building the plug-ins, formats and modules below.

## 10. What the established players keep

- **Journals keep selection, editing and their name.** They can set any fee. Exclusive terms and embargoes still work, as signed clauses. What they lose is being the only place a stamp can come from.
- **Universities keep degrees, laboratories, ethics boards and employment,** and their overhead, now a visible line.
- **Departments get back a choice they once had:** which work to hold. And they gain one: which work to vouch for.
- **Libraries keep holding the record and giving access,** without depending on a publisher's servers.
- **Funders keep their priorities and their panels.** Their priorities become clauses anyone can read beside the results they shaped.
- **Companies keep their secrets.** Private acts, periods of secrecy and patents remain; what is promised to become public, and when, is signed.
- **Senior researchers keep their standing.** A junior author's part is on the record beside theirs.

## 11. What MOR provides, and what it needs

**From the core:** identities and homes; routes, with an inbox and an outbox; relays run by identities; text works, work claims and their order; publications, references to acts and to web resources, and withdrawal; private acts in sealed containers, made public later by publishing a key; the anchoring task; agreements, deals that change only with every party's signature, keepers, time references and conditions; obligations, receipts and payers' claims; collectives with areas, lanes and a way to rotate; grants; stakes in existing works and in a collective itself, and stake transfers; split plans with named shares, and role shares on someone else's evidence; witness acts.

**Not everything above is settled in the core.** The points where its texts are silent or unclear are in section 14, and several of the pieces below wait on them.

**Open for others to build (cMIPs and Modules):**

- **A citation cMIP:** how a work marks a quote or a paraphrase, the passage concerned and its source, on MOR or on the web.
- **Writing-tool plug-ins:** attributing quotes and paraphrases as the author writes, in the editors researchers already use.
- **A scholarly text format:** formulae, figures, tables and reference lists, with the plain text always available.
- **Department and library relay toolkits:** acceptance by subject, mirroring, preservation, an inbox for submissions, an outbox for stamps.
- **Review cMIPs:** what a review names (the version, the matters judged), what a stamp names, how reviewers are assigned and conflicts of interest declared. Blind review is a choice among them, for example:
  - **signed review:** the reviewer's identity and record are visible;
  - **blind, revealed later:** the reviewer signs with a one-time identity, and may later confirm, by a link signed from both sides, that it was theirs;
  - **blind, never revealed:** the one-time identity stays alone, and its review weighs what a review with no visible record weighs, unless a way is found to show its owner's Trust Score without showing the owner;
  - **double-blind:** the author is hidden too, behind a commitment to the work made before review.
- **Trust Score modules by matter,** showing their counts, and the signed subject frameworks they need.
- **Version, correction and retraction cMIPs,** and their display in clients.
- **Priority evidence:** anchoring cMIPs, and a cMIP that reads the order citations give.
- **Funding cMIPs:** calls, proposals, panels, milestones, overhead lines, reports that point at receipts.
- **Contributor roles** in work claims.
- **Dataset and code formats,** and **replication reports.**
- **Embargo and timed disclosure modules.**
- **Matching modules:** finding passages that resemble earlier ones without naming them.
- **Bridges:** to existing identifiers for papers and researchers, and to existing preprint servers and repositories, so today's literature can be cited and today's authors linked.
- **Metrics modules:** counts and maps computed from lineage, each method visible.

## 12. Honest limits

Some things cannot be acquired right away by a new approach and a new tool. That is acceptable. What matters is to give the tool to those who value it.

- **Prestige is academia's currency, and MOR cannot mint it.** A department's stamp borrows standing the department already has; it creates none. A hiring committee that counts journal names will keep counting them until it chooses otherwise, which is why Noor's main paper still went to a journal.
- **A department stamping its own people proves little.** The record shows whose work a department stamps; it does not stop a circle of departments stamping each other's.
- **Money still bends research.** MOR puts the bend in a clause someone signed. It does not fund the clay soils or the cheap sensors.
- **Blind review and signed review pull against each other.** Signed review makes a reviewer answerable and exposes juniors who criticise seniors. Blind review protects them and hides their record. Each review cMIP chooses, and pays for its choice.
- **An uncited paraphrase stays invisible to MOR.** A trace exists only where the writer names the source. Matching modules can raise suspicion; they cannot prove where an idea came from.
- **Order is not truth, and not authorship.** A citation shows one work came after another. It does not show who was right, or who thought of it first in a notebook nobody anchored.
- **A Trust Score by matter rests on little data.** Most reviewers will have a handful of scored predictions per matter, and in slow fields the outcomes take decades. It can be gamed, and it can breed conformity.
- **Newcomers start with nothing.** Noor had no record; she borrowed a department's.
- **Hierarchy remains.** The principal investigator is still whoever the funder's rules say.
- **Most research earns no money.** Lineage pays where money flows, and most citations carry none.
- **Law comes first:** on copyright and patents, on data about people, on research ethics, on what public funders may require.
- **Adoption is slow.** Decades of literature live elsewhere and must be bridged, not replaced.

## 13. What it demonstrates

Knowledge and its recognition become granular. The maintaining and the tracking of knowledge gain new tools that open the door to new participants and ways of participating.

A six-page proposal by someone without a doctorate and a four-year programme with industry partners use the same rules. The three jobs a journal does can be done separately: any relay can carry, any identity with a record can stamp, and a name is whatever that record has earned. A department can take up the first two with the routes every identity already has. A sentence can be followed from the work that first carried it through every quote and every named paraphrase. A review carries its author's record on the matters reviewed, so an engineer with no academic post can be the reviewer whose word counts most on clay. And each flow of money can be read from its source to its use, with the reason it flows, and what it left unfunded, in terms someone signed. MOR does not replace the judgement of peers. It gives that judgement, and the work it judges, a record that belongs to the people who made it.

## 14. Open questions

*Where the core and the MIP drafts read are silent or unclear on something this case study uses. None is answered here.*

1. **Quoting a passage.** A reference (Envelope) names a whole act or a web address, never a passage inside a text. Where does "this passage, quoted from that passage" live: in a citation cMIP's own act beside the text, or inside a text format? Can anyone holding both check a quote against its source? The travelling sentence of section 4 rests on the answer.
2. **How a work names its sources and its earlier version.** The work claim's format (Law type 3) is still open, and work claims are a task handed to a cMIP. Is naming a source done by references, in the claim, or only by a lineage cMIP?
3. **Order from citations.** The roadmap files "knot evidence" as an idea above the core, for a cMIP that judges. Until one exists, "a citing work was made after what it cites" is true and checkable by hand, and carries no weight in any verdict. Is that the intended standing?
4. **A stamp as a collective's act.** A review cMIP fills no listed task. In a collective with areas, an act under a specification the collective has not adopted counts for nothing, so a department would adopt its review cMIP as an extension, or give stamps to an area naming that act type. Is that the intended path for a collective's acts under cMIPs that fill no task? And a stamp, like any act in a collective's name, is done only once sealed to every member or public: fine for a stamp, but a department's unpublished verdicts cannot be kept from its own members.
5. **Corrections and retractions shown.** The journalism case study proposed that a conforming client showing a work must also show its author's correction; the technical pass of 9 October lists it as undecided. This case study needs the same answer for retractions, and for a reader of a citing work whose source was later retracted.
6. **What a relay can prove it holds.** A relay may publish a signed commitment to the set of acts it holds; its construction is still open in Envelope. Can a reader check that one given work is in a department's commitment, or is "carried by this department" only ever a hint?
7. **A hidden reviewer's Trust Score.** A Trust Score is computed by a module from public records, and a Module signs nothing. For a reviewer behind a one-time identity, either a service signs "this identity's owner scores so on this matter", which is trust in that service, or a credential cMIP proves it without one. The identity proofs and credentials task exists; whether it can carry this is not said.
8. **Money to spend against money to share.** A stake in a collective is "a share of all that collective's income". An award paid to a collective is a budget, not income to divide. Does the core tell them apart? Here the question is avoided by paying the award to the university.
9. **A publisher's stake.** Law rule 16 leaves open how an identity is marked as a publisher, so that a verifier can tell a journal's publication stake from an owner's (F140).
