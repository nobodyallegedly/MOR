## Why this case study

In 1937 the economist Ronald Coase asked a simple question: if markets are so efficient, why do firms exist? His answer: because using a market has costs. Finding a partner, agreeing on terms and making sure the other side keeps its word all take time and money. When those costs are high, it is cheaper to bring everything inside one company.

The largest online marketplace is Coase's answer at planetary scale. It made finding, agreeing and trusting cheap for millions of independent sellers, warehouses and couriers, by bringing all of it inside one company. Then it owned the coordination. Sellers pay a fee on every sale, fees for storage and delivery, and increasingly for advertising just to be seen in search results. Their reviews, rankings and customers belong to the marketplace. The referee also sells its own products against the players.

This case study asks what happens when the costs Coase described fall without a firm: when finding is an open catalogue, agreeing is a standing offer, and trusting is a signed record and a portable reputation. It follows one maker from selling to her neighbours to taking part in an open marketplace with its own logistics network, organised by nobody in particular.

## 1. One maker

**Inès makes ceramics** in her workshop. She has an identity, like every creator in these case studies.

**A listing is a signed offer naming a vow.** A bowl on her shelf is something MOR cannot hash, so Inès signs a vow for it, in words ("this bowl, as photographed, shipped within a week"), and lists it as a standing offer naming that vow: its price and delivery terms. Photos, description, stock and variants are the listing cMIP's; the core counts nothing under an offer. The listing is published on relays, where any client can find it.

**An order is a sale of the vow.** A buyer accepts the offer and pays; the payment names the vow, and the sale is pending. Inès ships the bowl with a local courier, paid through the order's split with its own receipt; the courier checks its cut at its own flow address. When the bowl arrives, the buyer's client acknowledges the courier's last handover, and the sale is confirmed. A buyer who never receives it contests; nobody else can, and Inès vouching for her own delivery changes nothing.

**Made to order works the same way.** Inès signs a vow, "a set of six bowls, glazed to your colour, in six weeks"; the buyer pays the offer naming it, and the sale is pending until the bowls arrive and the buyer confirms. Selling what does not exist yet needs nothing more than selling what sits on the shelf.

**Reviews belong to Inès.** The buyer rates the purchase, and Inès rates the buyer. Each rating is a signed act tied to the order, under the rating cMIP from the gig economy case study. The ratings belong to her identity, not to any shop. Wherever she sells next, they come with her.

## 2. Limited editions and resale

**Inès makes a numbered series:** twenty vases, each signed 1/20 to 20/20. She signs one vow for the series, "twenty vases, numbered, fired this winter", its twenty promises listed in its words, and sells them before they are fired; each collector's sale is pending until the vase arrives and the collector confirms. Which number each collector got, and the signed record that travels with the object, are the provenance cMIP's: the core's claims cover works, and a claim on the vow's name covers nothing it became.

**Provenance is a chain of signed transfers.** When a collector resells vase 7/20, the transfer is a signed act of the provenance cMIP naming the previous one; the core's own transfers are of stakes, not of objects. Anyone can follow a vase's history back to Inès's workshop. A counterfeit has no chain.

**Resale can pay the maker.** The EU and the UK give artists a share when original works of art are resold through the art market; on MOR, any maker can write it into the terms of sale. When vase 7/20 changes hands, the resale is a new sale under the reseller's own vow and terms, and it pays Inès her share if those terms honour hers; if they do not, the reseller's published split shows exactly that. The core sees no further. The vase is what it cannot hash, so it cannot tell that the reseller's vow and Inès's describe one object: a resale under a fresh vow escapes anything the core could mark. Resale is carried by the provenance cMIP, with its registries, certificates and reputations, and the place to look is the fresh vow, signed by the reseller.

**This is where a provenance cMIP earns its place:** items of value whose proven origin moves the price. A vase with an unbroken record back to Inès's workshop sells for more than the same vase without one, so sellers have a reason to keep the record and buyers a reason to ask for it. A bowl for the kitchen needs none of this.

**A provenance cMIP is needed** to link a physical object to its record, for example:

- **a signed certificate** kept by each owner and handed over with the object;
- **a tag or code** in the object, linked to its record;
- **an authenticator's attestation** at each resale, signed.

**Authenticators examine the object.** A record says which vase was sold; only someone holding the vase can say that this is it. An authenticator examines the physical item and signs that it is the same item the vow and its earlier sales name: "yes, it is the same item". The examination is a service, sold like any other under a vow. The attestation is the authenticator's signed word, weighed by their reputation, not a proof the core checks; the provenance cMIP says how attestations are read. An authenticator who signs for a fake has signed it in their own name, and every later buyer can see who did.

## 3. Makers share a warehouse

**Inès joins five other makers** who share a warehouse run by a small logistics company. The warehouse is a service with its own identity and ratings.

**Storage and handling are visible.** Storage is a service, so the warehouse's standing offer names its vow, "storage per month, packing per order", and each maker's month is a sale of it, confirmed or contested by that maker. The warehouse's fees appear in each order's settlement, and the warehouse checks its cut at its own flow address. Makers compare warehouses by their real fees and their ratings.

**Stock is attested.** The warehouse signs regular statements of what it holds for each maker. A listing can show "in stock at a warehouse rated 4.8 by 300 makers", and the claim can be checked.

**Makers can leave.** If a warehouse raises its fees, the makers move their stock elsewhere. Their listings, ratings and customers stay theirs.

## 4. A marketplace without an owner

**The open catalogue.** Every listing from every maker, shop and brand is published on relays. No company owns the catalogue.

**Storefronts are clients.** A storefront is a client that shows part of the catalogue to buyers: a design shop showing handmade ceramics, a general store showing everything, a local shop showing what is available nearby. Each earns a visible role share in the sales it makes, like curators and publishers in the other case studies. [OPEN: F184]

**Search and ranking are modules.** How results are ordered is a ranking module, and storefronts compete on the quality of their ranking. **A ranking cMIP is needed**, for example:

- **by relevance and rating,** with no paid placement;
- **by distance,** favouring local makers and short deliveries;
- **with paid placement, clearly labelled:** sponsored results allowed, visible for what they are, as in the advertising case study.

**The referee cannot secretly play.** A storefront that sells its own products shows its identity on its own listings. Buyers see when the shop is ranking its own goods above others. And a storefront that offers a maker's claimed vow outside the maker's agreement is shown as outside it by every conforming client.

## 5. The logistics network

**Delivery is a chain of services.** A package from Inès to a buyer abroad may pass through the warehouse, a regional courier, a customs broker, an international carrier and a local courier. Each is an independent service with its own identity, fee and ratings: the gig economy case study at the scale of logistics.

**Each handover is signed.** When a package passes from one service to the next, both sign the handover. The order's record shows where the package is, who holds it, and who held it last. The last handover is what the buyer's client acknowledges to confirm the sale: the core needs no delivery rule of its own.

**Responsibility follows the handovers.** If a package arrives broken or never arrives, the signed chain shows where it was last seen intact. **Who answers for a failure is a question for a delivery cMIP**, for example:

- **the last signer:** whoever held the package when it failed answers for it;
- **the seller:** the seller answers to the buyer and settles with the chain afterwards;
- **insurance:** each order carries insurance from a named insurer, paid through the split, and the insurer settles.

**The route is disclosed.** Like payment routes in the Money MIP, each hop's fee is a line in the settlement. The buyer sees what delivery cost and who was paid; Inès sees which carriers are worth using.

**Returns and disputes** use the same tools: a return is a new agreement reversing the order, with the courier's fee named; a disagreement starts with the buyer's contest, which marks the sale contested and voids nothing, and goes to the dispute cMIP from the gig economy case study; what is owed back is what the order's terms say.

## 6. Coase, revisited

The three costs that make firms necessary each fall on MOR:

- **Finding:** an open catalogue, searchable by any client, ranked by competing modules.
- **Agreeing:** standing offers, accepted in a click, with terms legible before signing.
- **Trusting:** signed agreements, receipts, custodians for payment, attested stock, signed handovers, and reputations that belong to those who earned them.

**Firms do not disappear.** Where doing things inside one company is still cheaper, firms remain the better answer: a brand with its own factory, a carrier with its own fleet. What changes is that nobody needs to own the whole network to coordinate it. Coordination becomes a shared language, not a company.

## 7. What large retailers keep

- **Large retailers become participants.** A large retailer can run storefronts, warehouses and delivery fleets on MOR, and compete on what it does best: speed, scale, service. Its efficiency is real; on MOR it competes on it rather than on lock-in.
- **Brands sell direct** through any storefront, keeping their customers' relationship without renting it.
- **Carriers and warehouses** gain customers from the whole network, rated on their performance.
- **Paid placement is still allowed,** clearly labelled.

## 8. The network behind it

- **Makers, shops and brands:** listing products.
- **Storefronts:** showing the catalogue, earning role shares.
- **Ranking modules:** competing on search quality.
- **Warehouses, couriers, carriers and customs brokers:** earning through fees and ratings.
- **Insurers and custodians:** covering orders and holding payments.
- **Provenance services:** certificates, tags and registries.
- **Authenticators:** examining items of value at resale and signing that each is the same item, earning through fees and a reputation for being right.
- **Developers:** building listing tools, storefronts, ranking, tracking and logistics modules.

## 9. What MOR provides, and what it needs

**From the core, nothing new.** Standing offers, including offers naming a vow, and the state of each sale, agreements, payments and receipts, route disclosure, splits and role shares, signed attestations, stake transfers, keepers, time references and identities are already defined; custodians, and transfers of objects, are built from these core pieces by the cMIPs below.

**Open for others to build (cMIPs and Modules):**

- **Listing cMIPs:** products, stock, variants, delivery terms, and how a vow's promises are listed.
- **Rating cMIPs** for sellers, buyers, warehouses and couriers.
- **Ranking cMIPs and modules:** relevance, distance, labelled paid placement.
- **Provenance cMIPs:** certificates, tags, registries, how authenticators' attestations are read, resale shares.
- **Stock attestation cMIPs** for warehouses.
- **Delivery cMIPs:** signed handovers, responsibility for failures, insurance.
- **Returns and dispute cMIPs.**
- **Storefront clients,** from niche shops to general stores.

## 10. Honest limits

- **Physical goods can lie.** A signed listing can describe a fake; a signed handover can hide damage. Ratings, provenance and insurance reduce this; they do not end it.
- **The core cannot see the vase.** It cannot tell that two vows describe one object, so a reseller who signs their own vow for a vase escapes anything the core could mark. Certificates, registries, authenticators and reputation catch it, through the provenance cMIP, not the core; and an authenticator's attestation is a signed word, which can be wrong or bought, worth what the authenticator's reputation is worth.
- **Scale is real.** Large networks of warehouses and fleets deliver faster and cheaper than small ones. An open network must grow dense before it can match next-day delivery.
- **Returns cost money** and someone must bear it.
- **Tax and customs** differ by country, and a decentralised network must still comply with each one.
- **Consumer protection law** applies, whoever the seller is.
- **Open catalogues attract spam.** Ranking modules and ratings must keep it down.

## 11. What it demonstrates

A bowl sold to a neighbour and a vase resold across borders use the same rules. Finding, agreeing and trusting, the three costs that made the largest marketplaces necessary, can be carried by an open catalogue, standing offers and portable reputations. The network keeps its coordination; it loses its owner. Every maker keeps their customers and their ratings, every fee is a line in a settlement, and anyone who organises part of the network, a storefront, a warehouse, a carrier, an authenticator, earns by doing it well, not by holding the others in place.
