# MOR Case Study: Honey for Eggs

*Case study 22, the voucher market, draft 1, 12 November 2026. What MOR could be, with your help.*

*How to read this draft. The Agreements MIR is being rewritten from scratch, so this study is written from the MICS document and the plain purpose of each layer, and cites no Agreements rule. Wherever a passage leans on Agreements it carries the mark **[A]**; section 14 lists what each mark assumes, to be checked once the redraft lands. The study adds no rule to the core. Its idea is the author's, Nobody, allegedly, quoted where his words carry it; where his words in the working session of 12 November differ from the brief this draft was written from (on baskets, and on the split in two parts), the draft follows his words and section 15 says so.*

## Why this case study

> "Money is memory, a record of who owes what to whom."

The author's sentence has a long history in economics: in 1998 Narayana Kocherlakota showed, in a paper titled "Money is Memory", that in his models anything money achieves can also be achieved by a record of what everyone has done [1]. Money, on that reading, is what people use when they have no such record.

MOR is a record. So this study asks what happens when the record is good enough: can people trade goods for goods, track what things are worth from the trades themselves, extend each other credit on their reputation alone, and never need a currency to do it?

> "It's an absurd idea… it is intentionally silly. But, it is what the economy should be… so whatever ends up being designed in the back is the experiment."

It is told in two parts, as the author set them: "The first half is a practical bartering exchange with value tracking mechanism. The second extends it ridiculously large, really poking at what money truly is, or should be."

**Part one** looks like a silly swap market for honey and eggs, and could be built. **Part two** is a thought experiment, and is marked as one.

# Part one: a barter exchange that tracks value

Goods only. No services yet: a jar of honey is either received or not received, and that is the whole of what part one needs anyone to say.

## 1. The silly front

**Mirela keeps bees. Tobias keeps hens.** Each has an identity, like everyone in these case studies.

**An offer is a promise in words.** Honey is something MOR cannot hash, so Mirela signs a vow for it: "500 g of honey, handed over at the valley market, on Saturday". Tobias signs his: "20 eggs, handed over at the valley market, on Saturday". The vow is the core's; what honey is, how much 500 g is and where the valley market is belong to the database of goods (section 12), not to the core.

**A barter is two promises tied together.** Mirela and Tobias agree to swap: his vow for hers. No money moves and nothing is priced. The deal has two legs, and each leg has its own answer. **[A]**

**"Received" or "not received".** On Saturday Tobias takes the honey and his client signs "received" for Mirela's leg; Mirela takes the eggs and signs "received" for his. Only the one who was promised something can say whether it arrived; Mirela vouching for her own honey changes nothing. A leg nobody has answered stays pending. **[A]**

**Both sides are rated.** Tobias rates Mirela, and Mirela rates Tobias: each rating is a signed act pointing at the deal, under a rating cMIP, as in the gig economy and commerce case studies. The author's words: "Reputation runs both ways." The ratings belong to the people rated, not to any exchange.

**That is the whole front.** A swap, two answers, two ratings. Everything below is computed from it, which is why the shape of this deal comes before anything else on the list in section 12.

## 2. Value, not price

> "It's bartering with attempt at reading and computing data of settled deals to manage the market value of the various assets. If someone is willing to exchange x grams of honey for y grams of eggs it says something about the value of both. An exchange that tracks value, not price."

**A settled deal is a ratio.** Mirela's swap says: 500 g of honey went for 20 eggs, and both sides said "received". That is one fact about the value of honey and the value of eggs, signed by the two people who lived it.

**Only settled deals are data.** An offer is a wish, and a deal with one leg unanswered or contested is a story still open. A value reader counts a deal only when both legs are received.

**Aiko bakes.** She and Tobias swap 10 eggs for 1 kg of bread; both say "received". Now there are two ratios:

- 500 g of honey for 20 eggs;
- 10 eggs for 1 kg of bread.

**Nobody has traded honey for bread.** Yet eggs sit between them, a common knot, and a value reader can follow the thread: 500 g of honey is worth about 2 kg of bread. It is a reading, not a price: nobody offered it and nobody is bound by it. An honest reader gives it with what it rests on: *one path, two deals, both from this month.*

**Then the connection happens.** Mirela and Aiko swap for the first time: 500 g of honey for 1.8 kg of bread. The author's addition names what this allows:

> "connections that never existed can be measured against previous data on both using common knots."

The new deal lands 10% away from what the web said before either of them had met. Is that the reading's error, the cost of Aiko's better flour, or a bargain? The record does not say. It shows the distance, and the next deals move the reading.

**More deals, more threads.** With hundreds of people and dozens of goods, most pairs of goods have never been swapped directly, and most can still be read through several paths at once. The author's aim for the mechanism: it "uses the data generated by the network to adjust values across multiple goods", and "at high volume almost all of them".

**No yardstick.** Nothing in this web is the unit everything else is measured in. Mirela's client can show her everything in honey; Aiko's shows the same web in bread. The author's words: "I think baskets should not be the focus, not needing them is."

## 3. Two places, and loops that do not close

**The same good in two places is two goods.** In the valley, where the bees are, 500 g of honey goes for 20 eggs. In the port, a day's drive away, it goes for 35. "Where handed over" is part of what a good is, so the database of goods holds *honey, at the valley market* and *honey, at the port market* as two entries, and the web has a ratio between them like any other.

**Ngozi prices the gap.** She takes honey in the valley, carries it, and hands it over in the port. Her swaps are ordinary deals with ordinary answers. What she earns is the gap less the cost of carrying, and every deal she settles narrows it for the next person. Import and export need nothing special: people set up for it if they wish, and nobody has to match locally.

**Loops that do not close.** If honey to eggs, eggs to bread and bread back to honey returns more honey than it started with, someone can trade round the loop and keep the difference, and doing so pulls the three ratios into line. Nobody is appointed to do this. Whoever reads the web most carefully is paid for making it consistent.

**This is the architecture under test.** A silly honey market, once it has two places and a few dozen goods, already needs everything a serious market needs: many assets, each valued against all the others, the values moving as deals settle.

## 4. The promise that travels

**In March, Mirela has no honey.** She wants eggs now. She signs a vow for later: "500 g of honey, handed over at the valley market, by the end of June", and Tobias gives her 20 eggs for it today. His leg is received at once; hers stays pending until June. This deferred leg is the **voucher**. **[A]**

**Mirela has just been given credit.** No bank lent her anything. Tobias did, and what he looked at was her record. This is where credit begins, and it is the same mechanism as the Saturday swap with one date moved.

**The voucher changes hands.** Tobias wants bread more than he wants honey in June, so he passes the voucher to Aiko for bread. Aiko passes it to Ferran for a sack of flour. Each passing is a signed transfer naming the one before it, so the chain from Mirela to the present holder can be followed by anyone. **[A]**

**Ferran has never met Mirela.** Before taking the voucher he reads her **honouring record**, computed from her own past promises: 41 issued, 39 received on time, 2 received late, none refused, 3 still outstanding; an identity at least three years old (section 5). He takes it.

**In June, Mirela hands Ferran the honey,** and Ferran signs "received". One promise has paid for eggs, bread and flour on its way round, and ended as a jar of honey. Between March and June it did the work people usually ask money to do.

**An exit scam.** Dario trades olive oil. For a year he honours every small promise, thirty of them. Then in one week he issues two hundred vouchers for oil in autumn, takes goods for all of them, and is never heard from again.

**Who it lands on: whoever holds the vouchers at the end.** Everyone who passed one on was paid by the next person and is whole. The last holders signed "not received" in autumn and have nothing.

**What MOR does about it.** It does not get the goods back, and it does not judge. It keeps the record: two hundred promises under Dario's identity, each answered "not received" by its holder, for good. Dario can start again under a fresh identity, at zero, with no age (section 5).

**What could have been seen.** Thirty small promises honoured, then two hundred large ones in a week: the jump was on the record before anyone took a voucher. Whether a reader warns about it, and whether an exchange caps how much anyone may promise against their record, are open questions (section 11).

**Whether the loss travels back up the chain is a question for the voucher cMIP,** for example:

- **the last holder bears it:** as told above; simple, and it makes every holder read the record;
- **each passer answers for it:** whoever passed the voucher on stands behind it to the next holder, as endorsers of a bill of exchange once did; safer to hold, heavier to pass;
- **a pool answers:** holders pay a little into a mutual pool that covers broken promises, as in the gig economy case study.

## 5. Identity: why cycling is costly

MOR will not stop anyone from making a new identity. It makes the old one worth keeping.

**Age can be shown.** An identity is the hash of its genesis act. Anyone may anchor an act on a named clock, which proves the act existed by that point. Mirela anchored her genesis three years ago, so anyone can check that her identity is at least that old. An anchor made today cannot be dated to last year.

**An honest issuer keeps her record through a key change.** Mirela loses her phone. She rotates: a new signing key, the same identity. Promises and agreements bind identities, not keys, so her 41 promises and their answers are still hers, and Ferran's voucher is still good.

**A fresh identity starts at zero.** No age, no honoured promises, nobody who has ever said "received" to it. Dario's second identity can trade, but only on the terms a stranger gets: goods handed over on the spot, or small promises that few will take.

**Reading the record is made easy.** The author's words: "various ways to track the data and various ways to compute it." The data is the same for everyone; the next section is about the computing.

## 6. The record is one; the readings compete

**Everything above is computed from the same signed acts:** vows, deals, "received" and "not received", transfers, ratings, anchors. Nobody owns them, and no exchange holds them hostage.

**Reputation readers are Modules.** Each turns a person's record into something a stranger can use, for example:

- **plain:** promises honoured out of promises made;
- **weighted:** recent and large promises count more, and an identity's age counts;
- **relational:** an answer counts for more when it comes from someone with a long record of their own.

**Value readers are Modules too,** for example:

- **shortest thread:** the value of honey in bread through the fewest deals;
- **every thread:** all paths between two goods, newer and larger deals counting more;
- **who trades with whom:** as above, each deal weighed by how independent its two sides are (section 11, the hardest question).

**Each reading can be checked by recomputing it.** A reader publishes its rule and names the exact set of deals it read. Anyone holding the same acts runs the same rule and gets the same answer, or shows that the reader lied. Two honest readers may still differ: by rule, which is the competition, or by holding different deals, which is why each reading names its set.

**Each value comes with its footing:** how many paths it rests on, how many deals, and how old they are. "About 2 kg of bread, on one path of two deals" and "about 2 kg of bread, on forty paths this week" are different statements, and a client shows the difference.

**An exchange is a choice of readings, made public.** The author's words for it: "if you use this exchange you agree on the use of the following implementations". The valley's exchange publishes its terms: this database of goods, this deal shape, this voucher, these readers. A person accepts them by signing, once, and can leave for another exchange with the whole record. **[A]**

# Part two: the same thing, absurdly large

*A thought experiment. Nothing here has been tried, and section 10 says what nobody knows.*

## 7. Everything on the web

**Suppose it grew.** Not dozens of goods but all of them: grain, steel, cloth, fuel, in every place they are handed over. Suppose volume is high enough that almost every good is tied to almost every other by many recent paths.

**Then nothing needs to be the yardstick.** A steel mill reads the world in steel, a farmer in grain. Each reads the same web from where they stand. Promises of goods pass from hand to hand and are honoured or not, and every identity carries what it has honoured.

## 8. The grocer has to pay its staff

**Paulo works the till at a large grocer.** The grocer cannot hand him the right amounts of honey, eggs and bread each Friday. So how is Paulo paid? At this point, and only here, something like a basket appears: not as the measure of value, which the web already is, but as a way to pay people.

**The grocer has more than one way to do it,** for example:

- **its own promise:** a voucher for a named bundle of goods from its shelves. Paulo redeems it there or passes it on; it is worth what the grocer's honouring record is worth;
- **a list, not a promise:** the wage is stated as a bundle of goods, and paid in whatever promises the grocer holds that Paulo's own reader values the same;
- **no bundle at all:** Paulo names what he wants, and the grocer pays in that.

Which of these a grocer would choose, and which its staff would accept, the study leaves open.

**Labour enters here.** Paulo's work is a promise too: "eight hours at the till, on Friday". With wages in the web, an hour of work is tied to bread and honey like everything else. Services are harder than goods: whether a jar arrived is one question, whether a day's work was done well is many. Part one avoided them for that reason.

## 9. Some baskets pass from hand to hand

**If large players pay in bundles, some bundles circulate.** The author: "if big players start paying in baskets, some baskets start to be treated like currency, or at least we assume so." A grocer's voucher that half a town is paid in becomes something the baker takes without thinking, because she can always spend it.

**It is an assumption, and one outcome among several.** Asked whether baskets were the destination, the author answered: "It's one of the ways it can end up being run."

**Where two baskets circulate, the web prices each in the other,** like any two goods. Someone who notices that one grocer's bundle buys more in the port than the other's will trade the gap, as Ngozi did with honey. A basket that circulates is still a promise from a named issuer, with an honouring record anyone can read, and it can fail the way Dario's oil did, at a larger size.

**None of them becomes the money.** In one town a grocer's basket passes like currency. In another, people settle in promises of grain. In a third, nobody uses anything but the web. All three trade with each other through the same record.

## 10. What it would be

> "The result of the second half is a moneyless commodities (and services) exchange without a single way of computing trust or even value."

**No single way does not mean no way.** It means many: many readers of trust and many readers of value, side by side, each checkable from the same record, none official.

**An economy becomes a choice.** An economy, here, is the set of people who accepted the same terms: the same goods database, the same deal shape, the same readers (section 6). Nobody has to belong to only one. The author's ending:

> "a person can chose to belong to the economies (no single one) where the vouchers it can produce himself are the most valued"

A beekeeper joins the exchanges where honey is wanted. A cashier joins the ones where an hour at the till buys the most bread. Leaving costs nothing that was earned: the record goes along.

**What this says about money.** Economists give money three jobs. Here they come apart:

- **the measure of value** is the web of settled deals, read from wherever one stands;
- **the means of exchange** is a promise of goods, taken on its issuer's record;
- **the store of value** is the weak one. A promise of honey by June is no way to save for old age: goods spoil, promises expire, issuers die. What holds value across decades in such an economy is not answered here.

**What nobody knows:**

- whether a web of ratios stays readable at that size, or whether people need one number so badly that a yardstick returns by habit;
- whether credit on reputation alone survives a bad year, when many honest issuers fail at once;
- whether the fake-trade problem (section 11) can be held down when the prize is the value of everything;
- whether anyone would accept wages this way, and whether the law would let them (section 11).

# The hard parts, the list, and the checks

## 11. Hard parts, as open questions for builders

None of these is solved above. Each is a question for whoever builds.

- **Whose word counts on "received" and "shipped"?** The sender says shipped; the receiver says nothing arrived. MOR records, it does not judge: both statements stay on the record, signed, and so does any carrier's handover. What a reader makes of a contested leg, and who if anyone is asked to decide, is a cMIP's question. A value reader can leave contested deals out; a reputation reader cannot ignore them.
- **A voucher's shared shape.** Vouchers can price each other only if they say the same four things in the same way: what, how much, where handed over, by when. Too loose, and "honey" hides ten different goods; too strict, and nobody's honey fits.
- **Thin markets at the start.** With few deals, honey read through eggs and honey read through bread disagree, and one odd swap moves everything. A reading on one path must not look like a reading on forty. How an exchange starts before its web means anything is unanswered.
- **Credit limits, or none.** Nothing above stops anyone from promising more than they can ever deliver. A limit could be set by each exchange's terms, computed by a reader from the record, or left to each holder's judgement. Every limit also shuts out an honest newcomer.
- **Where a broken promise's refund waits.** When a voucher is not honoured, what is owed, to whom, and in what: the eggs Tobias gave in March, their value by some reader on some date, or nothing but the mark on the record? And where does that debt sit while it is unpaid?
- **Fake trades: the hardest open question.** One person behind two identities swaps one gram of honey for a thousand eggs, both say "received", and the web now holds a settled deal at an absurd ratio. Counting deals is not enough: a value reader must weigh who trades with whom. Candidate defences, each with a cost: discount deals between identities with no independent history (and punish real newcomers); discount closed circles (and punish real villages); require a third party's handover signature (and add cost to every swap); trim outliers (and miss the patient attacker who moves the ratio slowly). Nobody has shown one that holds.
- **Aged identities can be prepared.** Age shown by an anchor is a floor, and someone can create a hundred identities today to use in three years, or sell an old one with its keys. Age raises the cost of cycling; it does not end it.
- **A reading is only as complete as the acts its reader holds.** The core decides alike on the same evidence and does not promise the same evidence. A reader that never received Dario's refusals computes a clean record for him, honestly.
- **Money, securities, wages and tax law.** In some places a transferable promise that circulates may fall under rules on money, payment services or securities. Paying wages in vouchers is restricted in many countries: the ILO's Protection of Wages Convention, 1949 (No. 95), Article 3, prohibits paying money wages in "promissory notes, vouchers or coupons" [8]. Tax and customs do not vanish with money, and usually ask for a value in the national currency. This is a note for builders to check where they build, never a jurisdiction in the core.

## 12. What MOR provides, and what it needs

**From the core, nothing new is asked.** Identities, rotation and what it keeps, anchoring, vows and the state of each sale, agreements between identities, signed references, obligations and their release, time references, and specifications whose rules anyone can run are in the MICS. Whether they are enough for a deal with no payment in it is not certain: section 14 lists what must be checked.

**Open for others to build (cMIPs and Modules).** Each line: what it accepts, what it produces. Items 1 to 3 were named by the author, items 4 to 10 by the Dubsar Suite lead; the rest came up in the writing.

1. **The exchange made public (cMIP).** Accepts: a named set of specifications (goods database, deal, voucher, readers) and a person's signature. Produces: a signed acceptance, so every deal on that exchange is read under terms both sides chose.
2. **The database of goods (cMIP and Modules).** Accepts: a description of a good (what, how much, where handed over, by when). Produces: an entry others can name, so two vows for the same good can be recognised as the same good.
3. **Reputation, both ways (cMIP and Modules).** Accepts: a settled or broken deal and a rating from each side. Produces: signed ratings on each person's record, and readings of them, as in the gig economy and commerce case studies.
4. **The barter deal (cMIP).** Accepts: two vows and both parties' signatures, with who hands over first. Produces: one deal with two legs, each answered "received" or "not received", partial delivery included. Every value is computed from it, so its shape comes first.
5. **The voucher (cMIP).** Accepts: a deferred leg ("honey, by the end of June"). Produces: a promise that is issued, transferred and redeemed, with its chain of holders and its final answer; where credit begins.
6. **Value readers (Modules).** Accept: a named set of settled deals. Produce: a value for any good in any other, read through common knots, each given with how many paths it rests on and how old they are.
7. **Baskets (cMIP).** Accepts: a list of goods and weights, signed by its maker, signed again when it changes. Produces: a bundle that can be promised, compared and traded like any good; one way an economy may run, never the measure of the others.
8. **The defence against fake trades (Modules).** Accept: settled deals and the history of who traded with whom. Produce: a weight for each deal, published with its rule. The hardest open question; see section 11.
9. **Quality and grading (cMIP and Modules).** Accepts: a good and a grader's signed examination. Produces: a grade in the database of goods (which honey), with independent graders as a trade of their own.
10. **Handover and transport (cMIP).** Accepts: a good passing between two identities at a place. Produces: a handover signed by both, linking each leg to the commerce case study's logistics network.
11. **Honouring-record readers (Modules).** Accept: every promise an identity issued and its answer. Produce: what a stranger reads before taking a voucher, age and outstanding promises included.
12. **Credit limits (cMIP).** Accepts: an issuer's record and an exchange's terms. Produces: how much that issuer may have outstanding there, or a plain statement that there is no limit.
13. **Broken promises (cMIP).** Accepts: a voucher answered "not received". Produces: what is owed, to which holder, and where the debt stands until it is met or released.
14. **Contested legs (cMIP).** Accepts: a leg where the two sides disagree, with any carrier's handover. Produces: the disagreement shown as one record, and how readers are told to count it.
15. **Exchange clients.** Accept: all of the above. Produce: the silly front: a swap, two answers, two ratings.

## 13. Prior art: what this takes, and where it differs

Checked on 12 November 2026 from abstracts, catalogue records and secondary descriptions; the full texts were not read in this session, and each claim is kept to what those support.

- **WIR (Switzerland, since 1934).** A cooperative of small and medium businesses that extend each other credit in a unit of their own, used beside the Swiss franc [2]. James Stodder found its use rises in recessions, when ordinary money is short [2]. *Takes:* businesses can clear trade among themselves without bank money. *Differs:* WIR has one issuer, a bank, and its unit is reckoned against the franc; here there is no issuer and no unit.
- **Sardex (Sardinia, since about 2010).** A mutual credit circuit of businesses, with no interest, its unit counted as one euro and not exchangeable for euros [3]. Its founders and researchers report that trust was central to making it work [3]; a study of its transactions found money moving in closed cycles between businesses [4]. *Takes:* credit between businesses without a bank, resting on trust, as the author says. *Differs:* Sardex still needs a yardstick, the euro, and a company that admits members and sets their credit; here the measure is read from the deals and the record is open.
- **The Credit Commons Protocol** (Matthew Slater and Tim Jenkin, 2016). A way for separate mutual credit groups to keep accounts with each other, federated as a tree, each pair trading at a rate they agree [5]. *Takes:* many economies, none on top. *Differs:* its rates between groups are negotiated and kept stable by design; here ratios are read from settled deals and move with them.
- **Commitment pooling** (Will Ruddick, Grassroots Economics, 2023). Vouchers as formal commitments to redeem goods or services, gathered in pools where they are exchanged against each other [6]. The nearest relative: the voucher of section 4 is this. *Differs:* as described, a pool is curated, its accepted vouchers and their relative values set by those who run it; here nobody sets a value, and the hope is that readings of open deals can do that work. That hope is exactly what is untested.
- **Keynes's Bancor** (the International Clearing Union, proposed 1941 to 1943). A unit for clearing trade between nations, with charges on countries that ran large balances in either direction, creditors as well as debtors [7]. It was not adopted. *Takes:* the idea that a persistent surplus is as much a problem as a persistent debt, which bears on credit limits. *Differs:* bancor was one unit, issued by one institution, for central banks only.

## 14. Where this study leans on Agreements

To be checked once the redrafted Agreements MIR lands. Each **[A]** above assumes one of these.

- **A deal between two people with no payment in it** (sections 1 and 4). The study assumes two identities can agree to swap one vow for another, and that such an agreement stands without any money moving.
- **Each leg answered by the one it was promised to** (section 1). The study assumes the pending, confirmed and contested states of a vow's sale apply to each leg of a swap, and that only the person promised something may answer for it.
- **A promise passing to a new holder** (section 4). The study assumes the right to receive, and to answer "received" or "not received", can pass by signed transfer to someone who never dealt with the issuer.
- **A deadline** (section 4). "By the end of June" assumes a time reference the deal names, since MOR has no clock.
- **Terms accepted by signing once** (section 6). The study assumes a person can accept an exchange's public terms by one signature, and that later deals can name those terms.
- **What a broken promise leaves owed** (sections 4 and 11). The study assumes nothing here, and asks.

## 16. What it demonstrates

A jar of honey swapped for twenty eggs, answered "received" twice, is enough to start from. From deals like it, and nothing else, a value can be read between goods nobody has traded, a stranger can decide whether to take a promise, and a beekeeper can be given credit in March on what she did last year. Stretched as far as it will go, the same record suggests an economy with no currency in it and no single way of saying what things are worth or who can be trusted: many readings of one record, and each person free to stand where what they make is valued most. Whether that holds is the experiment. The front is silly on purpose; what must be built behind it is not.
