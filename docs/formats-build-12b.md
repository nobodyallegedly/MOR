## In plain words

**Selling.** A standing offer now has an exact shape: what it sells (a publication, or access to something like a live stream), its price, and who is paid. When co-owners sell together, the offer names their agreement, every party of a deal signs it, and the money goes either to a payee their split service handles or, with no service, straight to each owner by their shares. Nobody can slip in an offer that pays only themselves and still looks like the group's. A person selling alone needs no agreement: their offer *is* the agreement with the buyer, with its own deadline and refund terms if they want them. An offer changes by new versions on its own little chain, and one that sells nothing withdraws it. Because an offer now says what it sells, a relay that served the song can finally be paid on a sale made through an offer.

**Refunds.** An agreement (or a lone seller's offer) may set a point in time after which a refund is no longer claimable. Past it, the refund is over: the owners keep the money, and it no longer stops a collective from closing. Terms set in bad faith are not refused; they stay public.

**Dividing.** The split plan now has an exact shape. A helper's share is named by its role ("relay"), never by the hash of one transport specification, so a new transport needs no new agreement. A share nobody fills goes back to the owners unless the plan says otherwise. Fees are their own field: a collective can give the power over fees to one person and the power over the plan to another, and nobody holding the plan can add a fee alone. A fee says when it applies; a split service that leaves out a fee it owes is in the wrong, and the fee becomes its debt. Someone counting plays for a metric can never be paid by their own count. Late evidence gets no machinery: the buyer's app asks the relay for its record before paying and confirms it when it pays.

**Owning.** A work claim says who made a work. Where no agreement says who owns what, the creator who opened the claim is shown as the owner, "as recorded". A stake's sale is signed on the seller's own unbreakable line, and a seller who keeps cashing payouts for a stake he sold signs receipts that count for nothing; the buyer is owed that money by the service. A publication can carry two agreements: the work's owners, as one participant, and the publisher; a sale through the publication pays the publisher's share and passes the owners' line on to them.

**Living together.** A resignation lists the drafts its signer leaves behind, so none of them can pull her back in. A notice before closing can reach a payer who used only a one-time key, by being published; after its deadline the debt no longer blocks the closing. A request to a judge, and a request to settle a deal's fork, count only if the judge can actually see them. A request to a judge and a sign of presence (liveness) now have formats.

**Retired, never reused:** the delivery confirmation (the seller's receipt does that job), the import (adopting an act is done by citing, acknowledging or paying on it), and the Module fee-terms act (a Module states its fee in its own specification). The concurrency rule field is withdrawn.

## Questions for Nobody, allegedly

One at a time. Each is a place where a mechanic could not respect a decided rule without changing what another one does.

1. **QJ1. A receipt "after" a transfer on the seller's own line** (F217 beside Identity). *Laid beside it, F217:* "a payout receipt he signs for that stake after it on his own line is a wrong receipt"; "the second-device case is answered by the chain signature; the build checks it and brings back anything that does not hold." **It does not hold as F217 imagined:** the chain signature is on the seller's identity chain, but Identity binds an everyday act (a receipt) only to genesis or a rotation, never to a chain signature; the test shows a receipt bound to the chain signature is invalid. So a receipt can be placed after the transfer only once it is bound to a rotation after it. *As built:* that. The story: Ben signs the transfer by chain signature on Monday and keeps receipting Carla's share for months without rotating; none of his receipts is "after", and the window F217 called legible stays open until his next rotation. Options: (a) Identity lets an everyday act's binding name a chain signature, so a seller's client binds new acts to its latest chain act (an Identity change; a stale device binding to the older rotation stays "before", which F217 accepted); (b) as built, "after" means bound to a later rotation, with that cost stated; (c) the transfer takes effect for receipts from the first split citing it (split key 8), which F217 refused as the moment.
2. **QJ2. How a split service pays the work's agreement its line** (F216's mechanic, not settled). Payer-side splitting passes outside in as built. With split services, the publication's split pays the work's agreement's line to one of its payees (its field 14), whose service then splits it again under the work's agreement, the receipt naming the first split. As built, `split` checks a payout on the publication's stake against holders by identity, so a payout to the work's agreement's payee shows as a mismatch. Options: (a) the payout's receiver is a payee of the work's agreement, the work's service's receipt naming the publication's split (Money's routes, a receipt naming the previous one); (b) the payout's receiver is the work's agreement's hash itself, paid to its pointer; (c) leave the publication's agreement to payer-side splitting only until step 13.
3. **QJ3. OF1's `[1, work]`, an offer selling any publication carrying a work, against two agreements** (F216's mechanic). Under F216 a publication's agreement pays the publisher; a work-wide offer under the work's agreement would sell the publisher's publication around the publication's agreement, and a stranger's republication with it. *As built:* `[1, work]` refused as unsupported (fail closed); offers sell publications or access. Options: (a) keep it out; (b) allow it, selling only publications whose agreement is the work's own (none carrying a publication's agreement); (c) allow it as proposed, the cost stated.

## Readings taken, to confirm

- **R1.** A payment following an offer that does not count (in a deal, one party signed alone; a payee field 14 does not list; not public) is no purchase, owed back by its payee, though it names the claim: the reading that closes review 2.2's hole in the purchase. A collective's offer is the exception: whether it is the collective's own act is its consent's, shown beside, and a purchase under it is settled by the collective's chain recording the sale (W2), as before.
- **R2.** A purchase following co-owners' offer names a version of the agreement the offer is made under (field 0's lineage); naming another agreement holding a stake in the work is no purchase under that offer (it would be one under its own offer, T-1).
- **R3.** "An act of any cMIP whose type definition names that role" (review 7.2) is read as client conformance, not a core check: QG3 has every verifier give the same answer whatever cMIP defines the act, and only a verifier holding the specification could read its type's name. The payer's acknowledgement is the gate (F184, F193).
- **R4.** Standing for a request to a judge is read, as built, as a party who signed the version; rule 57a's stake holders, keepers and arbitrators are not yet counted.
- **R5.** F219 ends a refund only on the time reference's answer, which the verifier states (`refunds_past_terms`), as for notices and judges; the library tracks money owed back as debts only where it did before (a broken stretch's sales), so F219 reaches closings there.
- **R6.** F214's scope "payments for a named publication" covers a payment following an offer that sells it.
- **R7.** In a deal, an offer's later version stays under the agreement its first version names.
