## The grammar as a whole, in plain words

**The central idea: an agreement can be a party.** People sign an agreement. That agreement can then stand as one party in another agreement, as a person would. Being named asks nothing of it: it need not sign to be pointed at, and what reaches it is passed on by its own terms. Where it does sign, it signs as its own terms require: a small work by all its owners, a collective by a vote or a named delegate, as its rule says. A delegate who signs in the agreement's name, under a power its own terms gave, makes the agreement's signature, and that binds its parties as its own terms say. Signing is one level: an agreement signs through its own signers, and never needs the signatures of the people inside the agreements it names, unless its terms say otherwise. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "The key concept is « agreement as a party » then it’s what you build with it that changes". Everything else in this text is there so that this can be read safely by a stranger. What people build with it is theirs:

- a **work**: an agreement among those who made something, saying who owns it and in what shares;
- a **service**: someone who does a job for a work (dividing its money, holding a payment in escrow) under a **deal**, which is an agreement between the service and the work as a party, signed by the service and by the work, as the work's own terms require; the work names that deal as its receiver;
- a **delegation**: an agent acting for an identity, or for an agreement as a party, which are the same thing in two flavours;
- a work that stands on other works names them as parties, so that one split pays into the next: a **sequence** of agreements. Each thing owed and carried out on the way, between whoever carries it out and whoever receives it, is a **leg**;
- a **collective**: nothing separate. "A collective is simply an agreement that decided to define many aspects of how it can govern itself", and "all agreements are collectives if there are shares, whether loose or hyper defined". An agreement with shares defines as much or as little of its own governance as it chooses, and the grammar has no other notion of a group.

People agree in order to defend themselves against the other party's future self, and their own. Honest drift and lying look the same from outside, so every written agreement is written for a third person who may turn up later: a newcomer, an heir, a judge, a buyer. The least two people need to write down is exactly the least that third person needs to see.

**Agreements does not rule.** It is a grammar: the words people use to write down their own rules, and the few ways those words must be read so that a stranger reads them the same way. What people agree (a price, a vote, a split, a deadline) is theirs, written in cMIPs they choose. The grammar only makes sure that what they wrote can be checked by anyone, later.

Most of what an agreement needs is already provided by the layers around Agreements: words that cannot shift (State), a mark that binds someone (Sign), getting the words to someone (Deliver), placing them before or after something else (Order), and power over a thing (Control). Agreements does not redefine those. It adds only what those five cannot give on their own, and every addition here must answer two questions:

- **Which lie does it expose that signed, ordered text cannot already expose?**
- **Whom does it protect who did not choose it?**

One more test stands over the whole text, in the words of Nobody, allegedly: "Building blocks over solutions." In plain words: the grammar gives blocks (an act, a party, a spot, an update condition, a contest, a name by fingerprint), it uses one block that belongs to all of MOR (the report), and people build their solutions from them, in cMIPs. Where this text would itself solve something a cMIP could build from those blocks, it gives the block and stops.

One more sentence of his says what the grammar is for, and what it is not for: "What the previous version of agreement focused on solving issues… this one supports and specifies the language for creating solutions". A problem found is the grammar's only where the solution cannot be said in the language. Where it can, building it is a cMIP's. Draft 15 asked him about two things a review had called solutions: the contest and the condition. Both stay, as blocks (draft 16): the contest because "Making greed legible does not work without contest, it makes it core" (G11), and the condition because "triggered or not is what the core cares about" (G10). He has since said what the condition is (draft 17): "Condition is short for update condition", and "it is wether it is valid or not valid". So the block is the update condition, and the grammar reads of it whether a proposed update is valid or not valid (G4).

Those tests leave fourteen grammar rules for a verifier. Everything else an agreement needs is handed to cMIPs as thirteen tasks, each asking one question. What fits neither is written down as a cost, openly, instead of being patched with another rule.

In one sentence: **Agreements fixes what was agreed and who is bound by it, and says honestly what it does not know; everything about the world, about time and about value is somebody's signed word, reported as such.**

**Four words that are kept apart.** A **chain** is the versions of one agreement, each replacing the one before: one agreement's own history. A **line** is any acts that each point at the one before, such as one person's acts in an agreement. A **sequence** is several agreements linked to one another, what one owes reaching the next: a payment passing on. A **succession** is the agreements by which a baked work's rights pass from holder to holder, forward in time.

The way out is the reason for G5: nothing ties a person except their own signature, and nothing changes an agreement except what its own signers agreed in advance. An agreement never ends; it only gets a new version, signed as its own Terms require. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "Terms rule, because they were agreed by all signers". The cost is equally plain, and is written in section 7: an agreement that names no way to change it can change only when everyone signs again.

And the same reason keeps the shower free. ("The shower" is the model in which each work pays, by choice, the works it stands on.) In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "Mor won’t force the shower, it will sustain it". No grammar rule makes a work pay the works it stands on. G1 makes it possible (a work may name the works it stands on as parties), G11 lets each leg of the climb be read by its own two sides wherever a report was called for and given, and lets the whole be read only along paths its people signed for, G8 makes it visible, and the choice stays with those who sign.

## G1. An agreement as a party

**Plain words.** An agreement can name another agreement (a song, a library, a drawing, anything with its own agreement) as one of its parties. It need not sign to be named, and no group has to be formed. The grammar has no notion of a collective apart from this: an agreement with shares is all a collective is, and it defines as much or as little of how it governs itself as it chooses. Works, services and their deals are built in the same way.

An agreement named as a party can also sign, as a person would. Then it signs as its own Terms require: a small work by all its owners, a collective by a vote or a named delegate, as its rule says. Nobody outside it signs in its place.

How it signs is one act. Someone signs in the agreement's name, and says under which of the agreement's own Terms they do so. If those Terms gave them that power, the act is the agreement's signature. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "I sign this agreement y under agreement x, parties are at agreement x, I sign on their behalf." The delegate's signature is then the signature of everyone in x, and it binds them as far as x's own Terms say, and no further.

And signing is one level. An agreement signs through its own signers: the people its own Terms name. Where one of its parties is itself an agreement (a film that names a song, a song that names a band), the people inside that agreement are not asked. So a film's deal with its service never needs a signature from every member of every band of every song the film uses. An agreement's own Terms may say otherwise.

An agreement names who receives for it: its **receiver**. That may be an identity (one of its own parties) or another agreement, named by its fingerprint. There is one mechanism, not two. Whatever is owed to an agreement goes to its receiver. Where the receiver is itself an agreement, a reader looks at that agreement's receiver in turn, until it reaches a person: that person is who receives. What that person then passes on, by their own agreement's terms, is another leg, and the grammar does not follow it.

Paying through several splits is the first thing people will build with this. A work says who its owners are and what share each has. A service that divides money makes a deal with the work, and the work names that deal as its receiver: its own deal with the service, never the service itself, nor an offer the service makes to everyone. That deal is signed on both sides: by the service, and by the work, as the work's own Terms require. The payer pays the work and needs to know nothing else. An owner that is itself a work is paid in the same way, and so one split pays into the next. All of that is built, by cMIPs: where the work is paid, what the service charges, how it divides, what happens to a late payment. The grammar gives only the blocks under it: the receiver that may be an agreement (this grammar rule), the payment that names the version it read (G13), the report a leg may call for (G11), and who said each thing (G8).

**Lie exposed.** "I receive for that work", said by someone who once did and no longer does, or never did; and "that work was paid", when it was paid to a past receiver. Signed text in the paying agreement cannot say where another agreement is paid; only that agreement's own Terms can, and G1 sends the verifier there and labels each answer with where it came from. And (G1.2) "the work agreed", said of a signing that the work's own Terms did not let those who signed make for it.

**Protects.** The people of the work pointed at, who did not sign the pointing agreement and need not: they are not bound by it (G1.2), and what it owes their work reaches them by their own agreement's words, which no payer can change. Also the one who pays, who needs to know one thing, the work, and nothing of who its owners are. And the people of an agreement that does sign: it signs only as their own Terms require, so no one of them, and no service, signs for all of them. And the people inside the agreements an agreement names: its signing is not theirs, and asks nothing of them.

**Reasoning.** Asked whether a party can be another agreement, Nobody, allegedly: "I think it is essential… it ensures a series of split is possible.", and: "The new agreement points to the other agreement as one party, no collective involved". So an agreement can be named as a party without being a group that must sign, and where it has not signed it is not bound. Its words are pinned (its root is a fingerprint), and what it says is read from its own versions, which is why G2, G7 and G13 together suffice. Without a receiver, something could only be "owed to an agreement", which is not a person, and the step from there to a person was made outside any grammar rule; the receiver is that step, inside the grammar.

On whether a split needs a field of its own beside the receiver: "I mean, receiver still exists, it happen to also accept agreements hashes as input, no?" So there is one field, which takes an identity or an agreement's root (G1.3). On what a work names for a split: "Almost, the target is not the split service but the agreement with the split service, not some kind of public offer." That is why the plain words above say a work names its own deal. What makes an agreement such a deal, what a verifier reads from it, what shows that a work was paid, and how a pay-out is matched against the shares, were written into this grammar rule in earlier drafts; they are a solution built on the blocks, and are now the cMIP of the service (task K8; section 5). The grammar keeps the blocks.

On collectives: "A collective is an agreement, more than an ID, we gave it ID capabilities (keys) and then removed them. So, agreements as parties is nothing new"; and of the whole: "The key concept is « agreement as a party » then it’s what you build with it that changes". So this grammar rule is named for the idea, not for one use of it, and the grammar defines no collective: governance is whatever powers an agreement's own Terms give (G5).

*Where it signs.* Earlier drafts went further, and said that an agreement named as a party never assents or signs. That was more than his words. Of the counter signature he had said: "Which for an agreement as party means, all signature or whatever satisfies the collective agreement". The MOR lead put the consequence to him: under that grammar rule, in a deal with a service, only the service could make a call. He described the deal instead: "So, I am assuming everyone on the work signed an agreement that pointed to a receiver. So, the agreement with the service is signed by all, the report about the payment only comes from the receiver." And then, of the sentence "an agreement as a party never signs": "Also, if you keep that quote a collective becomes nearly impossible to build." So G1.2 no longer says it. An agreement signs as its own Terms require. "A small work by all its owners" is his "signed by all"; "a collective by a vote or a named delegate, as its rule says" is the MOR lead's wording for his "whatever satisfies the collective agreement". The exchange is about a deal. It does not say that every agreement named as a party must sign: "no collective involved" still holds for a work that is only pointed at. So the default of G4.1 is left as it was, and what else is not said is listed in P17.

*An agreement's signature (draft 15).* Fable's review of draft 14 found the central block half built: the text said an agreement can sign, and not by which act, and `for` held a person. The MOR lead had put the case to Nobody, allegedly: a collective whose delegate signed with a service that pays the members. He first found the gap real ("The signature is only by a delegate, but that is handled by agreements of more complex nature. If I were them, I’d use a data cMIP to bridge the gaps."), and then: "The delegate signs for the agreement, isn’t that a collective signature of sorts? I sign this agreement y under agreement x, parties are at agreement x, I sign on their behalf." Asked whether the gap is closed: "I think it is. And if it isn’t a cMIP closes it." So G1.2 says it once: `for` may name an agreement, `under` names the Terms of that agreement that give the power, and the act is the agreement's signature. Nothing new binds anyone: G5.1(b) already said that a person is bound through Terms they signed that give a power, and it now reads `for` as naming a person or an agreement those Terms belong to. "As x's Terms say" is the MOR lead's wording. The exchange is about a delegate; an agreement with no power still signs by all its signers, and by which acts is still not said (P17).

*One level.* A test built on this text (`docs/click-sketch-12-report.md`, branch `claude/click-sketch`, its 2b) showed that "by all its owners" could be read two ways. Read through every agreement a work names, a film's deal with its service would need a signature from every member of every band of every song the film uses, and a band member would be a signer of the film's deal. Read one level, each work signs through its own signers only. The MOR lead settled it, as technical wording (recorded with the exchange "The payer's seat"): signing is one level, an agreement signing through its own signers unless its Terms say otherwise. So a party further up never needs the signatures of the people inside the agreements below it. This is the MOR lead's wording, not the words of Nobody, allegedly. It also bears on who sees what: the people inside an agreement that a leg names did not sign that leg's Terms, and are not, by being inside it, a side of that leg (G11.6).

## G2. Whole

**Plain words.** Everything the agreement uses is pinned by fingerprint. A link to something that could change is not part of the agreement.

**Lie exposed.** "The document we referred to said something else", and "there was a side piece". A signature over words that point at something changeable proves only that the pointer was signed. (#34 Whole, #1 Fix.)

**Protects.** Every reader who arrives later: newcomer, heir, buyer of a part, judge. Also the party who signed without fetching what was referred to. None of them chose how the reference was written.

**Reasoning.** A cMIP can be revised; a document can be edited; a name can come to mean something new. Naming cMIPs by fingerprint is what lets an agreement be read correctly after its cMIP's author is gone, which is the good-ancestor requirement applied to meaning. G2 does not prove that nothing else exists outside the Terms; that is G7. An agreement named as a party is named by its root, which is a fingerprint; what it says *now* is not pinned by G2, and is read by G1.

## G3. Author

**Plain words.** The agreement says whose words these are. If the words turn out to be unclear, the unclearness falls on them.

**Lie exposed.** "We wrote this together" or "I meant something else by that word", said by the one who did write it. Signatures alone cannot show who wrote the words: every party signs the same Terms.

**Protects.** The party who accepted words they did not write, above all whoever takes up an open offer or fills a spot (G10), who never had a chance to change the words.

**Reasoning.** Whether two people meant the same thing is an inner state at a past moment; no operation reaches it (`agreement-grammar.md`: Mind). The research's answer is "do not try to prove; assign in advance who bears the failure", and the open offer shows the assignment: whoever writes the words carries their ambiguity. G3 makes the assignment readable; what a judge does with it is the judge's task. It assigns Align only. Able (#3) and Free (#5) are not assigned by any rule; see section 7.

## G4. In force

**Plain words.** Signing first does not bind you alone. Your assent counts only once all the assents the agreement asks for are present.

Terms stop being in force when Terms that replace them are in force. New Terms replace old ones only if they carry the signatures the old Terms require: everyone's, unless the old Terms gave a power to fewer. New Terms without those signatures replace nothing, and the old Terms simply go on. If the agreement names a place, the place says which Terms are the latest; if it names none, a verifier can say only which are latest among the acts it holds, "as shown". If two Terms acts each replace the same Terms and each carries those signatures, that is a fork (G12): a verifier shows both, and never picks one. While the fork stands, neither branch is in force: the Terms both replaced stay in force, and everything that rests on the Terms in force (who receives now, who fills a spot, how a rule divides) is answered from them, until the fork is resolved (draft 16: both tips stop, and nothing built on either counts meanwhile, G12).

An agreement may also say, in advance, which updates it accepts (draft 17). It names one cMIP for that, in one simple field: its **update condition**, or condition for short. The grammar asks that cMIP one question about a proposed new version: can the agreement be updated like this, or not? The cMIP answers valid or not valid. Everything else is the cMIP's: which parts may change, when, and on what. It works from the bytes it is given, the version in force and the proposed one, so anyone who holds them can run it, and nobody has to publish anything for it to answer. New Terms that the condition answers not valid replace nothing, whoever signed them. Who must sign is a separate question, answered as before (G5). And nothing changes by itself: there is no condition that goes off on its own.

An agreement never ends. It only gets a new version: one with new conditions replaces the last one as any version does, carrying the signatures the last one requires. What happens to the work and its shares is in the new version's conditions; the grammar does not list the ways, and has no word for ending. Until such a version is in force, the last version stays in force and keeps protecting everyone in it: whoever acts against it alone, without the signatures it requires, has made nothing that replaces it.

Nothing counts until every party has signed. Until then an assent is no more than an offer held out to the others, and whoever gave it may take it back, by a **counter signature**: a signature that cancels the party's own earlier one. It is available only until every party has signed, and not after; from then on only a new version changes anything. Where one of the parties is itself an agreement, "signed", for that party, is whatever its own Terms require: all its signatures, or whatever satisfies its own rule.

**Lie exposed.** "You signed, so you are bound", said by the party who never signed. Signed text shows that one side signed; it cannot by itself show that the signature was conditional on the other's. And (G4.5 to G4.7) "these Terms still hold", said of Terms already replaced, and "these are the Terms now", said of one of two replacements while the other is hidden. Each Terms act is signed and looks valid on its own; only the chain of replacements, read per place or as shown, says which stopped, and two branches of it convict each other as a fork does. And (G4.8) "these are our new Terms", said by some parties over the heads of others whose signatures the old Terms required. And (G4.9) "this agreement is over", said by one party to escape what its last version still says, and "I can do this alone now", said against a version that requires others' signatures. And (G4.10) "you signed, so you are held to it", said to a signer who took their assent back before the others had signed; and "I had cancelled my signature", said of Terms that were already in force. And (G4.11, draft 17) "enough of us signed, so it is changed", said of a change the agreement's own update condition does not accept.

**Protects.** The first to sign, who did not choose to be bound alone, and who is not left holding an open offer against themselves for as long as the others choose to wait (G4.10). And whoever reads the agreement later and is shown an old version, or one branch of two: the payer looking for the receiver for the act, the newcomer, the judge. And (G4.7, G4.8) the party left out of a replacement they never signed, and everyone who would otherwise be stuck with no Terms in force while a fork stands. And (G4.9) everyone in the last version, which keeps binding until those whose signatures it requires agree a new one. And (G4.11, draft 17) whoever relies on a part of an agreement that its update condition lets no update touch: the owner whose share two others could otherwise rewrite.

**Reasoning.** "Each party must hold the other's commitment, not their own" (#7 Exchange). Exchange may run one way until the other side acts, which is why G4.2 requires Terms with a spot to say how acceptance is recognised instead of falling back to a default that cannot apply to a role.

*When Terms stop.* A rule that says when Terms come into force and nothing about when they stop gives two answers to everything that rests on "Terms in force". Nobody, allegedly, on old Terms stopping: "that’s where a whole lot of special cases were born if I remember right", and, asked whether Terms stop being in force when Terms replacing them are in force, two competing replacements being a fork that is shown and never chosen: "Yes, less likely to bloat and overdefine." So G4.5 to G4.7 are one grammar rule, read three ways: what stops (G4.5), where the answer comes from (G4.6), and what two answers mean (G4.7). With G4.3, an assent to replaced Terms stops binding its signer once the replacing Terms are in force; the signer is then bound by the replacing Terms only through G5.2.

*Who can replace.* On a case of two replacements each signed by two of three parties: "Both term 2a and 2b are invalid as they do not carry the required signatures that were in term 1." So G4.8: a replacement replaces only with the signatures the replaced Terms require; where those are everyone's, two replacements signed by different two-thirds are not a fork at all, and the first Terms stay in force. His word for them is "invalid". Draft 15 draws one line between invalid and not shown (section 2 of the draft): where a missing assent may still exist, the answer is **not shown**. So G4.8 now answers not shown for such a replacement. The effect is his: it replaces nothing. Whether the answer's name should be his word is listed for him (the draft's section 8.2). Asked whether, for a real fork (the Terms let fewer than all replace them, or the same people sign two replacements), the Terms both branches replaced stay in force until it is settled: "It does, and more sense than anyone ending up stuck in the first one". So G4.7 keeps the fork shown and unchosen, and answers from the Terms both branches replaced. How a fork is resolved is G12, for every kind of act.

*Never ended.* Asked whether ending an agreement is a replacement by Terms that say "ended": "An agreement never ends, it can resolve. It’s a new version of the agreement with new conditions signed by all signers of previous version"; and, asked whether that needs everyone who signed the previous version even where the Terms let fewer change them: "Terms rule, because they were agreed by all signers". So ending needs no act, field or answer of its own: it is a replacement whose conditions say what becomes of the work and its shares, signed as G4.8 already says. What G4.9 adds is that nothing else stops Terms: there is no "ended" for a verifier to compute. His example shows why: owners who all agree to make a work public and free, and one of them then selling it alone; the version that made it free is still in force, and "the previous agreement says « sorry can’t do this alone, the other two signatures are needed »". ("Resolve" was his word of the moment and is not a term of this text for anything an agreement does. From draft 16 the word is used of forks only, where it is also his: G12.)

*Counter signature.* Asked whether an assent may be taken back until the Terms are in force: "Yes, a counter signature is a good idea. Nothing counts until all parties have signed,". Asked what he meant by it: "Yes, I meant it as a signature that cancels the previous one, this becomes unavailable once all parties have signed. Which for an agreement as party means, all signature or whatever satisfies the collective agreement". So the counter signature is the party's own signature, cancelling its own earlier one, and it is a second defined kind of Statement. "Once all parties have signed" is read as "once the Terms are in force": by default that is every party named as a signer (G4.1), and where the Terms carry their own `in_force` rule (as every agreement with a spot must, G4.2), it is what that rule asks (section 8.4). His second sentence is the first place where he says that an agreement as a party signs. Earlier drafts kept beside it a grammar rule that such a party never does; that grammar rule is dropped, on his later words (G1, reasoning). G4.10 says how "signed" is read for such a party where Terms ask for it. What is still not said is in P17. Withdrawing is still a race against the last assent (section 7).

The grammar reads the counter signature itself, instead of leaving it to a cMIP, because Terms with no `in_force` rule name no cMIP that could.

*The update condition (draft 17).* Draft 16 had two things that each answered a question about a change to an agreement. The rule of a power for replacement was given the version in force and the proposed one, and answered allowed or not (G5.2). And a version could name a condition, which its cMIP answered as triggered or not (G10.7). The MOR lead asked Nobody, allegedly, whether the condition should have a task of its own. He: "Condition is short for update condition". The MOR lead read that as an update signed in advance that triggers by itself. He: "No, it is wether it is valid or not valid." The MOR lead then put it together: the condition is what an update must meet to count; given the version in force and a proposed update, it answers valid or not valid; it is the same block as the power's rule; rights stop or an offer closes only because a valid update says so; "triggered" and "no longer applies" go. Asked whether that is it: "Yes, and the cMIPs replies."

So there is one block, written in one place: G4.11, beside the clause that says when new Terms replace old ones (G4.8). The place is Claude's choice. The condition is about updating an agreement, and G4 is where updating is read; G10 is about spots, and G5 about who is bound.

Two more answers of his shaped the wording. The MOR lead said that an offer closing on a date would then need someone to publish the closing update. He: "No, the cMIP can run the verification via bytes, so it should be able to run." So G4.11 says that the cMIP runs from the bytes, and that its answer waits for no one to publish anything. The MOR lead then widened the condition to any act that would change who or what applies, a new claim included. He: "It answers wether it can be updated or not. Because agreements make these conditions potentially complex the core still needs a simple field the cMIP can then specify in details." So the condition is asked of updates and of nothing else; the core keeps one simple field; and the cMIP that field names carries all the detail. Both of those readings of the MOR lead's were withdrawn by him, and are not used.

What is left of each. Of the power's rule: who may sign (G5, below). Of G10.7: that a filling stays a filling, and that a later version may offer again (G10, below). The words "triggered" and "no longer applies" are no longer answers. "Triggered" was his own word, in the earlier exchange ("triggered or not is what the core cares about"); read as one train of thought, the later answer refines the earlier one: what the core cares about is whether an update is valid or not valid.

Three things here are Claude's least readings, where the exchange is silent, and they are listed for him (the draft's section 8.2). An update that every party signed still needs the condition's answer: "It answers wether it can be updated or not" is read as it stands. The cMIP may read the acts held and the clock, as any rule may, so that a condition can say "not before June". And the condition is not asked of a resolving update, which carries no words of its own (G12). His sentence about an offer closing on a date is the hardest to place: this text reads it as being about how the cMIP runs, and leaves whether a spot still takes new people where draft 16 had it, with the spot's own cMIP (G10).

## G5. Own signature

**Plain words.** Nobody is bound by anything they did not sign. The only exception is an agreement they did sign that gives someone a power over them, and then the act must name that agreement at the time it is made, not afterwards.

A power to change the agreement says who may sign the change. Whether the change itself is one the agreement accepts is another question, and the agreement's update condition answers it (G4; draft 17).

A grant of authority is a delegation: someone lets an agent act for them. The one who delegates may be a person, or a business, a collective, which is an agreement: then there is no "you" any more, there is the agreement as a party, and an agent acting for it is the same delegation in another flavour. Whoever delegates can end the delegation, at any time: that is the default. The agreement may add signed conditions to that (a period of notice, a fixed term, a cause), and then it ends as they say. The grammar does not defend the agent's job: pay, notice and severance are for cMIPs the parties choose. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "we’re not Law."

The grammar puts no limit on what such a signed condition may say. Even a delegation that only the agent may end is not "never": nobody knows whether the agent will ever use that freedom, so the honest answer is "unknown". And it is legible: the condition stands in the signed Terms, for anyone to read before relying on the delegation.

**Lie exposed.** "She bound you", "the group decided", "I was acting for him" (said after the fact), "I was still authorised". And (G5.5) "you cannot take your authority back without me", said by an agent whose delegation states no such condition; "this can never be taken back", said of a delegation whose signed condition leaves ending to the agent; and "I can still end it alone", said by a delegator who signed that only the agent may. A signature shows who signed; it cannot show that the signer could bind someone else. The pointer in `under` lets anyone check the power against the principal's own signature. (#27 Authorise, #28 Scope, #29 Name, #30 Decide, #14 Admit, #15 Bind, #47 Amend, #48 End.)

**Protects.** The person bound through someone else: the principal, the outvoted member, the absent party. Also the counterparty of an agent, who can check the power before relying on it.

**Reasoning.** "A group deciding and a person acting for another are the same: a few bind others, within limits. Amending an agreement is one more decision of that kind" (`agreement-tasks.md`, what collapsed). So one grammar rule covers agency, groups, judges, admission and amendment. A grant of authority is itself Terms: the principal is author and party, the agent is party, and the power names the kinds of act and the rule that limits them. Revoking it is a new version of those Terms (G4.9).

With no power named, a new version needs every party, which would let an agent block their own dismissal. On what a grant is, Nobody, allegedly: "Wait… act as you… but as a business… a collective… therefore an agreement, there is no « you » anymore, there is agreement as a party… an agent acts for an ID different flavor of the same delegation". So the grant is a delegation, and its delegator may be an agreement as well as an identity; nothing separate is written for a business. Asked whether the core guarantees only that a delegator can always take its delegation back, or a grant can be made irrevocable by its own terms: "I lean a. The default is delegator can end it, but with provision that an agreement can add conditions to its ability to do so." So G5.5 is a default, not a guarantee beyond the reach of what was signed: the delegator ends it alone unless signed conditions say how. And on protecting the agent: "Yes, we’re not Law. So, core defending employment rights is not a good idea. But, a cMIP should be able to do so if the parties need it." G5.5 is written only for a delegation by one delegator to an agent. A power shared among several (a vote, a judge's decision) is not read as a delegation each of them can end alone (G4.8).

Asked whether a condition amounting to "never" (a delegation that only the agent may end) is the one limit on conditions: "Dunno if the agent will ever act on its freedom… so it’s a I don.t know, not a never". The case was put to him as the one possible limit, and he did not take it as one. So G5.5 writes no limit and adds no check: a verifier reads such a condition as it reads any other. What it must not do is turn "unknown" into "never". What the delegator gives up by signing it is a stated cost (section 7).

The default is in the grammar, although a delegation's own Terms could give the same power to the delegator alone, because it is his stated default and it is the way out for whoever delegates.

A newcomer is bound (#15 Bind) by their own assent to the Terms in force when they join; their assent names exactly that Terms act, so they are not bound by a version they never saw, except through powers in it they accepted. Filling a spot (G10) does not bind anyone either: the signer who fills it is bound only by their own assent, under G5.1.

There is no default power. An agreement that names no power to replace itself can be replaced (G4.9) only by every party's own assent. This is the conservative way out, and its cost is in section 7.

*What a power's rule is asked (draft 15).* Fable's example: three owners with shares of 40, 30 and 30, Terms that say "two of us can change these Terms" and nothing on shares; two of them rewrite the third's share to 1. Nobody, allegedly: "« Two of us can change these terms » so the cMiP builds a 2 out of 3 signature scheme.  Verifier accepts or declines based on the cMIP. « These terms » that’s what needs to be defined… the whole agreement? Part of it (specific tasks) the cMIP can build the solution that is needed." And: "Exactly, the core just want to get the answer, allowed to edit this task or not." So the grammar protects no part of an agreement by itself, and is not asked to. It asks the power's rule one question about one change, and reads the answer. For the rule to be able to say "this part, yes; the shares, no", it must be given both versions: the one in force and the one proposed. That is the one sentence added to G5.2. Which parts a rule lets through is its cMIP's.

*What is left of it (draft 17).* That question, about the change itself, is now the update condition's, and is asked in one place (G4.11): "Condition is short for update condition", and it answers "wether it is valid or not valid". The update condition is given the same two versions the power's rule was given. What the power's rule keeps is the other half of his sentence: "a 2 out of 3 signature scheme", which is who may sign. In Fable's example, two of the three owners may still sign an update. Whether an update may touch the third owner's share is what the update condition answers, if their Terms name one; if they name none, the answer is as before: they did not use the right cMIP. That who may sign stays with powers, and only the answer about the update moves, is the brief's reading of "the same block"; the exchange does not draw that line itself (the draft's section 8.2, item 12).

## G6. Own line

**Plain words.** Each person's acts in one agreement are a single line: each one points at that person's previous one. If two acts point at the same previous one, that person has told two stories, and that is proven without a judge.

**Lie exposed.** Showing one history to one person and another history to another (equivocation), and hiding an act in the middle of one's own line. Without a single shared order, two signed acts can each look like the latest; the pointer turns the contradiction into proof (`agreement-grammar.md`, second test: "a fork convicts itself").

**Protects.** The stranger who is shown only one branch, and any party who has no place to look.

**Reasoning.** G6 is checkable by anyone holding the two acts, with no outsider. It does not stop the lie, it makes it provable afterwards, which deters only where the liar is known and has something to lose (section 7). What a proven fork costs its signer is their standing with whoever sees it, which anyone may compute (task K13). It cannot reveal that a signer's line has a later act that was not shown; only G7 answers "nothing later". What a fork removes, while it stands, was open, and draft 15 proposed a reading: that both branches go on binding their signer. That proposal is not taken (draft 16). His answer, given from MOR's side: both tips stop, and nothing built on either counts until the fork is resolved (G12). What that leaves open for one signer's own line is in P9. How a fork is resolved is G12.

## G7. Place to look

**Plain words.** An agreement may name one place where its acts are lodged in order. Then the questions "is this the latest?", "is this all of it?" and "who holds this now?" have answers, and anything not lodged there does not count. Without a place, nobody can prove that nothing else exists, and a verifier must say so. The place an agreement names is the one everyone uses for that agreement, and so is the clock it names. Only where it names none may a reader accept an anchoring of its own choosing for those questions; the answer then says which.

**Lie exposed.** "There is nothing later", "there is no side piece", "nobody else holds it", "you were told" (by publication), "the deadline has passed". A document cannot prove an absence; only a single place to look can (`agreement-tasks.md`, what collapsed: Current, Whole, Alive). (#33 Current, #34 Whole, #11 Title, #12 Sum, #13 Transfer, #32 Notify, #36 Wait.)

**Protects.** Everyone who arrives later and must rely on "nothing else exists": the newcomer, the buyer of a part, the heir, the judge, the counterparty of an agent whose delegation may have been replaced.

**Reasoning.** This is the closed-world rule ("what is not in the register does not count"), which the research calls a design decision, not a discovery. It is made local: each agreement names its own place, so "a thing's ownership is exactly as global as its witness set". G7 is a MAY because a bilateral agreement about deeds barely needs a shared order; the research finds that one is needed for exactly two things, scarcity and freshness. G7.3 is the price of not naming one, stated to the reader instead of hidden. Changing the place is a replacement of Terms and goes through G5. G1 asks of another agreement which of its versions is in force; that answer is per *that* agreement's place, or, where it names none, per the anchoring the reader accepts (G13.4).

On whether a reader may set aside the place or the clock an agreement names, Nobody, allegedly: "Whatever the agreement states as reference clock is the clock everyone has to use for that agreement." So G7.4: the place and the clock an agreement names are everyone's for that agreement, and a reader's free choice of anchoring is what is left where it names none (G13.4). His sentence names the clock; the place was in the question it answers, and the two are read together.

## G8. Say who said it

**Plain words.** Whether a thing was delivered, whether it was what was promised, how much there was, whether someone is alive, what time it was, whether money moved, who filled a spot, what a leg passed on, how often something was seen: an agreement can record who said so, never make it so. A verifier always reports who said it. Where an act is tied to a payment, it may name the settlement: the Money layer's own proof, plugged in here and reported on the word of whoever gave it.

**Lie exposed.** A claim laundered into a fact: "it was delivered" when only the deliverer said so; a backdated act; "you were paid" when only the payer says so; "I am the one who did it" when only the claimant says so. Signed text proves that the signer said it, and nothing more; this grammar rule stops a verifier's answer from saying more than that.

**Protects.** The receiver, who did not choose to be declared paid or served, and every reader who would otherwise take a verifier's summary as fact.

**Reasoning.** The research's largest residue is truth: whenever an agreement touches the world, "someone must say what happened, and their signature proves only that they said it". And "the record cannot hold value": every entry about value is a claim or an instruction, neither is the value. G8 does not rank sources; whether a receiver's acknowledgment (#20) or three witnesses suffice is a rule (task K4) chosen in the Terms. On the settlement, Nobody, allegedly: "The settlement proof is provided by the Money layer but needs to plug somewhere no?" The field is that somewhere, and the grammar says nothing of what must be done with it: a rule may ask for it, as in his words "Show me referees that lead to settlement only" (he meant "referrers"), or not, since "Advertisement industry can still build a model that still rewards pure attention, if they wish."

## G9. Unchecked is not passed

**Plain words.** If a verifier lacks something it needs to check (a cMIP it does not have, a place it cannot reach, the acts of an agreement named as a party), it says "unchecked". It never says "fine" or "broken" instead.

**Lie exposed.** "My software says it checks out", from software that could not check. Signed text cannot expose what a verifier silently skipped.

**Protects.** Anyone relying on someone else's verification, and the parties of an agreement whose cMIP has become rare.

**Reasoning.** Because every agreement can name any cMIP, verifiers will always meet ones they do not have. Without G9, the convenient path is to treat the unknown as passing. With it, an agreement written today stays honestly readable when its cMIPs are old. G9 alone would leave a whole sequence unchecked wherever one agreement's cMIP is missing; G11 is what keeps the sequence readable there, where that agreement's Terms called for reports and were answered, for a reader who holds those reports (G11.6).

## G10. Spots

**Plain words.** Some participants are not known when the agreement is written. The agreement keeps a spot for each of them, and says what earns it: having done something, having received something from the one before, holding something, or being someone who fits a description. Whoever proves it fills the spot, once for each proof: the same spot can be earned again by a later proof, and each filling is tied to the proof that earned it. Whether a spot holds one person or many is for the spot's cMIP to say. For those nobody could foresee in any of these four ways, a fifth, open kind of spot lets a cMIP say both who the participant is and how they are confirmed. The grammar does not say how anything is proven: the cMIP the spot names does. The grammar names no use of a spot. It only says that the spot, and its cMIP, are fixed in advance, and that each filling is reported with its proof and whoever confirmed it.

An open offer does not end. It can stop taking new people: by a new version, or by what its version says in advance ("open until June", "first 100 buyers"). Whether a spot still takes new people is for its cMIP to say, from the version in force. Whatever it says, everyone who already filled the spot keeps their filling, so that they can still point to a valid agreement that says so.

Whoever earned a filling has the rights the agreement offered when they earned it: exactly those, with whatever conditions that version put on them (a time limit, a creator's statement, anything its cMIPs read). The grammar does not care what the conditions are. No later version changes them unless the holder signs it, or unless the version they earned under said, through its update condition, that such an update is valid (draft 17).

Nothing stops by itself (draft 17). Earlier drafts let a version name a condition under which what applied no longer applied, and draft 16 had a verifier say of it that it had "triggered". That is gone. The condition a version names is its update condition (G4): it says which updates are valid. So rights stop applying, or what the agreement offers changes, only when an update is made and the condition answers that it is valid. A verifier no longer says "triggered" or "no longer applies". It shows the filling, the version it was earned under, and any later version that validly changed its rights. What that change means for a licence is in the versions' own words, which the grammar does not read. That is not the same as an act that never counted, for which "invalid" is the word. Whether the holder is still a party depends on how things are set up: the versions say, and the grammar defines nothing more. And nothing stops the agreement from going on afterwards: a new version can offer again, the offer renewed, and it can plug in values already in the chain (the earlier payment, the earlier proof) or not.

**Lie exposed.** "I am the one who did it", "it was passed to me", "I hold it", "I am one of them", said after the fact, with a confirmer chosen after the fact. Without a spot fixed in advance, the one who pays decides afterwards who earned it, and the claimant cannot check that the test was the one promised (`late-roles.md`: "The payer as sole confirmer appears in the affiliate and bounty cases"). With it, the test is pinned by fingerprint before anyone claims, and the answer always says on whose word the spot was filled. And (G10.5) "the offer was withdrawn, so your filling is gone", said to someone who earned the spot while it was open. And (G10.6, G10.7) "the terms have changed, so what you bought is gone", said by an author over a later version the holder never signed; and "that licence never counted", said of one that counted; or "your licence has lapsed", said where no update was made that the condition of the holder's own version answers valid (draft 17).

**Protects.** Whoever earns a spot and was not there to sign: the referrer, the heir, the holder, the later contributor, the beneficiary. Also every other party, who agreed in advance to the test a stranger must pass. And (G10.5) whoever filled a spot before it stopped taking new people, who keeps a valid agreement to point to. And (G10.6, G10.7) whoever paid for or earned rights, who keeps exactly what was offered when they earned it; and the later reader, who is told whether a filling never counted, which version it was earned under, and which later update, if any, validly changed its rights (draft 17).

**Reasoning.** On participants who arrive later, Nobody, allegedly: "some participants are not tied to a specific ID when the agreement is drafted, they are tied to a role that will be linked to an ID in the future." And the four kinds are his: "Prove that you have done X and earn spot 1. Prove you have received Y and earn spot 2. Prove that you are holding Z and earn spot 3 (relays for example) Prove that your ID fits the conditions in W and earn spot 4". `late-roles.md` finds that about eighteen recorded roles link to a person in only four ways: "by **doing** something, by **receiving** from the previous holder, by **holding** an object, or by **being** someone who fits a description"; his four spots name the same four. The kinds are in the grammar, although the fifth could carry them all, because a kind says what sort of answer a verifier is reporting (a deed, a handing on, a holding, an identity) and so whom it rests on; *how* each is proven is the K12 cMIP's question. The fifth kind exists because the research shows roles no agreement anticipated (the unknowing source, the rescuer, the owed but unfindable, the contributor recognised afterwards, the early user), where "someone with judgment decides afterwards". His words for it: "Maybe a customizable “future participants” with the cMIP specifying how that participant is defined and confirmed". A future spot lets an agreement name that judgment in advance, by its cMIP.

Asked whether a spot is earned per proof, not once: "Yes, it leverages the above discussion as intended I think". So G10.2 ties each filling to its proof, and the same spot can be earned again by a later proof without a new grammar rule. Asked where holding stops and being seen starts: "I don’t think it should be defined, what matters is “HOLD” with cMIP defined what the proof is, no?" So no use of a kind is named here.

On withdrawing an open offer: "The offer does not end, it is simply not open to new IDs anymore. Whoever bought the rights should still be able to point to a valid agreement that says so". The second sentence is what the grammar keeps (G10.5, last part; G10.6). How an offer stops taking new identities, or takes them again (a mark in a new version, a date, a count of buyers), is something a spot's cMIP can compute from the version in force, so the grammar writes no act and no field for it (section 5).

Asked whether an offer's author could sign that earlier licences no longer count: "Whoever paid the agreement has the rights the agreement offer." Asked whether that version can itself limit the rights: "That version could have conditions tied to time, creators statements… bla bla bla… core does not care". So G10.6 says only whose offer a filling holds (its own version's), and reads none of the conditions. He then asked that the agreement be able to give a verifier what it needs to answer that rights have stopped; G10.7 gives it that say, through one rule named in advance. Asked directly what stopping changes: "The question depends on how things are setup. Void simply means, watever applied before this does not apply anymore… it is updating the agreement within its conditions. Nothing stops the agreement to be updated again. So, offering again, and plug in values already in the chain (or not) is an option". Then: "So techincally void is the wrong term… updated within its specified conditions (some updates stop the offer, some don’t)". Read as a whole: there is no state to be in or to leave. A condition the version signed in advance has been met, and what applied before no longer applies. So there is one notion, a condition, which may or may not stop the offer, and G10.7 has one field for it. Drafts 9 to 14 said "in force" and "no longer in force, by its own condition", the MOR lead's wording, chosen for him: "void" is not a term, and neither is "updated", since nothing in MOR is updated, only published or signed again. Draft 15 keeps "in force" for Terms alone, so that the words have one test, and says of a filling that it **applies** or **no longer applies**, which is nearer his own "does not apply anymore". Coming back is not leaving a state: it is the agreement offered again.

*The condition is a task (draft 16).* Fable's review of draft 14 called G10.7 a solution: G10.6 says the grammar does not read rights, and G10.7 then gave a verdict on them, the answer "no longer applies". Draft 15 put the question to Nobody, allegedly. The MOR lead leaned that the `condition` field stays as a socket, and that whether it is met is answered by the cMIP it names. He: "Condition is the right task, triggered or not is what the core cares about. How it triggers is cMIP". So G10.7 now reads one thing: whether the condition a version names has triggered or not, as its cMIP answers. How it triggers is the cMIP's, in his words. What its triggering stops was already the condition's own to say in draft 15; the brief for draft 16 gives that to the cMIP as well, and the grammar no longer reads it.

The brief asked that the answer **no longer applies** be kept only if it is the grammar's way of saying "triggered". It is not. "No longer applies" was said of a filling's rights: it told the reader what the condition had stopped. "Triggered" is said of the condition, and stops there. So the answer is now **triggered**, and "no longer applies" goes back to being plain words, near his own "does not apply anymore". The verdict on rights that the review objected to is gone with it. This is Claude's choice, listed for him (the draft's section 8.2, item 1), with what it costs: a reader who lacks the cMIP learns that the condition triggered, and not what that means.

"Condition is the right task" is not read as adding a task to the table. The condition is still a cMIP that answers K4 (Rule), and K4's row says that for a condition its answer is triggered or not. Whether he meant a task with a number of its own is listed too (item 2).

*The update condition (draft 17).* The passages above record how G10.7 stood up to draft 16; "item 1" and "item 2" in them are items of draft 16's section 8.2. The MOR lead put the question of a task to him again. His answers are quoted under G4: "Condition is short for update condition"; "No, it is wether it is valid or not valid."; and, to the MOR lead's summary that rights stop or an offer closes only because a valid update says so, and that "triggered" and "no longer applies" go: "Yes, and the cMIPs replies." His earlier words, quoted above, already said it this way: "it is updating the agreement within its conditions. Nothing stops the agreement to be updated again", and "updated within its specified conditions (some updates stop the offer, some don’t)". The conditions are conditions on updates.

So the block moved to G4.11, and G10.7 says only what is left. Nothing triggers. A filling stays a filling. A later version may offer again.

For a filling's rights, G10.6 needed one more line. Until draft 16, a later version could not touch what someone had earned without their signature, except through the condition their own version named. That door is kept, in the new words: a later version changes a filling's rights without the holder's signature only where the update condition of the version the filling was earned under answers that later version valid. The first half is the summary he said yes to. That the condition asked is the holder's own version's is draft 16's guard, carried over. It is Claude's reading, listed for him with its cost (the draft's section 8.2, items 3 and 4): what a holder keeps is as safe as the update condition of the version they earned under.

Whether a spot still takes new people is not covered by the exchange. It is left with the spot's own cMIP, as draft 16 had it, less its reading of triggering (G10.5). So "open until June" still closes the offer in June, as that cMIP reads the version in force. The MOR lead's reading that this would need someone to publish a closing update was answered "No, the cMIP can run the verification via bytes, so it should be able to run", and withdrawn. Which cMIP he meant is not said, and is listed (the draft's section 8.3, item 1).

## G11. Legs of a sequence

**Plain words.** One click may have to climb: a payment reaches a work, part of it goes on to the works that work stands on, and part of that goes on again. The agreements on the way are a **sequence**.

A **leg** is one thing owed and carried out, between two sides: whoever carries it out, and whoever receives it. A buyer paying a work is a leg, between the buyer and whoever receives for the work. A service dividing that payment among five owners is five legs, each between the service and one owner. A band member who receives for the band and passes the money on to three bandmates is three more. The grammar says who the two sides of a leg are, and nothing more about it.

Each leg is carried out under one agreement's Terms, and those Terms choose their own cMIPs: one work may use one split service, the next another, and nothing has to be chosen for the whole climb. That freedom has a price. A reader who lacks the cMIP of one agreement could not read what was done under it, and so could not tell whether the click reached the people beyond.

What lets a leg be read is a block that belongs to all of MOR, not to Agreements: **the report**. In short (this is a summary; the block is the shared language's): some work is worth hearing back from, and most is not. So any task may carry a **call** for a report, or not. The call says from whom a report is expected: the one who did the work, the one who received it, or both. It never says where, and never names a reader. The where is implied by the leg: a report goes to the other side of its own leg, where that side receives. Only those who signed the act that holds the task can make the call, and it binds nobody else. A **report** is a signed act that answers a call. Whatever the task, it carries three things: the call it answers, the acts it relied on, and who signs it. Each side signs only its own half, and an act that both halves name ties them together. A call nobody answered is shown as unanswered, and a task with no call as "no report called for": neither is read as anything else.

Agreements uses that block for one task: passing money on, the Control task (K8). The Terms that hold it may carry a call. Most often those are a service's deal with a work. The work signed that deal as much as the service did, by its own signature (G1), so the call in it is the work's parties' as much as the service's. They can also be a work's own Terms, where one of its owners receives for the others. A **leg report** is a report that answers that call. What it says beyond the block is K8's to say: what was received, what was passed on and to whom, the doer's own part where it has one, what was kept as a fee, and, apart from those, what is still owed, each with its amount. Where one report speaks of several legs, which of its lines each receiving side is shown is not the grammar's: it is for the cMIP of that task to say.

No Terms are made to call for reports, and nobody is made to answer a call. But a call is written in signed Terms. So anyone can read, before paying, which agreements call for reports. Which calls were answered is seen by the two sides of each leg.

**Every leg is the same exchange.** Whoever carries out an obligation reports that they did. Whoever received confirms, by a report of their own; on some ways of paying, that confirmation can be automatic. Sometimes the one who received holds money for others: a band member who receives for the whole band. Then it does not end there. That person now has an obligation of their own, to pass the money on within the band's own agreement, and the same exchange happens there: they report that they did, and each bandmate confirms. Where that person is one of the band, part of the money is their own. That part is not a fee: their report states it as their own part, apart from anything kept as a fee. No call is attached to someone because they receive. A call is always attached to a task, in the agreement under which that task is carried out.

**One leg, and never further.** A report travels one leg. It answers the call of its own leg, and goes to the other side of that leg. That holds in every layer of MOR, not only here: it is the block's. Nothing carries a report further, up or down: no call names a place or a reader, nobody outside a leg can call for that leg's reports, and no report is made to carry another leg's reports along. The grammar itself goes no further either. It finds who receives, and stops. How far the money then travels, and whether it comes round in a loop, are questions only for someone who reads several legs together, and that someone needs a path people signed for.

**Reports are data.** Sometimes someone further away does want to see. A partner who wants to check that a split among people was really carried out cannot trust the one who received; it can trust only the end of the waterfall. But a report says who paid whom, and how much: it is data about the people of its leg. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "Data needs to move along paths that people agree, with signature." The leg is such a path. Any other path is agreed in the same way, and only so: a data cMIP focused on agreements, named in an agreement the people concerned signed. A call does not do it: a call names no reader. So people are seen from beyond their leg only where they agreed to it: "People have to AGREE to be under surveillance, in an agreement they signed." Which consent a data cMIP asks for is that cMIP's to say. Which cMIP and which rule apply is the signers' choice, made when they make the agreement. And where the rules of their agreement say that the majority decides, the majority decides: a member who was outvoted is read as the rule that member signed says, and the grammar answers nothing more about them. That agreeing is done in an agreement, and nowhere else: no other layer of MOR carries a report further than its leg. "Or surveillance become cheap and omnipresent."

**The buyer's seat.** Leg by leg holds for the one who clicks too. Paying the work is a leg: the buyer carries it out, and whoever receives for the work receives it. The payment is the buyer's own act, and whoever signs an act that holds a task may call for a report on it. So the buyer may call for the confirmation that the payment was received, and the grammar reads every payment as making that call: it is made by default, and nobody has to write it. The confirmation goes to the buyer, who is the other side of that leg. Then comes access to what they bought. That is all the buyer is shown by default. Whether the money then reached every member is further legs. The buyer sees one of those only along a path the people concerned agreed to, with their signature: a data cMIP. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "Leg by leg by default. Buyer needs to receive confirmation payment has been received. Then, access to wathever they bought. Not that the money reached all members for example."

**The contest.** A contest is an act of the grammar, as an assent is. It is made against an offer (an agreement that was signed before everyone taking part was known) or against a vow (the same, in his words, "without bytes attached"). Whom it reaches follows from that, and needs no rule of its own: it reaches the people who signed that offer or vow. For a buyer, a contest comes only where what was bought was not delivered after payment. It does not run through the whole sequence. It reaches back to where it all started: the offer or vow the buyer bought under. Its signers are contested, and not the services they picked. If those signers believe the fault is their service's, they contest the service in turn. So a contest goes one agreement at a time.

With that, a reader who holds the reports of several legs can follow a click both ways: down from the payer, and up from a contributor. Who holds them is what "one leg" settles: the two sides of each leg hold their own; the buyer holds the confirmation of their own payment; and anyone else holds a leg's reports only along a path its people signed for. Two reports are tied by the act they both name: for a payment, the settlement. And because each states its amounts, two reports that disagree are shown side by side. Whether what was passed on is what the Terms required is still their cMIP's to say; the report only makes the whole readable.

**Lie exposed.** "It all went through", said by one side alone; "we passed it on", said where nobody on the other side can contradict it, or in a form that only readers of one agreement's cMIP can read; "you never asked", said by those whose own signers did; "that was our fee", said of money that is still owed; "that was a fee too", said of a holder's own share; "your payment never arrived", said to a buyer who holds the receiving side's confirmation of it; "take it up with our service", said to a buyer by the signers of the agreement the buyer bought under; "only the service can ask", said to the people of a work that signed the deal; and "you agreed to be watched", said from upstream to people whose signed Terms name no such thing. Each agreement's own record is signed and may be complete in its own form; to a reader without that agreement's cMIP it shows nothing, and G9 would then answer "unchecked" for everything beyond it. A call written in signed Terms shows who asked to hear back; a report in a form every reader reads shows what each side said; and the settlement both name shows where two sides agree and where they do not.

**Protects.** Those further along a sequence, and the payer, who did not sign the agreements before them: they can read, before paying, which of them call for reports, and from whom. The buyer, who holds the receiving side's own confirmation of the payment, and who contests the people they bought from, not a service they never chose. The people of a work, whose deal is theirs as much as the service's. An owner who receives for the others, whose own share is not shown as a fee taken from them. A member of a collective, who is read by the rule that member signed and by nothing else. The people of every leg, whose reports move nowhere they did not sign for: they are seen from beyond their own leg only where they agreed to it in an agreement they signed. Every signer, whom no one outside their own act can oblige to report. Each agreement, which keeps its free choice of cMIP: none has to adopt another's in order to be read. And whoever is still owed, whose money is not shown as someone's fee.

**Reasoning.** What must hold, in the words of Nobody, allegedly, is: "can I with a single click reward all the people that contributed to the service or good via MOR". A click that climbs is a result that depends on a sequence of agreements. When the details of paying were handed to cMIPs (G1, section 5), the MOR lead's doubt was that "paid", read wholly by each service's cMIP, would be unreadable wherever a reader lacks one agreement's cMIP. Nobody, allegedly: "I actually thought about this a few days ago… yes, the whole chain of payment and also the chain of agreements that dictates does not require to always use the same cMIPs, the choice of cMIP stands on the leg, not the whole. So I think you are right, when the result depends on a chain of agreements, informing everyone of the whole where needed has its value." (He later said of his own words that "sequence is better" than "chain of agreements"; this text follows that.) So G11.1 puts the choice on the leg: on the Terms each leg is carried out under.

*Must every leg report?* On his "where needed": "When needed could be all the time that we did not check". To the proposal of letting a test that builds cMIPs on top of the grammar answer it: "Good idea, test by building on top, I like it". The test was built by the Dubsar Suite lead (`docs/click-sketch-report.md`, branch `claude/click-sketch`): three split services, a band member three legs from the click. It showed that one click can reach everyone, but only if every leg on the way reports; that a payer could not tell, before paying, whether it would; and that the report, as it was then written in this grammar rule, could not be followed as its own words said.

*The report is not Agreements'.* Asked, after that test, what is truly useful in a leg report: "We need to be able to track it both directions, as exercise. The agreements need to point to the right places to receive the confirmations." To a field for it, proposed by the MOR lead: "« where my reports are found » feels too broad", and then: "Tasks may or may not call for a report. Therefore, a task can if needed specify where it expect its report from." Asked whether that is a rule for all of MOR: "Yes, I lean so too. It is elegant, and can be used wherever it is needed." Asked whether a report carries only what it answers, what it relied on and who signs: "Seems enough yes". Asked who can ask for a report: "Who signed the act that holds the task, no?" So the report is a block of the shared language, written down by the MOR lead as a proposal, and this grammar rule is only its use: which task carries the call here (K8), who the doer and the receiving side are for that task, and what that task adds to a report. He gave the same answer again in another window, on the question whether a leg that receives for others should be required to report: "Excellent question, turned into an MOR rule that extends to all tasks, a task may or may not request a report, if it does it states where it expect it."

*How the block answers the three flaws the test found.*
- **Where the next report is.** As first written, this grammar rule followed each entry that named an agreement to a report made within that agreement. But a work paid through its deal is reported on within the deal, so the climb stopped at the first deal. The block's answer, as it now stands: the where is implied by the leg. A report goes to the other side of its own leg, so each side holds its own leg's reports, and nobody has to guess from the agreement a report names (G11.4(a)). A reader who is no side of a leg is led to its reports by nothing in the block (G11.6).
- **How one report is linked to the next.** As first written, each report had to name the report before it, and nothing told the next service which report its money came from: the settlement arrives first, the report later. The block's answer: halves are tied by an act both name. For a payment that act is the settlement, which both sides hold. Neither report needs to know the other (G11.4(b)).
- **How a disagreement is seen.** As first written, a report named acts and no amounts, so two reports could not be seen to disagree on how much. The block's answer: everything beyond its three parts is the task's to define. K8 defines amounts as part of a leg report, so two reports that disagree are shown side by side (G11.4(c)).

The same answer serves a fourth thing the test found: money held back and still owed looked exactly like a fee kept. K8's row asks for the two apart.

*No leg is made to report.* His words are "may or may not" and "if needed", and the block obliges nobody outside the act that holds the call. So this grammar rule makes no Terms carry a call and makes nobody answer. What it gives instead is sight: a call is in signed Terms, readable before paying, and silence is shown as silence. What that leaves uncovered is a stated cost (section 7).

*Who makes the call in a deal.* "Who signed the act that holds the task" was, in a deal, the service alone, for as long as an agreement named as a party never signed. He described the deal otherwise: "The service signed with work and pointed to the receiver." And: "So, the agreement with the service is signed by all, the report about the payment only comes from the receiver. It’s in that agreement that a request for surviliance can be cooked." So the work signs its deal, as its own Terms require (G1.2), and the call in it is made by the work's people as much as by the service (G11.2). On the work's side, the report about a payment comes from its receiver, and from no other of its people.

*Every leg is the same exchange.* The click test found a work whose receiver was one of its owners: that owner held the other's money, and no report was asked of anyone. Of such a person he said: "So, he is reporting his leg, fulfilling his obligation." The MOR lead proposed that a call might be attached to a receiver. His answer described something simpler: "The service fulfilled its obligation, reported that it did so. The band receiver confirmed it via report (this report can be automatic on certain rails). Now, the band receiver needs to fulfill his obligation. So the same exchange of reports happen within the band." So G11.5 adds no new thing. The one who receives for others is the doer of the next obligation, within their own agreement; G11.2 lets that agreement carry the call, and the proposal of a call attached to a receiver was withdrawn by the MOR lead.

*One leg.* He then asked his own question: "Why would the service care? Obligation fulfilled on their end. A partner, that wants to verify that a human split was executed would want to see it." And: "The partner cannot trust the receiver, it can only trust the end of the waterfall." So: "the answer is it depends, and if it depends it needs to be built to accommodate both". He let the idea run as far as it would go ("Yes, upstream of payment can request, and anyone upstream of them should be able to do so too."), and said why: "Yes, it’s a huge stretch on purpose. To see where it snaps. I think the grammar to support it should be there, in agreements only. Never extended to the other layers. Or surveillance become cheap and omnipresent. People have to AGREE to be under surveillance, in an agreement they signed." Asked how far a confirmation travels: "One leg, that’s all it can do. For everything else’s it’s DATA cMIPs focused on agreements". So G11.6: in the grammar a report travels one leg. Both needs are met, each in its place: the service, whose obligation ends at its own leg, by the grammar; the partner who wants the end of the waterfall, by data cMIPs that the people concerned agreed to in an agreement they signed. What the grammar gives for the second is only what it already has: Terms that name their cMIPs by fingerprint (G2), and nobody bound by what they did not sign (G5). And it is written here, in Agreements, and nowhere else.

*The buyer's seat.* A second test was built, on this text as it then stood (`docs/click-sketch-12-report.md`, branch `claude/click-sketch`). It showed that the one who clicks, the "I" of the wish, had no seat: having signed nothing in the sequence, the payer was shown no leg at all, not even that the payment had arrived. The Dubsar Suite lead asked whether a leg's call may name "whoever paid" among where its reports go. Nobody, allegedly: "Leg by leg by default. Buyer needs to receive confirmation payment has been received. Then, access to wathever they bought. Not that the money reached all members for example." So G11.7. His answer names no call. It gives the buyer what a leg gives: the payment, and the confirmation that it was received; then access. Reading the payment to the work as a leg of its own, the buyer's, is the MOR lead's reading of "leg by leg", and this text follows it. "By default" is why no call needs to be written: draft 15 reads it as the payer's own call, made by default (below). What lies beyond the default is a path people agreed to: a data cMIP its people signed for (G11.6(a)). His "needs to receive" is not written as a MUST on anyone, since no leg is made to report; a payment nobody confirmed is shown as one side only (section 8.2).

*The contest.* In the same conversation: "The contest comes only if goods or service were not delivered after payment. Then the buyer does not contest the one leg, it contests the whole sequence." The MOR lead asked whether that means that every leg whose money traces to the payment sees that it is contested, or that the contest is addressed to the seller, who answers for the whole. He: "À. I mean it all started from an agreement, so it’s just need to reach back the agreement. So maybe the whole sequence is not needed, the signers of the agreement are contested at first, not the services they pick. If the signer believe the mistake is at the service they contest the service" Read as a whole, the later words refine the earlier: he began at the whole sequence and ended at the agreement it all started from. So G11.8: the contest reaches the agreement the buyer bought under, and its signers first; the service is theirs to contest. Until draft 15 a contest had no kind of its own here: it was a Statement of a kind a cMIP gives, and the grammar said only whom it reaches (changed in draft 16, below). Earlier versions of this text already spoke of a buyer who contests (section 7, "Paid, and the author gone"; escrow, section 5.1); they did not say whom a contest reaches.

*The contest is an act (draft 16).* Fable's review of draft 14 called G11.8 a solution: a policy about who is blamed first, from which a verifier computes nothing. Draft 15 put the question to Nobody, allegedly, and the MOR lead leaned to move the contest to a cMIP. He: "Contest is MOR act though. It is just extended. I contest an offer (an agreement with unknown participant at the time of signing) or I contest a vow (which is the same thing but without bytes attached)". And why it is core: "Making greed legible does not work without contest, it makes it core." In plain words: the whole grammar makes what people do with each other readable by a stranger. If the stranger can read it and can say nothing back, reading changes nothing. So the contest is one of the grammar's own acts, a third kind of Statement beside the assent and the counter signature.

G11.8 is rewritten around the act and what it is made against. Whom it reaches is then not a policy: a contest made against an offer reaches those who signed that offer, as any Statement is made within one agreement. That is the earlier exchange ("the signers of the agreement are contested at first, not the services they pick") and this one, read together; this exchange does not itself say whom a contest reaches. What draft 15 said of a buyer is kept where the earlier exchange supports it: a buyer's contest comes only where what was bought was not delivered after payment.

Two words are new. "Offer" is defined by his words, and read as an agreement whose Terms name a party by role. "Vow" had no meaning in this text; it gets the least his words give, "the same thing but without bytes attached", and nothing in the grammar turns on telling a vow from an offer. Both readings, what "It is just extended" points at, and who may contest besides a buyer, are listed for him (the draft's section 8.2, items 3 to 6). The signers' own contest of their service is left where draft 15 had it, within their deal; whether a deal is an offer or a vow is not said (section 8.3).

*An outvoted member.* A collective may sign by a vote. The MOR lead asked whether a member who was outvoted has then agreed to be seen. Nobody, allegedly: "That’s up to the cMIP to decide, not me. Then, it’s up to the signers to decide at inception of the agreement. If the rules of the agreement state « majority decides » then majority decides." So G11.6(b) does not answer it. The consent asked is the cMIP's; the cMIP and the rule are what the signers chose when they made the agreement; and a rule that says the majority decides is read as it says, as G5.1(b) reads any power.

*Leg by leg only; reports are data.* The text before this one let a call say where its reports were expected, and let that place be a partner's. That was a fix of the MOR lead's, from the second test: a partner named by every leg read the whole climb with the report block alone. The MOR lead then named a tension to Nobody, allegedly: the report is a block for all of MOR, and seeing further was to be in Agreements only. He leaned that in every layer a call sends reports only between the two sides of its own leg. Nobody, allegedly: "Leg by leg only." The MOR lead asked whether that holds in Agreements too, withdrawing his own fix. He: "This to me this is data. Data needs to move along paths that people agree, with signature." And when the MOR lead said the report would then lose its "where": "Well we had agreed the where was needed, we just did not realize the where was implied." So G11.2 and G11.6. A call says whether a report is wanted and from whom, and names no place and no reader. A report goes to the other side of its own leg. Every other path is one that people agreed to, with their signature. That such a path is built as a data cMIP is from his earlier words, quoted above ("For everything else’s it’s DATA cMIPs focused on agreements"); this exchange does not repeat them. "Implied by the leg" and "where that side receives" are the MOR lead's reading of "implied". His earlier "Leg by leg by default" (the buyer's seat, above) and the later "Leg by leg only" are read as one train of thought: what the core gives is one leg, and whatever goes beyond it is agreed, with signature. Where this reading says more than the exchange is listed in `history.md` (Part G, section G.3; and Part H, section H.5).

*The two sides of a leg (draft 15).* Draft 14 used "leg" for two things: one agreement of a sequence, and a path with two sides. Fable's review found a third wording in the block ("two sides who both signed"), and a test built on draft 14 found the cost: inside one agreement, a service paid by another service was shown that agreement's whole split, with every owner's amount, though it had signed nothing in it. The MOR lead asked Nobody, allegedly, who the two sides are where those who signed a deal are not those paid. He: "Oh what leg?" To the proposal that a leg is always between the two parties of one agreement: "If it’s  a collective agreement with a service and the service is a split there are many legs. Each member receives the payment and can confirm in the other direction, but each member also has to inform the agreement it is under that it got paid, no?" So a leg is not an agreement. It is one obligation carried out, between whoever carries it out and whoever receives it, and a split is many legs. That is the one meaning draft 15 gives the word, in the MOR lead's wording. His earlier "the choice of cMIP stands on the leg, not the whole" still holds: each leg is carried out under one agreement's Terms, and their cMIPs read it.

Where only a delegate signed for the collective, he found the gap between who signed and who is paid "real", and then closed it himself: the delegate's signature is the collective's (G1, "An agreement's signature"), so each member signed the deal through her agreement. Where no such signature exists, "a cMIP closes it". So G11.2 says who the sides are whether or not they signed, and leaves the unsigned case to a cMIP.

What each side is shown of a report is not the grammar's. On another question from the same test (a band that owns most of a song and is not counted when the song's Terms change), he said: "That deal could exist… but it’s not the only option. I think we’re fixing small problems often in the same way. The answer is often: you did not use the right cMIP then." The MOR lead applies that here: which lines of a report each side receives is for the cMIP of the task. The grammar defines the sides, and stops (G11.3).

*The buyer's call, made by default (draft 15).* Fable's review found that G11.7 and the block disagreed: G11.7 expected a confirmation "with no call written in any Terms", and the block's R2 says "A report that names no call is no report". The MOR lead's smallest way to close it: a payment is itself an act that holds a task, the task of paying; the payer signed it; so the payer may call for the receiving side's confirmation, as the signer of any such act may. The buyer's confirmation "by default" is that call, made by default. This uses the block as it is written for who may call. It still needs two things the block does not say, and they are listed in the draft's section 8.1; the block's file is not changed.

*Never beyond one leg (draft 15).* Earlier drafts left open how far one receiver may point onward to another, and what a loop answers (P8). With reports moving leg by leg only, the answer for the grammar is short: it never follows a receiver beyond one leg. It reads the `receiver` field until it finds the person who receives, which may mean reading through an agreement (a work names its deal; the deal names the service). That finds one side of one leg. Everything after that is another leg, read by its own two sides. Depth and loops are then questions only for a reader who puts several legs together, and that reader exists only along a path people signed for: a data cMIP.

*A holder's own part.* One more thing in this grammar rule is the MOR lead's, settled as technical wording from the second test and recorded with the exchange "The payer's seat"; it is not the words of Nobody, allegedly. The test showed a band member who receives for the band, whose own share could only be written as "kept (a fee)" (its 2c). So K8's row has a line for the doer's own part.

What a leg report says is in this text, in K8's row, and not in a cMIP, for the reason the shape was first kept: what must be read by those who lack a leg's cMIP cannot be defined by that cMIP (G11.3).

## G12. Forks

**Plain words.** Some acts come in a line, each pointing at the one before: a person's own acts in an agreement (G6), an agreement's Terms, each replacing the last (G4). The next act in a line is an **update**. Where two updates follow the same act, and each was signed by everyone who needed to sign it, the line has split: that is a fork. The grammar sees it from the acts alone, and says nothing of what anyone meant. Each of the two is a **tip**.

While a fork stands, both tips stop. Neither is the next step, and nothing built on either counts. The last act both share goes on as before: for Terms, the last shared version stays in force, so nobody is stuck. A fork is never chosen by anyone's software: a verifier shows both tips. And the software a person looks through, the client, must not show either tip as settled; that is a duty of clients, which no verifier can check.

A fork is resolved by one more update of the shared act, signed as any update of it must be, that names the tips and says which one continues. The other tip never counted as the next version. Because the problem can always arise, an agreement may decide in advance how it will be solved: it may give someone a power to sign the resolving update, under a rule of its own choosing, so no single way is forced. If two resolving updates disagree, they are themselves a fork, resolved the same way. Either way, what other people did relying on a tip that is set aside still stands, and is shown as disputed. This is one grammar rule for every act that can fork; there is no list of cases.

**Lie exposed.** "This one is the real one", said by whoever a tip suits, to whoever is shown only that tip, or shown it as settled; and "we settled it", said of a fork by people who could not have signed the step where it parted. Each tip is signed and looks valid alone; only the two together show the fork, and only the shared act shows who could resolve it.

**Protects.** Whoever relied on a tip without causing the fork: the payer, the newcomer, the other party who answered an act (G12.5). Everyone in the last shared version, which goes on while the fork stands. Every party, who is not left stuck in a fork with no way out, because the way out (a resolving update, signed by those who could sign the step, or under a power given in advance) is fixed before anyone needs it. And the reader, who is shown the fork and every tip instead of one picked for them, and none of them as resolved.

**Reasoning.** Nobody, allegedly, asked: "Can we attempt  to solve incompatible forks (a fork that should not exist) for all sorts of acts that need it?" Asked whether one shape fits all acts (shown, never chosen; ended only by a join that names every branch and says which continues, or merges them; signed by whoever could sign the last act both branches share; what others relied on in a dropped branch standing, shown as disputed): "I think it does. Let’s focus on unresolved forks…" So G12 is one grammar rule, written for a line of any acts, with no list of cases; G4.7 and G6.2 say only where the grammar meets a fork, and how a fork of Terms is read while it stands. G12.3 reads "whoever could sign the last act both branches share" as whoever could sign the next act after it in that line: the same signer for one signer's line, and for Terms those whose signatures G4.8 requires. ("Join" was the word of that exchange, and of drafts up to 15. From draft 16 the act is a resolving update, and merging is not kept: below.)

On forks that nobody resolves, to the proposal that an agreement name in advance who or what settles them: "Resolver makes sense, the problem potentially exists, so deciding in advance how to solve it makes sense, and this way does not force a single way to do it." ("Resolver" was his word of the moment.) Deciding in advance needs no block of its own: the resolving update is already there (G12.3), and so is a power given in advance to make an act that binds the parties (G5). So G12.4 only says that the two meet: an agreement may give a power to sign the resolving update, and that power's rule is where "how to solve it" lives, as a cMIP answering task K4 (section 5). No single way is forced, because the rule is whatever the agreement names.

*A fork, from MOR's side (draft 16).* Draft 15 left open what a fork takes away, and proposed a reading: both branches go on binding whoever signed them, and only "which is latest" is unanswered. The MOR lead leaned to accept it. Nobody, allegedly: "I think it would be more correct to state that two acts got signed by all parties that needed to sign their update. (It is seen from MOR perspective, not the user). A client that sees both tips should not show it as a resolved act, what needs to happen is something that stops both tips until resolved." The MOR lead read that as: both tips stop; nothing built on either counts until resolved; the last shared version stays in force; a client shows the fork as unresolved; resolved by a join. He: "It is resolved by making one of the two void with an update." "Void" had been retired as a term on 13 November, so the MOR lead proposed "never counted as the next version" for the tip set aside. He: "Yes pls".

So the proposal is not taken, and G12 is reworked. A fork is defined from MOR's side: two updates, each signed by all the parties that needed to sign it. Nothing is said about who meant what, or who is to blame. Both tips stop (G12.2). A fork is resolved by an update that continues one tip (G12.3), and the other never counted. "Signed as an update must be" is read as: as an update of the shared act must be signed, because the shared version is the one in force while the fork stands; that is how draft 15's join was signed too.

What is left of "join": the word is no longer a term. The act it named, where it said which branch continues, is the resolving update, with the same fields under one new name (`resolves`). The other use of a join, merging the branches into one, is not kept: his words resolve a fork by making one of the two "void". People who want something from both tips can write it as the next update, once the fork is resolved.

Two things in G12 are the MOR lead's, in his arrived-at line, and not in the words of Nobody, allegedly. First, that two rival resolving updates are themselves a fork (G12.7). It is what stays of draft 15's proposal on two joins, and it has a cost, written in section 7: where fewer than all can update the Terms, a group can hold the agreement where it was. Second, that what others relied on in the tip set aside stands, shown as disputed (G12.5): that is from the earlier exchange above, and is kept as it was.

*The client's duty (draft 16).* "A client that sees both tips should not show it as a resolved act" is not something a verifier can apply: what a client showed a person leaves no signed act behind. So it is written as what it is, a duty of clients (client conformance), in one place, G12.8, and the draft says there that no verifier can check it. His word is "should"; this text has no SHOULD, and writes MUST NOT.

*One signer's own line.* His words are about updates that several parties needed to sign. G12 is one rule for every line, so "both tips stop" is read for one signer's own acts too: an honest signer who forked by accident (a lost device) has everything they built on either tip stopped until they resolve it, with their own key. And a signer could stop their own assent from counting by forking. Before Terms are in force, the counter signature already lets them take an assent back. After, Terms stop only by replacement (G4.9), so no fork takes Terms out of force. What is not said is whether that signer is bound meanwhile (P9).

## G13. Now is what an act cites

**Plain words.** After any act, other acts pile up: payments, thanks, fillings, new versions, each made for its own reason and tied to its own people. No clock runs over them. But every act names other acts, and each naming is a knot: an act that names another certainly came after it. Where lines of acts name each other, order emerges by itself. So an act's "now" is simply what it names. It is read against the versions and acts it cites, and a later version does not reach back to change what it did. That is why every act names what it relied on: a payment names the version of the work it read. An act is never read as having relied on something it did not name. Citing can show "after". It can never show "nothing later", or what time it was. For those two, every reader uses the place and the clock the agreement itself names. Only where the agreement names none does each reader accept the anchoring it is willing to accept, and every such answer says which anchoring it stands on, or is given as shown.

**Lie exposed.** "You paid the wrong one", said to a payer after the agreement changed behind them; "I was relying on the earlier version", said afterwards by someone whose act named nothing; "mine came first", said of two acts that nothing ties together; and "nothing came after", said on the strength of an anchoring the reader never accepted; and "by my clock it had not yet ended", said against the clock the agreement names. Signed text shows what an act names; without this grammar rule a verifier could read the act against whatever version suited the one asking.

**Protects.** Whoever acts on a version they read and named: the payer above all, who did not choose when others would change the agreement and cannot know of a change not yet shown to them. And the reader, who is told what each answer about "latest" or about time stands on, instead of being handed someone else's clock.

**Reasoning.** If "now" were read when someone asks, whether a payer paid the right person could change answer after they paid. Asked whether every act should instead be read against the versions in force at its own position, Nobody, allegedly, answered with what really happens: "What happens after it got paid? A series of acts have been created for various reasons, tying to various id… chains crossing, knots forming, order emerging". Asked whether that means free order (each citation a knot that says this came after that; an act's now is what it cites; a place needed only for nothing later): "Yes, I think so. Then, anyone accept the anchoring they are willing to accept." So G13.1 and G13.3 take order from what the acts themselves carry, which any holder of copies can check (section 1), and G13.4 leaves the two things citing cannot give to an anchoring. That every act names what it relied on is written as a grammar rule of reading (G13.2: what is not named was not relied on), because no verifier could check a duty to name. Time is written into G13.4 because G8.2 already says a time inside an act is only its signer's claim, so that an anchoring is the only thing left to answer it. Which anchoring wins is G7.4: what an agreement names binds every reader of it; "anyone accept the anchoring they are willing to accept" is what holds where it names none.

## G14. The baker

**Plain words.** A finished work is baked: its bytes are sealed in an envelope, and the envelope says who baked it. Anyone can copy those bytes and write a fresh agreement that claims to sell them. So the first thing to look at is the envelope: who baked it? An agreement for a baked work counts only if the baker is one of the parties it names. Or, where the baker has sold her baker's rights, if one of its parties holds them through the succession of the work's own agreements, which starts with the baker. A stranger with no such succession has nothing to sell. To sell the work as their own, a thief is at least forced to edit it and bake it again, and that is another envelope, with another baker.

**Lie exposed.** "This work is mine to sell", said by someone who copied its bytes and wrote an agreement of their own around them. That agreement is whole, signed and in force on its own terms; nothing in it shows that its signers had nothing to sell. Only the envelope says who baked the work, and only the succession from the baker says who holds her rights now.

**Protects.** The baker, and whoever holds from her, who did not sign the stranger's agreement and may never see it. And the buyer, who can check, before paying, that the one who sells is the baker or holds from her.

**Reasoning.** Someone who copies a work's bytes and sells them in a fresh agreement of their own is seen by no other grammar rule: that agreement is valid on its face. Nobody, allegedly: "That’s when we look at the envelope of the work. Who baked it? So, the thief should be at least forced to edit it and bake it again." Then: "So, the baker has to be one of the parties the agreement for that envelope states." Asked whether a buyer who bought the work outright counts through the transfers that start with the baker: "The baker, through the chain of agreements of the work has sold her bakers rights, no?" (His "chain of agreements" is what this text calls a succession: agreements that follow one another in time. A sequence is the other thing, agreements paying into one another.) So G14 is those three sentences: the envelope is where a verifier looks (G14.1), the baker must be a party (G14.2(a)), or the succession from the baker must reach one (G14.2(b)). How rights pass from one agreement to the next is each one's own business, read through the tasks it names. "Envelope", "bake" and "baker" are the core's words; this text names them and does not define them. G14 covers the stranger's sale only. The same baker selling the same work twice, in two successions, is still seen by neither (section 7, "Ownership is as wide as its place").

## The tasks: why these

**Why these, and why so few.** The research found that the same few operations answer the 49 questions, and that a "silent sixth operation", applying a stated rule to known inputs, needs no trust because anyone can redo it. So the tasks sort by whom they make you trust: nobody (K1, K4, K11, K13), a witness of the world (K2, K3, K5, and K12 for most kinds), a place (K6), a teller (K7), a hand (K8), a recipient (K9), a judge (K10). Each task is one question; many cMIPs may answer each one, pointed at different objects. Nobody, allegedly: "I think more tasks means less defining for each one. And clearer mapping for cMIPs." That holds inside each row too: a cMIP for share registers and a cMIP for votes both answer K4 without either defining the other.

**Why K13 is a task of its own.** Nobody, allegedly: "reputation tracking no platform owns or computes is important." K4 is one agreement's rule; reputation reads a signer's acts across many agreements. K13 says what such a reading may rest on (signed acts and place answers, nothing a platform alone holds), and that its answer is the same for anyone who holds the same acts. Which measures matter, and how they are weighed, is the cMIP's.
