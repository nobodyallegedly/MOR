## 0. In plain words

**The central idea: an agreement can be a party.** People sign an agreement. That agreement can then stand as one party in another agreement, as a person would, with nobody signing in its place: what reaches it is passed on by its own terms. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "The key concept is « agreement as a party » then it’s what you build with it that changes". Everything else in this text is there so that this can be read safely by a stranger. What people build with it is theirs:

- a **work**: an agreement among those who made something, saying who owns it and in what shares;
- a **service**: someone who does a job for a work (dividing its money, holding a payment in escrow) under a **deal**, which is an agreement between the service and the work as a party;
- a work that stands on other works names them as parties, so that one split pays into the next;
- a **collective**: nothing separate. "A collective is simply an agreement that decided to define many aspects of how it can govern itself", and "all agreements are collectives if there are shares, wether loose or hyper defined". An agreement with shares defines as much or as little of its own governance as it chooses, and the grammar has no other notion of a group.

Rule 10 is this idea, written for a verifier. It keeps its number from the earlier drafts; it is the first thing to read.

People agree in order to defend themselves against the other party's future self, and their own. Honest drift and lying look the same from outside, so every written agreement is written for a third person who may turn up later: a newcomer, an heir, a judge, a buyer. The least two people need to write down is exactly the least that third person needs to see.

**Agreements does not rule.** It is a grammar: the words people use to write down their own rules, and the few ways those words must be read so that a stranger reads them the same way. What people agree (a price, a vote, a split, a deadline) is theirs, written in cMIPs they choose. The grammar only makes sure that what they wrote can be checked by anyone, later.

Most of what an agreement needs is already provided by the layers around Agreements: words that cannot shift (State), a mark that binds someone (Sign), getting the words to someone (Deliver), placing them before or after something else (Order), and power over a thing (Control). Agreements does not redefine those. It adds only what those five cannot give on their own, and every addition here must answer two questions:

- **Which lie does it expose that signed, ordered text cannot already expose?**
- **Whom does it protect who did not choose it?**

That test leaves thirteen grammar rules for a verifier. Everything else an agreement needs is handed to cMIPs as fourteen tasks, each asking one question. What fits neither is written down as a cost, openly, instead of being patched with another rule.

In one sentence: **Agreements fixes what was agreed and who is bound by it, and says honestly what it does not know; everything about the world, about time and about value is somebody's signed word, reported as such.**

The thirteen grammar rules, in plain words (rule 10 is the central one):

1. **Whole.** Whatever the agreement relies on is named by its fingerprint. Anything named otherwise is not part of it.
2. **Author.** The agreement says who wrote its words. Whoever wrote them carries their unclearness.
3. **In force.** Signing first does not bind you alone. Your signature counts once the signatures the agreement asks for are all there. Terms stop being in force when Terms replacing them are in force, and new Terms replace old ones only if they carry the signatures the old ones require. Two replacements of the same Terms, each carrying them, are a fork (rule 12): while it stands, the old Terms stay in force. An agreement never ends: it only gets a new version, signed as its Terms require; until then, the last version keeps binding.
4. **Own signature.** Nobody is bound by an act they did not sign, except through an agreement they did sign that gives that power, and the act must say so at the time.
5. **Own chain.** Each person's acts in one agreement point at their previous one. Two acts pointing at the same previous one prove that person told two stories: a fork (rule 12).
6. **Place to look.** An agreement may name one ordered place. Then "nothing later" and "nothing else" have answers, and only what is found there counts. Without one, every such answer is "as shown", never "latest".
7. **Say who said it.** Whatever concerns the world, time or value is reported with the name of whoever stated it. An agreement never makes it true.
8. **Unchecked is not passed.** What a verifier could not check, it reports as unchecked.
9. **Spots.** An agreement may keep a place for someone not yet known: whoever proves they did X, received Y, hold Z, or fit W. A fifth, open kind is for those nobody could anticipate. A spot is earned per proof: it may be filled many times, each time by the proof that earned it, and its cMIP says whether it holds one or many. Who fills a spot is always reported with the proof and who confirmed it. An open offer does not end either: a new version can close a spot to new identities, and every filling already earned keeps its place. Whoever earned a filling has exactly the rights the version it was earned under offers, conditions included; no later version changes them without the holder's own signature. A version may name the rule under which its rights end: the answer is then **void**, with the cause shown beside it.
10. **An agreement as a party.** An agreement may name another agreement as one of its parties. Nobody signs for it, and no group has to be formed: a collective is only an agreement with shares. The payer pays the work. The work agreement points to its service agreement, a deal between the service and the work as a party, which gives the address to pay on each rail and the service's fee. When the service pays out, it divides by the work agreement's shares and identities, each owner's addresses coming from that owner's home. This is how one split pays into the next. (A work that points to no split may still name a receiver.)
11. **Seen, by the agreed witness.** Where someone is rewarded for being seen (a play, a view, a repost), only the witness the agreement names can count it. Of what the witness counts, only what the agreement's own rule sets up to be counted is counted: for example only the counts that led to a settled payment, or every view, as those who agree choose. Nothing has to be rejected.
12. **Forks.** Where a line of acts splits in two and only one act may follow (a person's own acts, an agreement's Terms, any act that names the one before it), that is a fork. It is shown, never chosen. It ends only by a join, signed by whoever could sign the step where the branches part, that names every branch and says which continues or merges them; or by an arbiter the agreement named in advance. What others relied on in a branch that is dropped still stands, shown as disputed.
13. **Now is what an act cites.** An act is read against the versions and acts it names, and a later version does not reach back to change what it did. Every act names what it relied on: a payment names the version of the work it read. An act that names another came after it, so order emerges wherever chains cite each other, with no clock. Citing never shows "nothing later", or what time it was: for those, each reader accepts the anchoring it is willing to accept, and the answer says which.

The way out is the reason for rule 4: nothing ties a person except their own signature, and nothing changes an agreement except what its own signers agreed in advance. An agreement never ends; it only gets a new version, signed as its own Terms require. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "Terms rule, because they were agreed by all signers". The cost is equally plain, and is written in section 7: an agreement that names no way to change it can change only when everyone signs again.

And the same reason keeps the shower free. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "Mor won’t force the shower, it will sustain it". No grammar rule makes a work pay the works it stands on. Rule 10 makes it possible (a work may name the works it stands on as parties, each paid as any work is), rule 7 makes it visible, and the choice stays with those who sign.

## 1. Purpose

The central idea of the grammar is that an agreement can be a party to another agreement (rule 10). Works, services, collectives and their deals are built with it; none of them is a separate thing in the grammar.

Agreements defines a grammar:

- the acts in which agreeing is written (Terms and Statement);
- the grammar rules by which a verifier decides, from acts alone, **who is bound by which Terms, and what is shown, unknown or contradicted**;
- the tasks that cMIPs fill so that people can agree on anything, by rules they choose: votes, sales, shares, splits, agents, deadlines, witnesses, judges, payments.

Agreements does not decide what anyone must agree. It gives the words and the reading; the rules people agree on are theirs, written as cMIPs filling its tasks.

Agreements does not define how words are fixed, how signatures work, how acts travel, how a shared order is kept, or how value is held. It names those operations (State, Sign, Deliver, Order, Control) where it needs them.

Agreements does not decide whether anything happened in the world. It records who said so.

### Two properties, not rules

Two of the research's 49 tasks are not tasks but qualities every operation must have (`agreement-grammar.md`, finding 1):

- **Durable** (#9 Keep): what an act fixes is still there and unaltered when needed.
- **Verifiable** (#10 Check): an outsider holding copies can test it.

Agreements relies on both and adds no rule for them. One consequence is a design constraint on everything below: **every grammar rule here is checked from copies of acts alone**, plus the answers of a named place where rule 6 applies. No rule needs a particular server, keeper, platform or software author to remain. Reputation follows the same constraint (task K13).

## 2. Definitions

Each word here means one thing throughout this text.

- **Act**: a signed statement in the core's sense. Published or signed again, never updated. The fields the core gives every act (who signed, the signature, the act's own fingerprint) are not repeated here.
- **Fingerprint**: the identifier the core gives to fixed bytes. "Named by fingerprint" means referred to by that identifier and nothing else.
- **Grammar rule**: one of the thirteen rules of section 4. It says how acts are read. It never says what people must agree. Written "rule 1" to "rule 13".
- **Task**: one question the grammar asks and does not answer itself. A task says what an answer to it accepts, what it produces, and whom it makes you trust. The fourteen tasks are K1 to K14 (section 5). The grammar rules may require that some task be answered; they never answer it.
- **cMIP**: a document, named by fingerprint, that answers one task, for some kind of thing or situation. Anyone may write one. Many cMIPs may answer the same task in different ways (one for land, another for shares). Choosing a cMIP is how people choose their own rules.
- **The research's 49 tasks** (#1 to #49): the questions an agreement may have to answer, from `agreement-tasks.md`. They are a checklist of what can go wrong, not this text's tasks. Where each one lands in this text is recorded in `companion-2.md`.
- **Terms**: an act that states what is agreed. Always capitalised and singular ("a Terms act", "these Terms"). Stating Terms is not agreeing to them.
- **Statement**: any other act made within an agreement. Its **kind** says what it is (see task K11). One kind is defined here: **assent**.
- **Assent**: a Statement by which its signer agrees to one Terms act.
- **Agreement**: the first Terms act together with every Terms act that replaces it, directly or through others, and every Statement made within them. Its identifier is the fingerprint of the first Terms act (the **root**).
- **Version**: one Terms act of an agreement. The first is the root; each later one replaces the one before (rule 3.8). The **last version** is the one in force (rule 3.6; while a fork of Terms stands, the one both branches replace, rule 3.7).
- **Never ended**: an agreement never ends. It only gets a new version, under the Terms' own rule (rules 3.8, 3.9), and goes on as its last version. This text has no defined term for ending.
- **Party**: one named in Terms. Named in one of three ways:
  - as a **signer**: a person, by the key the Sign layer gives them;
  - as a **role**: a spot for someone not yet known, linked to a signer later (rule 9);
  - as an **agreement**: another agreement, by its root (rule 10).

  Signers, and signers who fill a role, are the parties who may be bound. An agreement named as a party is never bound.
- **Work**: an agreement whose Terms say who owns something and in what shares. A plain word for a use of the grammar, not a separate thing in it: every rule reads a work as it reads any agreement.
- **Shares**: what an agreement's Terms say each party's part is. The grammar gives them no form: they are read by the rule (task K4) the Terms name for dividing. An agreement with shares defines as much or as little of how it governs itself as it chooses (powers, rule 4); the grammar has no separate notion of a collective.
- **Service**: a signer who does a job for an agreement (dividing what it is paid, holding a payment) under a service agreement. A hand, in the research's sense (task K8).
- **Service agreement**: an agreement between a service and another agreement named as a party (the work), which the work's own Terms point to in `service` (rule 10.6). A deal between the service and the work as a party.
- **Rail**: one way of paying, as the layer that holds value gives it. Named here, not defined.
- **Address**: where something is paid on one rail. A service agreement gives the address at which its work is paid; a person's addresses are given by that person's home.
- **Home**: where an identity publishes its own pointers, among them its address on each rail. Named here, not defined.
- **Discharge**: a service paying on what its work was paid, divided by the work's shares. The grammar reads only the service's signed word of it (rule 10.9).
- **Spot**: a role's place among the parties, before and after it is filled. It has a **kind**: **done**, **received**, **holding**, **fits** or **future** (rule 9). A later version may mark it **closed**: it then takes no new identities, and keeps the fillings already earned (rule 9.5); a version after that may reopen it, which touches no earned filling (rule 9.7).
- **Fill**: a spot is filled when the cMIP its role names (task K12) says that a signer is linked to it by a proof: the acts that earn it. Each filling is tied to the proof that earned it. A spot may be filled many times; whether it holds one filling or many is its cMIP's (rule 9.2).
- **Rights**: what a version offers whoever earns a filling under it, with every condition that version puts on it. The grammar does not say what they are, or read them (rule 9.6).
- **Receiver**: the one party that Terms name, in the field `receiver`, as receiving on the agreement's behalf when another agreement names it as a party and the agreement points to no service agreement (rule 10.3, 10.4).
- **Author**: a party whose words the Terms are.
- **Power**: an entry in Terms saying that a Statement of a given kind (or a replacing Terms act) binds the parties when a given rule says yes.
- **Rule**: a cMIP answering task K4, named by fingerprint, with its parameters. It is the people's own rule, not a grammar rule. It computes; it trusts nobody.
- **Seen**: being rewarded for being seen means being owed something per play, view, repost, delivery to an audience or other count of attention (rule 11).
- **Settlement**: the signed evidence, from the layer that holds value (Control), that a payment was made. Agreements names it and does not define it.
- **Line**: acts of which each names the one before it: one signer's acts within an agreement, by `previous` (rule 5); an agreement's Terms, by `replaces` (rule 3); and any other acts the grammar reads that way (rule 12.1).
- **Fork**: two acts that each count as the next act after the same act of one line (rule 12.1). The act both follow is the **shared act**; each of the two, with what follows it in the line, is a **branch**.
- **Join**: an act that ends a fork by naming every branch and saying which continues, or by merging them (rule 12.3).
- **Arbiter**: a cMIP answering task K14, named by fingerprint in Terms in advance, whose answer ends a fork as a join does (rule 12.4). Draft 5 and draft 6 called it a resolver.
- **Dropped**: a branch is dropped when a join or an arbiter ends its fork and it is not the branch that continues (rule 12.3, 12.4).
- **Bound**: a person is bound by an act when rule 4 says so.
- **In force**: Terms are in force when rule 3 says so.
- **Place**: an ordered place to look, provided by the Order layer, which can say of an act whether it is there and at what position, and whether anything is later.
- **Name** (or **cite**): an act names another act, or a version, when it carries its fingerprint in one of its fields. What an act names is what it is read against (rule 13).
- **Anchoring**: whatever a reader accepts as saying that nothing is later than an act, or what time it was. A place is an anchoring; the place an agreement's Terms name is the one its parties agreed. Each reader accepts the anchoring it chooses (rule 13.4).
- **Verifier**: whoever applies these grammar rules to a set of acts. A verifier answers questions; it does not decide disputes.
- **Answers**: a verifier's answer is one of:
  - **shown**: the acts held establish it under these grammar rules;
  - **not shown**: the acts held do not establish it;
  - **fork**: two acts are a fork (rule 12): two acts by one signer name the same previous act (rule 5.2), or two Terms acts each replace the same Terms (rules 3.7, 3.8);
  - **void**: a filling counted, and still is one, but its rights have ended under the rule its own version names (rule 9.8). Void is a state, always given with its cause. It is not the answer for an act that never counted: that stays **not shown**.
  - **unchecked**: the verifier could not apply a rule it needed (rule 8).

  Answers about what is latest, what else exists, or what time it was, carry a qualifier: **per anchoring**, with the anchoring named (rule 13.4), written **per place** where the anchoring is the place the Terms name; or **as shown** (meaning: among the acts this verifier happens to hold). An answer that rests on what someone relied on in a dropped branch also carries the qualifier **disputed** (rule 12.5).

## 4. Grammar rules

Each grammar rule is given in plain words, then precisely, then with its two answers and the reasoning. MUST, SHOULD and MAY are used as in RFC 2119. No SHOULD appears: every rule that survived the two questions turned out to be either required or a free choice.

These rules say how acts are read. None of them says what people must agree.

### Rule 1. Whole

**Plain words.** Everything the agreement uses is pinned by fingerprint. A link to something that could change is not part of the agreement.

**Precisely.**
- 1.1 The `body`, every piece in `relies_on`, and every cMIP named anywhere in a Terms act MUST be named by fingerprint.
- 1.2 Anything a Terms act refers to in another way is not part of those Terms. A verifier MUST NOT read it as part of them.

**Lie exposed.** "The document we referred to said something else", and "there was a side piece". A signature over words that point at something changeable proves only that the pointer was signed. (#34 Whole, #1 Fix.)

**Protects.** Every reader who arrives later: newcomer, heir, buyer of a part, judge. Also the party who signed without fetching what was referred to. None of them chose how the reference was written.

**Reasoning.** A cMIP can be revised; a document can be edited; a name can come to mean something new. Naming cMIPs by fingerprint is what lets an agreement be read correctly after its cMIP's author is gone, which is the good-ancestor requirement applied to meaning. Rule 1 does not prove that nothing else exists outside the Terms; that is rule 6. An agreement named as a party is named by its root, which is a fingerprint; what it says *now* is not pinned by rule 1, and is read by rule 10.

### Rule 2. Author

**Plain words.** The agreement says whose words these are. If the words turn out to be unclear, the unclearness falls on them.

**Precisely.**
- 2.1 `authors` MUST name one or more of the parties named as signers or roles.
- 2.2 The authors are the parties who bear the failure of the words to mean one thing (#4 Align). A cMIP answering task K10 (Judge) MUST accept `authors` as an input.

**Lie exposed.** "We wrote this together" or "I meant something else by that word", said by the one who did write it. Signatures alone cannot show who wrote the words: every party signs the same Terms.

**Protects.** The party who accepted words they did not write, above all whoever takes up an open offer or fills a spot (rule 9), who never had a chance to change the words.

**Reasoning.** Whether two people meant the same thing is an inner state at a past moment; no operation reaches it (`agreement-grammar.md`: Mind). The research's answer is "do not try to prove; assign in advance who bears the failure", and the open offer shows the assignment: whoever writes the words carries their ambiguity. Rule 2 makes the assignment readable; what a judge does with it is the judge's task. It assigns Align only. Able (#3) and Free (#5) are not assigned by any rule; see section 7.

### Rule 3. In force

**Plain words.** Signing first does not bind you alone. Your assent counts only once all the assents the agreement asks for are present.

*Rules 3.5 to 3.7 from S15; rule 3.7 rewritten and rule 3.8 added in draft 5, from S20.* Terms stop being in force when Terms that replace them are in force. New Terms replace old ones only if they carry the signatures the old Terms require: everyone's, unless the old Terms gave a power to fewer. New Terms without those signatures replace nothing, and the old Terms simply go on. If the agreement names a place, the place says which Terms are the latest; if it names none, a verifier can say only which are latest among the acts it holds, "as shown". If two Terms acts each replace the same Terms and each carries those signatures, that is a fork (rule 12): a verifier shows both, and never picks one. While the fork stands, neither branch is in force: the Terms both replaced stay in force, and everything that rests on the Terms in force (who receives now, who fills a spot, how a rule divides) is answered from them, until a join or the agreement's arbiter ends the fork.

*From S21, in draft 6; reworded in draft 7 (W).* An agreement never ends. It only gets a new version: one with new conditions replaces the last one as any version does, carrying the signatures the last one requires: the Terms rule, as they were agreed by all who signed them. What happens to the work and its shares is in the new version's conditions; the grammar does not list the ways, and has no word for ending. Until such a version is in force, the last version stays in force and keeps protecting everyone in it: whoever acts against it alone, without the signatures it requires, has made nothing that replaces it, and must make a new version, signed as it requires.

**Precisely.**
- 3.1 Terms are in force when the assents their `in_force` rule requires are present. If `in_force` is absent, they are in force when there is an assent to them in the name of every party named as a signer.
- 3.2 Terms that name a party by role MUST carry `in_force`.
- 3.3 An assent binds its signer only while the Terms it names are in force.
- 3.4 A party named as an agreement never assents and is never counted by 3.1 (rule 10).
- 3.5 Terms stop being in force when Terms that replace them (rule 4.2, 3.8) are in force. While a fork of them stands (3.7), no Terms that replace them are in force.
- 3.6 Which Terms of an agreement are in force is an answer about what is latest. A verifier MUST take it per the agreement's place, qualified **per place** (rule 6.2); or, where no place is named, per the anchoring the reader accepts, with it named, or else **as shown** (rules 6.3, 13.4). It MUST NOT give it as latest without saying what the answer stands on.
- 3.7 Where two Terms acts each replace the same Terms (3.8), and each has the assents its own `in_force` requires (3.1), they are a fork (rule 12); a join is not one of them (rule 12.3). A verifier MUST answer **fork** for which Terms replace them, show both branches, and MUST NOT choose between them, by position or in any other way. Until the fork ends (rule 12.3, 12.4), the Terms both branches replace stay in force, and a verifier MUST answer from them every answer that rests on the Terms in force: among others, a receiver now (rule 10.4), who fills a spot (rule 9.2), the shares a discharge is read against (rule 10.9), and what a rule the Terms name produces (rule 4, task K4). It MUST show the fork with each such answer, per place or as shown.
- 3.8 A Terms act replaces the Terms its `replaces` names only if it carries the signatures those Terms require for their replacement (rule 4.2): an assent to it in the name of every party bound by them, or what a power for replacement in them requires. A Terms act that does not carry them replaces nothing: the Terms it names stay in force, and it is not a branch of a fork.
- 3.9 Terms stop being in force only as 3.5 says: when a version replacing them is in force. No act ends Terms, or an agreement, without replacing them, and a verifier MUST NOT answer that Terms or an agreement have ended. An agreement only gets a new version: a verifier reads every replacing version alike (rules 3.8, 4.2), whatever its conditions say becomes of the work and its shares; what they say is theirs (rule 1, task K1). Every version replaced stays an act of the agreement, readable as it was (rule 1).

**Lie exposed.** "You signed, so you are bound", said by the party who never signed. Signed text shows that one side signed; it cannot by itself show that the signature was conditional on the other's. And (3.5 to 3.7) "these Terms still hold", said of Terms already replaced, and "these are the Terms now", said of one of two replacements while the other is hidden. Each Terms act is signed and looks valid on its own; only the line of replacements, read per place or as shown, says which stopped, and two branches of it convict each other as a fork does. And (3.8) "these are our new Terms", said by some parties over the heads of others whose signatures the old Terms required. And (3.9) "this agreement is over", said by one party to escape what its last version still says, and "I can do this alone now", said against a version that requires others' signatures.

**Protects.** The first to sign, who did not choose to be bound alone. And whoever reads the agreement later and is shown an old version, or one branch of two: the payer looking for the receiver now, the newcomer, the judge. And (3.7, 3.8) the party left out of a replacement they never signed, and everyone who would otherwise be stuck with no Terms in force while a fork stands. And (3.9) everyone in the last version, which keeps binding until those whose signatures it requires agree a new one.

**Reasoning.** "Each party must hold the other's commitment, not their own" (#7 Exchange). Exchange may run one way until the other side acts, which is why 3.2 requires Terms with a spot to say how acceptance is recognised instead of falling back to a default that cannot apply to a role. Withdrawing an assent before the Terms are in force is a race; see section 7 and P10.

Draft 3 said when Terms come into force and nothing about when they stop, and the tests found that everything resting on "Terms in force" then had two answers (`situations-1.md`, F1, F4; test 2, N1). Nobody, allegedly, on old Terms stopping: "that’s where a whole lot of special cases were born if I remember right", and, asked by the MOR lead whether Terms stop being in force when Terms replacing them are in force, two competing replacements being a fork that is shown and never chosen: "Yes, less likely to bloat and overdefine." So rules 3.5 to 3.7 are one rule, read three ways: what stops (3.5), where the answer comes from (3.6), and what two answers mean (3.7). Replacing Terms come into force as any Terms do (3.1, or through a power in the Terms they replace, rule 4.2), and, from draft 5, replace only with the signatures the replaced Terms require (3.8); until then the replaced Terms stay in force. With 3.3, an assent to replaced Terms stops binding its signer once the replacing Terms are in force; the signer is then bound by the replacing Terms only through rule 4.2. Rule 3.7 uses the answer rule 5 already gives a signer who told two stories, now for an agreement whose Terms branch: it is shown, and nobody's software picks a branch. Draft 5 kept ending Terms without replacing them, withdrawing an assent, and withdrawing an open offer outside this rule, as draft 3 had them (P10, section 9). Draft 6 brings in the first and the third, from S21 (rule 3.9) and S22 (rule 9.5); withdrawing an assent stays as draft 5 has it (P10).

Draft 4 answered **fork** for which Terms are in force, and so for everything resting on them, with nothing to end it. Draft 5 changes two things, from S20. First, on the MOR lead's scenario of two replacements each signed by two of three parties, Nobody, allegedly: "Both term 2a and 2b are invalid as they do not carry the required signatures that were in term 1." So rule 3.8 says a replacement replaces only with the signatures the replaced Terms require; with Terms that require everyone, two replacements signed by different two-thirds are not a fork at all, and Terms 1 stays in force. Second, asked whether, for a real fork (the Terms let fewer than all replace them, or the same people sign two replacements), the Terms both branches replaced stay in force until a join or an arbiter (then called a resolver) settles it: "It does, and more sense than anyone ending up stuck in the first one". So rule 3.7 keeps the fork shown and unchosen, and answers what rests on the Terms in force from the Terms both branches replaced. How a fork ends is rule 12, for every kind of act.

Draft 6 adds rule 3.9, from S21. Asked by the MOR lead whether ending an agreement is a replacement by Terms that say "ended", Nobody, allegedly, began: "That agreement can be ended in multiple ways… what happens to the work and its shares draws different ends"; then: "An agreement never ends, it can resolve. It’s a new version of the agreement with new conditions signed by all signers of previous version"; and "« we agree to resolve the previous agreement » is really what happens. The agreement stays and is useful." Asked whether resolving needs everyone who signed the previous version even where the Terms let fewer change them: "Terms rule, because they were agreed by all signers". Read as one train of thought, the last answer says who signs such a version: those the version before it requires, which is what rule 3.8 already says. So it needs no new act, field or answer: it is a replacement whose conditions say what becomes of the work and its shares, and the "multiple ways" stay in those conditions, not in the grammar. What 3.9 adds is that nothing else stops Terms: there is no "ended" for a verifier to compute. His example shows why: the work made public and free by all, then one of them selling it alone, and "the previous agreement says « sorry can’t do this alone, the other two signatures are needed »". The version that made it public and free is still in force (3.5); a new version without the signatures it requires replaces nothing (3.8); and a sale binds the others only through a power that version gives (rule 4). A sale made outside the agreement, in another one, is seen only as draft 5 says (section 7, "Ownership is as wide as its place").

Draft 7 keeps rule 3.9 and drops the word, from W. Asked by the MOR lead whether to rename "resolve" or "resolver", Nobody, allegedly: "I use the language I use because it feels right in the moment I use. The word resolve was the word I found at that moment to try to nudge the conversation". So "resolve" is not a term of this text: an agreement only gets a new version, under the Terms' own rule, and nothing in the rule changes.

### Rule 4. Own signature

**Plain words.** Nobody is bound by anything they did not sign. The only exception is an agreement they did sign that gives someone a power over them, and then the act must name that agreement at the time it is made, not afterwards.

**Precisely.**
- 4.1 A person is bound by an act if, and only if:
  - (a) they signed it in their own name, or
  - (b) the act's `for` names them, its `under` names Terms by which they are bound and which are in force, those Terms give a power for acts of this kind, and that power's rule, applied to the acts held, says yes.
- 4.2 A replacing Terms act (one with `replaces`) binds a party only through 4.1: by that party's own assent to it, or by a power for replacement in the Terms it replaces. Its `replaces` is read as its `under`.
- 4.3 An act with no `for` and no `under` is made in its signer's own name only. It MUST NOT later be counted as made for anyone else.
- 4.4 Where the person named in `for` is bound only through Terms that have been replaced, a verifier MUST answer from rule 6: per place if the Terms name one, otherwise as shown. (Whether Terms have been replaced is now answered by rules 3.5 to 3.8; from draft 6, Terms are not ended otherwise, rule 3.9.)

**Lie exposed.** "She bound you", "the group decided", "I was acting for him" (said after the fact), "I was still authorised". A signature shows who signed; it cannot show that the signer could bind someone else. The pointer in `under` lets anyone check the power against the principal's own signature. (#27 Authorise, #28 Scope, #29 Name, #30 Decide, #14 Admit, #15 Bind, #47 Amend, #48 End.)

**Protects.** The person bound through someone else: the principal, the outvoted member, the absent party. Also the counterparty of an agent, who can check the power before relying on it.

**Reasoning.** "A group deciding and a person acting for another are the same: a few bind others, within limits. Amending an agreement is one more decision of that kind" (`agreement-tasks.md`, what collapsed). So one rule covers agency, groups, judges, admission and amendment. A grant of authority is itself Terms: the principal is author and party, the agent is party, and the power names the kinds of act and the rule that limits them. Revoking it is a new version of those Terms, signed as they require (rule 3.9).

A newcomer is bound (#15 Bind) by their own assent to the Terms in force when they join; their assent names exactly that Terms act, so they are not bound by a version they never saw, except through powers in it they accepted. Filling a spot (rule 9) does not bind anyone either: the signer who fills it is bound only by their own assent, under 4.1.

There is no default power. An agreement that names no power to replace itself can be replaced (rule 3.9) only by every party's own assent. This is the conservative way out, and its cost is in section 7.

### Rule 5. Own chain

**Plain words.** Each person's acts in one agreement are a single line: each one points at that person's previous one. If two acts point at the same previous one, that person has told two stories, and that is proven without a judge.

**Precisely.**
- 5.1 Every act within an agreement MUST name in `previous` its signer's previous act within the same agreement, or none if it is the signer's first.
- 5.2 Two acts by one signer within one agreement that name the same `previous` (or that both name none) are a fork. A verifier holding both MUST answer **fork** for that signer, and MUST NOT choose between the branches. The fork ends only as rule 12 says.

**Lie exposed.** Showing one history to one person and another history to another (equivocation), and hiding an act in the middle of one's own line. Without a single shared sequence, two signed acts can each look like the latest; the pointer turns the contradiction into proof (`agreement-grammar.md`, second test: "a fork convicts itself").

**Protects.** The stranger who is shown only one branch, and any party who has no place to look.

**Reasoning.** Rule 5 is checkable by anyone holding the two acts, with no outsider. It does not stop the lie, it makes it provable afterwards, which deters only where the liar is known and has something to lose (section 7). What a proven fork costs its signer is their standing with whoever sees it, which anyone may compute (task K13). It cannot reveal that a signer's line has a later act that was not shown; only rule 6 answers "nothing later". What a fork removes from later answers, while it stands, is open (P9); how it ends is rule 12.

### Rule 6. Place to look

**Plain words.** An agreement may name one place where its acts are lodged in order. Then the questions "is this the latest?", "is this all of it?" and "who holds this now?" have answers, and anything not lodged there does not count. Without a place, nobody can prove that nothing else exists, and a verifier must say so. (From S27, in draft 7: a reader may accept an anchoring of its own choosing for those questions; the answer then says which.)

**Precisely.**
- 6.1 Terms MAY name a `place`.
- 6.2 Where the Terms in force name a place, an act within the agreement counts for any answer about what is latest (including which Terms are in force, rule 3.6), what is whole, who holds what, whether a period has passed, or whether something was made public, only once the place shows it, and at the position the place gives it. A verifier MUST take those answers from the place, qualified **per place**.
- 6.3 Where no place is named, a verifier MUST qualify every such answer **as shown**, or **per anchoring** where the reader accepts one, with it named (rule 13.4). It MUST NOT give such an answer as latest or whole without saying what it stands on.

**Lie exposed.** "There is nothing later", "there is no side piece", "nobody else holds it", "you were told" (by publication), "the deadline has passed". A document cannot prove an absence; only a single place to look can (`agreement-tasks.md`, what collapsed: Current, Whole, Alive). (#33 Current, #34 Whole, #11 Title, #12 Sum, #13 Transfer, #32 Notify, #36 Wait.)

**Protects.** Everyone who arrives later and must rely on "nothing else exists": the newcomer, the buyer of a part, the heir, the judge, the counterparty of an agent whose grant may have been replaced.

**Reasoning.** This is the closed-world rule ("what is not in the register does not count"), which the research calls a design decision, not a discovery. It is made local: each agreement names its own place, so "a thing's ownership is exactly as global as its witness set". Rule 6 is a MAY because a bilateral agreement about deeds barely needs a shared sequence; the research finds that one is needed for exactly two things, scarcity and freshness. Rule 6.3 is the price of not naming one, stated to the reader instead of hidden. Changing the place is a replacement of Terms and goes through rule 4. Rule 10 asks of another agreement which of its versions is in force; that answer is per *that* agreement's place, or per the anchoring the reader accepts (rule 13.4). Draft 7 (S27) reads a place as one anchoring among those a reader may accept: "anyone accept the anchoring they are willing to accept". Rules 6.1 and 6.2 are unchanged: the place the Terms name is the anchoring the parties agreed, and what counts between them is what is found there. Whether a reader may set that place aside for another anchoring is not covered by his words, and is open (P13).

### Rule 7. Say who said it

**Plain words.** Whether a thing was delivered, whether it was what was promised, how much there was, whether someone is alive, what time it was, whether money moved, who filled a spot, how often something was seen: an agreement can record who said so, never make it so. A verifier always reports who said it.

**Precisely.**
- 7.1 For any answer about the world (a deed done, a thing's quality or quantity, an event, a person's presence or absence), about time, or about value under Control, a verifier MUST report who stated it: the one who performed, the one who received, a named witness, the controller, or the place.
- 7.2 A time written inside an act is reported as its signer's claim. Only a place gives a position. What time it was is answered only per an anchoring the reader accepts, with it named (rule 13.4).
- 7.3 A verifier MUST NOT report value as moved on the strength of an Agreements act alone. An act can instruct Control; whether Control obeyed is the controller's or the receiver's signed word.
- 7.4 Who fills a spot (rule 9), how often something was seen (rule 11), and what a service paid on (rule 10.9), are answers about the world. A verifier MUST report them with whoever confirmed them.

**Lie exposed.** A claim laundered into a fact: "it was delivered" when only the deliverer said so; a backdated act; "you were paid" when only the payer says so; "I am the one who did it" when only the claimant says so. Signed text proves that the signer said it, and nothing more; this rule stops a verifier's answer from saying more than that.

**Protects.** The receiver, who did not choose to be declared paid or served, and every reader who would otherwise take a verifier's summary as fact.

**Reasoning.** The research's largest residue is truth: whenever an agreement touches the world, "someone must say what happened, and their signature proves only that they said it". And "the record cannot hold value": every entry about value is a claim or an instruction, neither is the value. Rule 7 does not rank sources; whether a receiver's acknowledgment (#20) or three witnesses suffice is a rule (task K4) chosen in the Terms.

### Rule 8. Unchecked is not passed

**Plain words.** If a verifier lacks something it needs to check (a cMIP it does not have, a place it cannot reach, the acts of an agreement named as a party), it says "unchecked". It never says "fine" or "broken" instead.

**Precisely.**
- 8.1 Where a verifier cannot apply a rule it needs, because it lacks a cMIP named by fingerprint, lacks an act named by fingerprint, lacks the acts of an agreement named as a party, or cannot get an answer from a named place, it MUST answer **unchecked** for every answer that depends on it.

**Lie exposed.** "My software says it checks out", from software that could not check. Signed text cannot expose what a verifier silently skipped.

**Protects.** Anyone relying on someone else's verification, and the parties of an agreement whose cMIP has become rare.

**Reasoning.** Because every agreement can name any cMIP, verifiers will always meet ones they do not have. Without rule 8, the convenient path is to treat the unknown as passing. With it, an agreement written today stays honestly readable when its cMIPs are old.

### Rule 9. Spots

*From S6, S7 and S8; rule 9.2 rewritten in draft 4 from S16, and the plain words from S17; rules 9.6 to 9.8 added in draft 7 from S26.*

**Plain words.** Some participants are not known when the agreement is written. The agreement keeps a spot for each of them, and says what earns it: having done something, having received something from the one before, holding something, or being someone who fits a description. Whoever proves it fills the spot, once for each proof: the same spot can be earned again by a later proof, and each filling is tied to the proof that earned it. Whether a spot holds one person or many is for the spot's cMIP to say. For those nobody could foresee in any of these four ways, a fifth, open kind of spot lets a cMIP say both who the participant is and how they are confirmed. The grammar does not say how anything is proven: the cMIP the spot names does. What proves holding, or any other kind, is the cMIP's; the grammar names no use of a spot. It only says that the spot, and its cMIP, are fixed in advance, and that each filling is reported with its proof and whoever confirmed it.

*From S22, in draft 6.* An open offer does not end. Its spot can be **closed** to new identities, by a new version made as the Terms require: from then on nobody new fills it, and everyone who already filled it stays in it, so that they can still point to a valid agreement, the version in force, that says so.

*From S26, in draft 7.* Whoever earned a filling has the rights the agreement offered when they earned it: exactly those, with whatever conditions that version put on them (a time limit, a creator's statement, anything its cMIPs read). The grammar does not care what the conditions are. No later version changes them unless the holder signs it. A closed spot can be opened again to new people, by a new version; that touches nothing already earned. And a version can give a verifier what it needs to say that rights have ended: it names, in advance, the rule under which they end. When that rule says so, the verifier answers **void**, and shows the cause beside it. Void is a state a filling is in. It is not the same as an act that never counted, for which "invalid" stays the word.

**Precisely.**
- 9.1 A role in `parties` MUST name:
  - its **kind**, one of: **done** (whoever proves they have done X), **received** (whoever proves they have received Y), **holding** (whoever proves they are holding Z), **fits** (whoever proves their identity fits the conditions in W), or **future**;
  - its `what`: the X, Y, Z or W, by fingerprint (rule 1), except for kind future (9.4);
  - the cMIP answering task K12 that says who fills it, by fingerprint.
- 9.2 A spot is filled, by a signer, when its K12 cMIP, applied to the acts held and the place's answers, names that signer on the strength of a **proof**: the acts that earn the spot. A spot MAY be filled many times; each filling is tied to the proof that earned it. Whether a spot holds one filling or many is said by its K12 cMIP. A verifier MUST answer each filling with its proof, each act reported by who signed it (rule 7.4), qualified per place or as shown (rule 6). The role and its cMIP are read from the Terms in force; while a fork of Terms stands, from the Terms both branches replace (rule 3.7).
- 9.3 Each signer who fills a spot is a party in it from then on. They are bound only by their own assent (rule 4). Filling a spot does not bind any other party.
- 9.4 A role of kind **future** names no `what`: its K12 cMIP defines who the participant is as well as how they are confirmed. It is for participants none of the four kinds can describe in advance. No verifier can check that a future spot was used only for them; whether it was is the authors' word, carried by them (rule 2).
- 9.5 *From S22, in draft 6.* A version MAY mark a role `closed`, keeping the role in `parties`; who must sign that version is said by rule 3.8. A closed spot is filled by no signer who had not filled it while a version before the closing one was in force. Each such earlier filling, with its proof, stays a filling, and its signer a party (9.3), in the closing version. Whether a signer already in a closed spot may fill it again by a later proof is said, as before, by its K12 cMIP (9.2). Which fillings came before the closing version was in force is an answer about what is latest: per place, or as shown (rule 6).
- 9.6 *From S26, in draft 7.* **Rights.** A filling has exactly the rights offered by the version it was earned under, with every condition that version puts on them. The version a filling was earned under is the version its proof names (rule 13.1). What the rights and their conditions are is that version's (rule 1, task K1); these grammar rules do not read them. No later version changes the rights of a filling already earned unless it carries an assent to it signed by that filling's holder in their own name (rule 4.1(a)). A verifier MUST name, with each filling, the version whose offer it holds: the version it was earned under, or a later one only where it holds the holder's own assent to that later version.
- 9.7 *From S26, in draft 7.* **Reopening.** A version MAY remove `closed` from a role; who must sign that version is said by rule 3.8. From then on the spot is filled as 9.2 says. Reopening changes nothing for a filling already earned (9.6).
- 9.8 *From S26, in draft 7.* **Void.** Terms MAY name in `void` a rule (task K4), by fingerprint (rule 1), under which the rights that version offers end. Where the rule named by the version a filling was earned under (9.6), applied to that filling, the acts held and the answers of the anchoring, says that its rights have ended, a verifier MUST answer **void** for that filling, and MUST give the cause beside it, as the rule gives it: an act, reported by who signed it (rule 7), or a time, per the anchoring named or as shown (rule 13.4). A void filling stays a filling, shown with its proof (9.2): void is a state, not an undoing. A verifier MUST NOT answer void under a rule the filling's own version does not name (9.6). It MUST NOT answer void for an act that never counted (Terms never in force, a replacement that replaces nothing, a claim that fills no spot): that is answered as before, **not shown**. Where the verifier lacks the rule, the answer is **unchecked** (rule 8).

**Lie exposed.** "I am the one who did it", "it was passed to me", "I hold it", "I am one of them", said after the fact, with a confirmer chosen after the fact. Without a spot fixed in advance, the one who pays decides afterwards who earned it, and the claimant cannot check that the test was the one promised (`late-roles.md`: "The payer as sole confirmer appears in the affiliate and bounty cases"). With it, the test is pinned by fingerprint before anyone claims, and the answer always says on whose word the spot was filled. And (9.5) "the offer was withdrawn, so your filling is gone", said to someone who earned the spot before it closed. And (9.6 to 9.8) "the terms have changed, so what you bought is gone", said by an author over a later version the holder never signed; and "that licence never counted", said of one that counted and then ended, or "it still holds", said of one whose own version says it has ended.

**Protects.** Whoever earns a spot and was not there to sign: the referrer, the heir, the holder, the later contributor, the beneficiary. Also every other party, who agreed in advance to the test a stranger must pass. And (9.5) whoever filled a spot before it closed, who keeps a valid agreement to point to. And (9.6 to 9.8) whoever paid for or earned rights, who keeps exactly what was offered when they earned it; and the later reader, who is told whether a filling never counted, or counted and ended, and why.

**Reasoning.** `late-roles.md` finds that about eighteen recorded roles link to a person in only four ways: "by **doing** something, by **receiving** from the previous holder, by **holding** an object, or by **being** someone who fits a description". Nobody, allegedly's four spots name the same four. The kinds are in the grammar because they say what sort of answer a verifier is reporting (a deed, a chain, a holding, an identity) and so whom it rests on; *how* each is proven is the K12 cMIP's question, since that is something cMIPs will solve. The fifth kind exists because the research's section 4 shows roles no agreement anticipated (the unknowing source, the rescuer, the owed but unfindable, the contributor recognised afterwards, the early user), where "someone with judgment decides afterwards". A future spot lets an agreement name that judgment in advance, by its cMIP. Rule 3.2 already requires Terms with a spot to say how they come into force.

Draft 3 linked a spot to one signer and left open whether it could hold many (P7); test 2 found that a seat earned again with every payment could be answered only by reading one payment among "the acts held", which K12 did not name (N2). Asked by the MOR lead whether a spot is earned per proof, not once, Nobody, allegedly, answered: "Yes, it leverages the above discussion as intended I think". So 9.2 ties each filling to its proof, and the same spot can be earned again by a later proof without a new rule; how many fillings a spot holds is its cMIP's. Asked where holding stops and being seen starts, Nobody, allegedly, answered: "I don’t think it should be defined, what matters is “HOLD” with cMIP defined what the proof is, no?" So no use of a kind is named here, and where holding stops and being seen starts (rule 11) is not defined by the grammar.

Draft 5 left open how an open offer is withdrawn, and whether one whose spot no one can sign for can be ended at all (P10; `situations-1.md`, F9). Nobody, allegedly, on withdrawing an open offer: "The offer does not end, it is simply not open to new IDs anymore. Whoever bought the rights should still be able to point to a valid agreement that says so". So 9.5 adds no withdrawal: closing is a new version (rule 3.9), and the only thing it changes for the spot is that no new identity fills it. Who signs it is who the Terms require (rule 3.8; S21: "Terms rule"). The fillings already earned stay fillings in the closing version, so the version in force still says so; the versions before it stay readable too (rule 3.9).

Draft 6 left open whether a later version may undo a closed spot or remove the fillings in it (draft 6, section 9.8, item 2). Asked by the MOR lead whether an offer's author could sign that earlier licences no longer count, Nobody, allegedly: "Whoever paid the agreement has the rights the agreement offer." Asked whether that version can itself limit the rights: "That version could have conditions tied to time, creators statements… bla bla bla… core does not care". So 9.6 says only whose offer a filling holds (its own version's), and reads none of the conditions. The lead then read it back (exactly the rights its version offers, conditions included; no later version changing them without the holder's own signature), and he answered: "Exactly. So the agreement should have a say to give a verifier the ingredients to provide VOID as a reply, no?" So 9.8 gives the agreement that say: a version names the rule under which its rights end, and the verifier answers **void** when the rule says so. The grammar still does not care what the conditions are; it only reports that the version's own rule says the rights have ended, and why. On the lead's distinction between invalid (the act never counted) and void (it counted, and its rights ended by its own version's conditions): "Yes, that distinction matters. Lasped feels associated to time. Void to state". So void is written as a state, whatever the cause, with the cause beside it; and an act that never counted is never answered void. Reopening (9.7) is not in his words: it is the lead's, in the arrived-at line, and follows from rule 3.8 (who signs a version) and 9.6 (nothing earned is touched); section 9.10 lists it.

### Rule 10. An agreement as a party

*From S5. In draft 7: renamed, and rules 10.6 to 10.9 added, from S23 and S24; rule 10.4 reads "now" as rule 13 says, from S27. This is the grammar's central idea. It keeps the number 10, and rules 10.1 to 10.5 keep theirs, so that earlier references still point where they did.*

**Plain words.** An agreement can name another agreement (a song, a library, a drawing, anything with its own agreement) as one of its parties. Nobody signs on its behalf, and no group has to be formed. The grammar has no notion of a collective apart from this: an agreement with shares is all a collective is, and it defines as much or as little of how it governs itself as it chooses. Works, services and their deals are built in the same way.

The payment chain is the first thing built with it. A **work** is an agreement that says who its owners are and what share each has. A **service** that divides money makes a deal with the work: a **service agreement**, between the service and the work as a party. The work agreement points to its service agreement. The service agreement gives the address at which the work is paid on each rail, and the service's fee, flat or a percentage. The payer pays the work, at that address, and needs to know nothing else: not the owners, not their shares. When the service pays out (at discharge), it divides by the work agreement's shares and identities; each owner's address on each rail comes from that owner's own home. An owner that is itself an agreement is paid as any work is, at the address its own service agreement gives. That is how a series of splits is possible: each one pays into the next, by its own signed words.

The grammar says only what a verifier reads of this chain: the two pointers, the address, the fee, the shares, and the service's signed word that it paid on. How a service works is its cMIP's.

A work whose owners chose not to point to a split may name a **receiver** instead, as in draft 6: one of its parties, who receives for it.

**Precisely.**
- 10.1 Terms MAY name a party as an agreement, by its root fingerprint.
- 10.2 A party named as an agreement never assents, is never counted by rule 3.1, and is never bound by any act of these Terms. No signer acts for it here, and none is needed.
- 10.3 Terms MAY name a `receiver`: one of their own parties, named as a signer or as a role.
- 10.4 **Receiver.** Where the version read names a `receiver`, whatever an act or a rule of one agreement gives or owes to a party named as an agreement, it gives or owes to that agreement's **receiver now**: the `receiver` named by the version of that agreement which the act that gives or owes names (rule 13.1). Where that act names no version, a verifier MUST take the receiver from that agreement's Terms in force, per that agreement's own place, qualified **per place** with that place named, or per the anchoring the reader accepts, or **as shown** (rules 3.6, 6, 13.4). Which Terms of that agreement are in force is answered by rules 3.5 to 3.8: while a fork of its Terms stands, the receiver now is the one named by the Terms both branches replace, and the verifier MUST show the fork with that answer and MUST NOT choose a branch (rule 3.7). If the version read names no receiver and no service agreement, or the spot named is not filled, the answer is **not shown**. If the verifier lacks that agreement's acts, it is **unchecked** (rule 8).
- 10.5 A rule (task K4) MAY take as an input a receiver now, so answered, or what 10.6 to 10.9 give.
- 10.6 *From S23, in draft 7.* **Service agreement.** Terms MAY name a `service`: the root of another agreement. That agreement is the first one's **service agreement** when its own Terms in force name the first agreement as a party (10.1), together with a signer, the **service**. A verifier MUST read both pointers; where either is missing, a service agreement is **not shown**. No one signs the service agreement for the work (10.2): the work's part in the deal is its own Terms naming it, signed as those Terms require (rules 3.1, 3.8).
- 10.7 **What a verifier reads from a service agreement.** For each rail, the address at which the work is paid; and the service's fee, a flat amount or a part of what is paid. Both are read from the service agreement's Terms, in the form their cMIP gives (rule 1, task K1), and are reported as stated by those who signed them (rule 7). How the service holds, divides and pays is its cMIP's (task K8); these grammar rules do not read it.
- 10.8 **Paying a work.** Where the version read names a `service`, whatever a payer, an act or a rule of another agreement gives or owes to a party named as an agreement, it gives or owes to that agreement itself, paid at the address its service agreement gives for the rail used. A payment names the version of the work agreement it read (rule 13.2). A verifier asked whether a work was paid MUST read: the settlement, on the word of whoever gave it (rule 7.3); the version of the work agreement it names; the service agreement that version names (10.6); and the address that service agreement gives for the rail (10.7). Where these agree, the answer is **shown** for that version, whatever version came later (rule 13.1). Whether that version was the one in force is a separate answer (rule 3.6).
- 10.9 **Discharge.** A discharge is the service's signed word that it paid on what its work was paid (rule 7.3, task K8). It names the version of the work agreement it divided by, and the version of the service agreement it took its fee by (rule 13.2). A verifier MUST read the division against the shares and identities of the work version named, and the fee against the service agreement version named, and MUST report whether the work version named was the one in force, per the anchoring named or as shown (rules 3.6, 13.4); while a fork of the work's Terms stands, the one in force is the Terms both branches replace (rule 3.7). Each owner's address on each rail is read from that owner's home, on that owner's word (rule 7). A share owed to an owner named as an agreement is paid as 10.8, or 10.4, says for that agreement.

**Lie exposed.** "I receive for that work", said by someone who once did and no longer does, or never did; and "that work was paid", when it was paid to a past receiver. And (10.6 to 10.9) "pay it here", said of an address the work's own agreement does not lead to; "we divide for that work", said by a service the work never pointed to; "the split was as agreed", when it followed other shares, or a fee the deal does not give. Signed text in the paying agreement cannot say where another agreement is paid, or by whom its money is divided; only that agreement's own Terms can, and rule 10 sends the verifier there and labels each answer with where it came from.

**Protects.** The people of the work pointed at, who did not sign the pointing agreement and need not: they are not bound by it (10.2), and what it owes their work reaches them by their own agreement's shares, which no service and no payer can change. Also the one who pays, who needs one address and no knowledge of who the owners are, and who can show they paid as the version they read says (10.8). And the owners among themselves: the shares a service must divide by are the ones they signed, not a copy the service keeps.

**Reasoning.** Nobody, allegedly: "The new agreement points to the other agreement as one party, no collective involved". So the agreement party is not a group that must sign, and is not bound. Its words are pinned (its root is a fingerprint), and what it says is read from its own versions, which is why rule 1, rule 6 and rule 13 together suffice. The test `shower-cmips-1.md` (item M2) found that draft 1 could only say "owed to the agreement TP1", which is not a person, and that "the step from 'owed to TP1' to 'paid to Bo' was done outside any rule"; draft 2's receiver was that step, inside the grammar. Test 2 (N1) found that "receiver now" rested on "Terms in force" while nothing ended Terms; rules 3.5 to 3.7 (S15) answered that, and draft 5 answered the fork from the Terms both branches replaced (S20).

Draft 6 left open what a payer keeps who paid the receiver now of a branch later dropped, and when "now" is read (P8b). The MOR lead walked such a fork and proposed that a payment name the version it read. Nobody, allegedly, asked instead: "Why can’t the agreement directly point to the split? Or did they chose not to?", and: "The correct chain is the agreement with the split service rather than the service." Then he set the chain out: "So, there is a work agreement with shares that dictates a split. A pointer for the payment which can be an agreement with a service. The id involved each provide the pointer to their addresses for the various rails through their home. then, the agreement with services specifies the address of the payment to the split on the various rails as well as plug the IDs and the share from the work agreement and specify the fee structure for the service, flat or %". When the lead read it back with the payer pointing at the service agreement, he corrected: "The work agreement points to the service agreement which provides the address on the rail, at discharge the split services uses the terms of the work agreement (shares and ids) as derived from the service agreement with the work. The service has a deal with a work which happened to be signed by 3 signatures of various weights." Rules 10.6 to 10.9 are that chain and nothing more: the pointer is in the work agreement (10.6), not in the payer's hands; the address and the fee are the service agreement's (10.7); the payer pays the work (10.8); the shares and identities stay in the work agreement, where the service reads them at discharge, and each identity's addresses come from its own home (10.9). The service is a hand (task K8), so no task is added, and how it works is left to its cMIP (S4).

On collectives: "A collective is an agreement, more than an ID, we gave it ID capabilities (keys) and then removed them. So, agreements as parties is nothing new"; "A collective is simply an agreement that decided to define many aspects of how it can govern itself"; "So all agreements are collectives if there are shares, wether loose or hyper defined". And, of the whole round: "The key concept is « agreement as a party » then it’s what you build with it that changes". So this rule is named for the idea, not for one use of it, and the grammar defines no collective: governance is whatever powers an agreement's own Terms give (rule 4).

"A pointer for the payment which can be an agreement with a service", and "Or did they chose not to?", are read as leaving the choice with those who agree: so the receiver of draft 6 stays (10.3, 10.4) for an agreement that points to no split. What is read where a version names both, and whether a receiver may itself be an agreement, are open (P8). When "now" is read is answered by rule 13 (S27): it is the version the paying or owing act names.

### Rule 11. Seen, by the agreed witness

*From S9; rewritten in draft 3 from S12, S13 and S14.*

**Plain words.** Where someone is rewarded for being seen (each play, each view, each repost, each delivery to an audience), only the witness the agreement names can count it. A count by anyone else does not count. And of what the witness counts, only what the agreement's own rule sets up to be counted is counted. A rule can count only the views that led to a settled payment, or it can reward pure attention, every view the witness states. (His example is about referrers: "Show me referees that lead to settlement only", where he meant "referrers". The same holds for them; see section 9.4, item 2.) That choice belongs to those who agree, in the cMIP they pick. Nothing is ever rejected: what the rule does not set up to be counted was simply never counted.

**Precisely.**
- 11.1 A role rewarded for being seen MUST name its `witness`, a signer or a role, in `parties`.
- 11.2 A count of being seen is counted, by any rule, only if it is stated in a Statement signed by that witness.
- 11.3 Which of the witness's counts are counted, and how, is said only by the rule (task K4) the Terms name for it. A count that rule does not set up to be counted is not counted. No act is needed to keep a count out, and the grammar defines none.
- 11.4 A Statement MAY name a `settlement` it is tied to. A rule MAY count only counts tied to a settlement, or every count the witness states; the grammar requires neither. Which counts are tied to which settlement, and how, is a cMIP's. A settlement named is reported on the word of whoever gave it (rule 7.3).
- 11.5 A verifier MUST report counts as stated by the witness, and which of them the rule counted.

**Lie exposed.** Inflated attention: plays by machines, views loaded by machines, payments in the wrong direction disguised as audience. `late-roles.md`: "Roles counted by attention suffered fabrication of the event itself", and the streaming case, where fake plays drew "about $8.09 million" from a shared pool, "so the money came from real rights holders". Signed counts prove only that the counter said them; without an agreed witness, whoever counts most is paid most.

**Protects.** Everyone paid from the same source as the counts: the real performers and rights holders whose share a fake count takes. Also the payer, who agreed in advance whose count binds them and which counts are counted.

**Reasoning.** Being seen is a deed that only an observer can report, so it belongs to the residue the research calls truth. The grammar cannot make a count true; it can only fix in advance who may count (11.1, 11.2). Draft 2 then gave a way to reject counts no settlement stood behind. Nobody, allegedly: "Well, more than rejected is never setup to be counted", and "I never meant for a rejection to be something. It was in a cMIP driven scenario." So draft 3 has no reject: what counts is what the rule in the Terms sets up to count (11.3), and nothing outside it needs refusing. The field `settlement` stays so that a rule can be asked the question in his words, "Show me referees that lead to settlement only" (11.4). It is not required, because "Advertisement industry can still build a model that still rewards pure attention, if they wish." Where payments must gather in a pool before they are divided, a single count may not be tied to its own settlement; that is part of P6.

### Rule 12. Forks

*New in draft 5, from S18, S19 and S20. In draft 7 the resolver is named **arbiter** (W); nothing else in the rule changes, except that 12.5 also reads `relies_on` (S27).*

**Plain words.** Some acts come in a line, each pointing at the one before: a person's own acts in an agreement (rule 5), an agreement's Terms, each replacing the last (rule 3). Where two acts both claim to be the next step after the same one, and only one should be, the line has split: that is a fork. A fork is never chosen by anyone's software: a verifier shows both branches. A fork ends in one of two ways. Whoever could sign the step where the branches part can sign a **join**: one act that names every branch and says which one continues, or merges them into one. Or the agreement named an **arbiter** in advance, a cMIP that says how its forks are settled, and the arbiter settles it; how it does so is the cMIP's, so no single way is forced. Either way, what other people did relying on a branch that is dropped still stands, and is shown as disputed. Until a fork ends, it stays shown; for Terms, the Terms before the fork stay in force (rule 3.7). This is one rule for every act that can fork; there is no list of cases.

**Precisely.**
- 12.1 A **line** is a set of acts of which each names the one before it, so that only one act may follow each: one signer's acts within an agreement, by `previous` (rule 5.1); an agreement's Terms, by `replaces` (rule 3.8); and any other acts these grammar rules read as following one another. A **fork** is two acts that each count, under these grammar rules, as the next act after the same act of one line (rules 3.7, 5.2). That act is the **shared act**; each of the two, with every act that follows it in the line, is a **branch**.
- 12.2 While a fork stands, a verifier MUST show every branch it holds and answer **fork** where rules 3.7 and 5.2 say, and MUST NOT choose between the branches, by position or in any other way. A fork stands until 12.3 or 12.4 ends it.
- 12.3 **Join.** A join is an act of the same line that follows the shared act (its `previous`, or for Terms its `replaces`, names the shared act), and names in `joins` the first act of every branch. If it names one of them in `continues`, that branch continues and the line goes on from that branch. If it names none, the join merges the branches and the line goes on from the join. A join ends the fork only when it carries the signatures an act following the shared act in that line requires: for one signer's line, that signer's (rule 5); for Terms, those the shared Terms require for their replacement (rule 3.8). A join is not a branch. Every branch the join does not continue is **dropped**.
- 12.4 **Arbiter.** Terms MAY name an `arbiter`: a cMIP answering task K14, by fingerprint (rule 1). Where a fork stands within an agreement whose Terms in force (for a fork of Terms, the Terms both branches replace, rule 3.7) name an arbiter, the fork also ends when the arbiter, applied to the fork, names the branch that continues or the act that merges the branches. Every other branch is dropped. A verifier MUST report the arbiter's answer with whoever it rests on (rule 7).
- 12.5 **What others relied on stands.** An act signed by someone who signed none of a dropped branch's acts, and that rests on an act of that branch (it names it, in `terms`, `under`, `answers` or `relies_on`, or was made within or under Terms of that branch), keeps the effect these grammar rules gave it. A payment that names a version of that branch as the one it read (rule 13.2) is such an act. A verifier MUST show that act, and every answer resting on it, as **disputed**.
- 12.6 A fork that no join and no arbiter has ended stands: of Terms, as rule 3.7 says; in one signer's line, as rule 5.2 says.

**Lie exposed.** "This branch is the real one", said by whoever a branch suits, to whoever is shown only that branch; and "we settled it", said of a fork by people who could not have signed the step where it parted. Each branch is signed and looks valid alone; only the two together show the fork, and only the shared act shows who could end it.

**Protects.** Whoever relied on a branch without causing the fork: the payer, the newcomer, the other party who answered an act (12.5). Every party, who is not left stuck in a fork with no way out, because the way out (a join, or an arbiter named in advance) is fixed before anyone needs it. And the reader, who is shown the fork and every branch instead of one picked for them.

**Reasoning.** Draft 4 had two forks, a signer's two stories (rule 5) and two replacements of the same Terms (rule 3.7), each shown, never chosen, with nothing to end either (draft 4, section 9.6, item 3). Nobody, allegedly, asked: "Can we attempt  to solve incompatible forks (a fork that should not exist) for all sorts of acts that need it?" Asked by the MOR lead whether one shape fits all acts (shown, never chosen; ended only by a join that names every branch and says which continues, or merges them; signed by whoever could sign the last act both branches share; what others relied on in a dropped branch standing, shown as disputed): "I think it does. Let’s focus on unresolved forks…" So rule 12 is one rule, written for a line of any acts, with no list of cases; rules 3.7 and 5.2 say only where the grammar meets a fork, and how a fork of Terms is read while it stands. On unresolved forks, to the MOR lead's three ways out: "Resolver makes sense, the problem potentially exists, so deciding in advance how to solve it makes sense, and this way does not force a single way to do it." So the arbiter (his word of the moment was "Resolver"; renamed in draft 7, W) is named in advance, by fingerprint, as a task's cMIP is (K14), and the grammar says only that its answer ends the fork; whether it computes from the place, names a judge or does anything else is its cMIP's (S4). The MOR lead's other two ways out are not added as rules: "each party carries on from the last Terms all shared" is, for Terms, what rule 3.7 now says from S20; "what happened stays visible" is 12.2 and 12.5. 12.3 reads "whoever could sign the last act both branches share" as whoever could sign the next act after it in that line, which is the same signer for one signer's line, and for Terms those whose signatures rule 3.8 requires (section 9.4, item 8).

### Rule 13. Now is what an act cites

*New in draft 7, from S27; the payment from S23.*

**Plain words.** After any act, other acts pile up: payments, thanks, fillings, new versions, each made for its own reason and tied to its own people. No clock runs over them. But every act names other acts, and each naming is a knot: an act that names another certainly came after it. Where chains name each other, order emerges by itself. So an act's "now" is simply what it names. It is read against the versions and acts it cites, and a later version does not reach back to change what it did. That is why every act names what it relied on: a payment names the version of the work it read. An act is never read as having relied on something it did not name. Citing can show "after". It can never show "nothing later", or what time it was. For those two, each reader accepts the anchoring it is willing to accept (the place an agreement names, or another), and every such answer says which anchoring it stands on, or is given as shown.

**Precisely.**
- 13.1 An act is read against the versions and acts it names, in any field that holds a fingerprint (`terms`, `under`, `replaces`, `previous`, `answers`, `joins`, `relies_on`, `settlement`). A version that replaces one an act names does not change what these grammar rules read that act as having done. Whether the act's signer is bound from then on is rules 3.3 and 4, unchanged.
- 13.2 An act that relied on a version or an act, of its own agreement or of another, names it: a Statement in `relies_on` where no other field names it, a Terms act in `relies_on` (rule 1). A verifier MUST NOT read an act as having relied on a version or act it does not name. A payment to a work names the version of the work agreement it read (rule 10.8); how a settlement carries that name is for the layer that gives the settlement (section 8).
- 13.3 **Order from citing.** An act that names another, directly or through acts that name it, came after it. A verifier MAY give that order as **shown**, from the acts alone, with no place and no qualifier. Between two acts of which neither so names the other, no order is shown from the acts alone.
- 13.4 **Anchoring.** Whether anything is later than an act, and what time it was, are never shown by citing. For those answers each reader accepts the anchoring it chooses: the place the Terms name (rule 6), or another. A verifier MUST give each such answer **per anchoring**, with the anchoring named (**per place** where it is the place the Terms name), or **as shown**.

**Lie exposed.** "You paid the wrong one", said to a payer after the agreement changed behind them; "I was relying on the earlier version", said afterwards by someone whose act named nothing; "mine came first", said of two acts that nothing ties together; and "nothing came after", said on the strength of an anchoring the reader never accepted. Signed text shows what an act names; without this rule a verifier could read the act against whatever version suited the one asking.

**Protects.** Whoever acts on a version they read and named: the payer above all, who did not choose when others would change the agreement and cannot know of a change not yet shown to them. And the reader, who is told what each answer about "latest" or about time stands on, instead of being handed someone else's clock.

**Reasoning.** Draft 6 left open when "now" is read (P8b; test 2, N1: "whether Ines paid the right person can change answer after she paid"). The MOR lead proposed that every act is read against the versions in force at its own position. Nobody, allegedly, answered with what really happens: "What happens after it got paid? A series of acts have been created for various reasons, tying to various id… chains crossing, knots forming, order emerging". The lead read that as free order (each citation a knot that says this came after that; an act's now is what it cites; a place needed only for nothing later), and he answered: "Yes, I think so. Then, anyone accept the anchoring they are willing to accept." So 13.1 and 13.3 take order from what the acts themselves carry, which any holder of copies can check (section 1), and 13.4 leaves the two things citing cannot give to an anchoring each reader chooses. That every act names what it relied on is the lead's consequence, in the arrived-at line; it is written here as a rule of reading (13.2: what is not named was not relied on), because no verifier could check a duty to name. Time is in the lead's line and not in his sentence; it is written into 13.4 because rule 7.2 already says a time inside an act is only its signer's claim, so that an anchoring is the only thing left to answer it (section 9.10). The place of rule 6 is unchanged: it is the anchoring the parties of one agreement agreed among themselves.

## 5. Tasks handed to cMIPs

A task is one question the grammar asks and does not answer (section 2). A cMIP answering a task accepts what the task says and produces what the task says; anyone may write one, and a cMIP may answer a task for one kind of thing only (one cMIP for land, another for shares). Where a task belongs to another layer's operation, it is named and not defined.

| # | Task | Question | Accepts | Produces | Trust |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | Body | What do these words say? | the `body` bytes | a reading for people, and any parts for machines that rules read | none: format only |
| K2 | Identity | Who is behind this signer? | a signer, an agreement | signed statements by those who vouch for the signer | witness (Sign layer) |
| K3 | Assurance | What was done to make it likely that a party was able, free and understood? | Terms, a party | signed statements of what was done (cooling-off, separate advice, witnessing) | witness; never a pass |
| K4 | Rule | What does this rule produce from these acts? | a rule's parameters, the acts held, the answers of the place or of the anchoring named, and receivers now and what a service agreement gives (rule 10.5) | yes, no, a list of who, or amounts; the same output for the same inputs, every time, for anyone | none: anyone can recompute |
| K5 | Witness | What happened in the world? | the question as the Terms state it, the named witnesses | each witness's signed statement | witness |
| K6 | Place | Is this act in the place, at what position, and is anything later? | the place's identifier, an act or an agreement | a signed answer by the place | place (Order layer) |
| K7 | Tally | What did a vote decide, without showing who voted how? | the ballots, the list of who may vote | the result, with whatever proof the method allows | teller |
| K8 | Control | Has the controller carried out this instruction? | an instruction, with the acts and rule result behind it | the controller's signed evidence | hand (Control layer) |
| K9 | Delivery | Did this act reach this person? | an act, a recipient | the recipient's signed acknowledgment, or the Deliver layer's evidence | recipient or Deliver layer |
| K10 | Judge | What did the named judge decide? | the Terms (including `authors`), the acts of the dispute | the judge's signed decision, which binds only through a power under rule 4 | judge |
| K11 | Kind | What does a Statement of this kind state, and which rules read it? | a `kind` fingerprint | the meaning of `content`, and how K4 rules may read it | none: definition only |
| K12 | Spot | Who fills this spot, by which proof, and on whose word? | the role (its kind and `what`), the acts held, the place's answers | each filling: the signer who fills it and the proof that earned it (the acts it rests on); or none; whether the spot holds one filling or many (rule 9.2); for kind future, also what defines the participant | whoever the cMIP names as confirming: for done, a witness or the counterparty; for received, the previous holder; for holding, as its cMIP says; for fits, those who vouch (K2); for future, as its cMIP says |
| K13 | Standing | What do one signer's acts show about them, to anyone who looks? | a signer, any acts and place answers the one asking holds | whatever measures the cMIP defines (proven forks, acknowledgments given and received, spots filled), computed from signed acts only, qualified per place or as shown | none: anyone can recompute; nobody keeps it |
| K14 | Arbiter | Which branch of this fork continues, or what merges the branches? | the fork (its shared act and every branch held), the acts held, the place's answers | the branch that continues, or the act that merges the branches; with the acts and statements it rests on (rule 12.4) | as its cMIP says: nobody, if it computes from the acts and the place; whoever it names, if it names someone to decide |

**Why these, and why so few.** The research found that the same few operations answer the 49 questions, and that a "silent sixth operation", applying a stated rule to known inputs, needs no trust because anyone can redo it. So the tasks sort by whom they make you trust: nobody (K1, K4, K11, K13), a witness of the world (K2, K3, K5, and K12 for most kinds), a place (K6), a teller (K7), a hand (K8), a recipient (K9), a judge (K10); and, for K14, whoever its cMIP says, since how an arbiter settles a fork is not forced (S19). Each task is one question; many cMIPs may answer each one, pointed at different objects, and "more tasks means less defining for each one" holds inside each row: a cMIP for share registers and a cMIP for votes both answer K4 without either defining the other.

**Why K13 is a task of its own.** Nobody, allegedly: "reputation tracking no platform owns or computes is important." K4 is one agreement's rule; reputation reads a signer's acts across many agreements. K13 says what such a reading may rest on (signed acts and place answers, nothing a platform alone holds), and that its answer is the same for anyone who holds the same acts. Which measures matter, and how they are weighed, is the cMIP's.

**Why a service needs no task of its own** (draft 7, S23). A service that divides a work's money, or holds a payment in escrow, is a hand: it answers K8, and its cMIP says how it works. What the grammar adds is only where a verifier finds the service (the work's `service` pointer, rule 10.6) and what it reads there (rule 10.7 to 10.9). The rule a version names in `void` (rule 9.8) is a K4 rule like any other: it computes from the acts held and the answers of an anchoring, and trusts nobody beyond them.

## 6. Open parameters

These are values the grammar rules depend on and this draft does not fix. P1 to P5 are draft 1's; P6 is from S10; P7 and P8 come from the new rules (P7 narrowed in draft 4 by S16); P9 to P12 are gaps the tests found (P10 narrowed in draft 4 by S15; P9 and P10 narrowed in draft 5 by S20; P10 narrowed again in draft 6 by S21 and S22), written here as open instead of closed (section 9). In draft 7, P8(b) is answered by S27, P8 gains (c) from S23, P11 is narrowed by S27, and P13 to P15 are new: each is where an exchange stops short of something a change touches.

- **P1. Default for `in_force`.** Rule 3.1 sets it to "every party named as a signer". Whether a different default is wanted is open.
- **P2. Depth of powers through powers.** Rule 4 lets Terms named in `under` themselves be bound through other Terms (an agent of an agent; a group within a group). Whether a verifier must follow any depth, or a limit applies, is open.
- **P3. Replacing a grant.** Under rule 4, a grant of authority gets a new version (rule 3.9) as its own powers say; with no power named, only by every party's assent, which lets an agent block their own dismissal. Whether a principal must always be able to replace a grant alone is a decision for Nobody, allegedly; it is not taken here (section 9.8, item 3).
- **P4. One place or several.** Rule 6 names one place. Whether Terms may name several places with a K4 rule over their answers (plural witnesses of order) is open.
- **P5. What a verifier holds.** Rules 5 and 8 depend on which acts the verifier holds. Whether a verifier answers from a set it is given, or must also ask the place for more, is open. K13's answers depend on it most.
- **P6. Pools.** In plain words: some ways of paying (rails) cannot pay each share at the moment it is earned. Money must first gather in one pot, and be divided later. Nobody, allegedly: "Sadly some rails will need pools… this headache won’t be easy." This draft does not solve it. What is open, all of it:
  - who holds a pool, and on whose word it is said to hold what it holds (rule 7.3: only the holder's or controller's word);
  - when a pool is divided, and by which rule;
  - how a claim on a pool is counted when each false claim takes from the real claimants (`late-roles.md`: "each fake claim takes from real claimants, and the payer has little reason to resist");
  - what "tied to a settlement" (rule 11.4) means for a count paid out of a pool, where one settlement covers many counts;
  - what happens to a pool's share that no one has yet claimed (the owed but unfindable).
- **P7. What happens to a filling.** Draft 3 asked whether one spot can be filled by many signers; draft 4 answers it (rule 9.2, S16): a spot may be filled many times, each filling tied to its proof, and whether it holds one or many is its K12 cMIP's. Still open, as in draft 3: whether a filled spot can pass to another, and whether a later claimant can displace an earlier one.
- **P8. Receivers.** (a) Whether a `receiver` may itself be an agreement, so that one receiver points onward, and if so to what depth, and what a loop answers. (b) When "now" in rule 10.4 is read. **Answered in draft 7** by S27: it is the version the act that owes names (rules 10.4, 13.1). (c) New in draft 7: what is read where one version names both a `service` and a `receiver`, and whether the receiver is still wanted at all once an agreement can point directly to its split. His words leave the choice open ("which can be an agreement with a service"); draft 7 keeps both and does not say which is read when both are named.
- **P9. What a fork removes.** Rule 5.2 says a verifier "MUST answer **fork** for that signer". Whether that is for the two acts only or for every later answer involving the signer, and whether a signer can make their own assent uncountable by forking on purpose, is open (`situations-1.md`, F2, F2a). Draft 5 answers it for Terms only (rule 3.7, S20): while a fork of Terms stands, the Terms both branches replace stay in force. For one signer's fork, what it removes while it stands is still open, as in draft 4; it now ends by that signer's join or by an arbiter (rule 12).
- **P10. Ending and withdrawing.** Draft 4 answers two of draft 3's questions here (rules 3.5 to 3.7, S15): replacing Terms end the ones replaced once in force, and two replacements of the same Terms both in force are a fork, never chosen. Draft 5 adds (rules 3.7, 3.8, 12, S20): a replacement replaces only with the signatures the replaced Terms require; while two replacements fork, the replaced Terms stay in force; the fork ends by a join or an arbiter. Draft 6 answers two more (rules 3.9, 9.5, S21, S22): Terms are never ended without being replaced, since an agreement only gets a new version, signed as its Terms require; and an open offer is not withdrawn but closed to new identities by a new version, every filling already earned staying a filling (`situations-1.md`, F1, F9). Still open, as in draft 3: how a withdrawal of an assent is read (`situations-1.md`, F3).
- **P11. Dates.** A place gives positions, not dates. Whether a K4 rule may read a calendar date, and from whom, is open (`situations-1.md`, F6). Narrowed in draft 7 by S27: what time it was is answered per the anchoring each reader accepts (rule 13.4). Still open: whether Terms may name the anchoring a rule must read for time, so that every reader computes the same answer (it matters most for a `void` rule that ends rights at a time, rule 9.8).

## 7. Stated costs

Cases that do not fit the grammar. None is closed by a rule.

- **Minds.** Whether a party was able (#3) or free (#5) cannot be shown by any act. K3 can make it more likely and record what was done; nothing assigns its failure. Only Align is assigned (rule 2).
- **Conceal (#39).** Terms can be kept private and their fingerprint lodged in a place; nothing can show the words were not told to someone, and telling cannot be undone.
- **Enforce beyond what was posted (#45).** Words can order a release; something must obey. Against value not under Control, force lies outside the protocol. A proven liar (rule 5) loses only what they posted and their standing with those who see the proof (K13). Against a stranger who vanishes, the proof is worth nothing.
- **Deeds lean on witnesses.** The more an agreement is about services and acts in the world instead of value under Control, the more it rests on K5, whose statements prove only that the witness said them.
- **Spots lean on their confirmers.** Each kind of spot has its own recorded fraud (`late-roles.md`): doing is faked "by simulating the act or stealing credit"; receiving "by forging the chain"; holding "by forging the object or issuing more than exists"; being "by false or multiplied identities". Rule 9 fixes the test in advance and rule 7.4 reports who confirmed; neither makes the confirmer honest.
- **Future spots are the authors' word.** Whether a future spot was used only for participants no one could anticipate cannot be checked (rule 9.4). Authors who use one for an ordinary participant, to escape the four kinds' clearer tests, are not stopped by the grammar.
- **Seen counts lean on one witness.** Rule 11 makes the agreed witness the only counter, so a witness who lies, or who is also the payer, counts as they like. Which counts are counted is the Terms' choice (rule 11.3): a rule that counts only what led to a settlement leaves out someone truly seen whom no payment yet stands behind, and a rule that rewards pure attention rests every count on the witness alone.
- **"Now" is what was read, and no more.** Under rule 13, a payer who names the version they read has paid as that version says, even if the agreement had already changed without their being able to know. That protects the payer; it also means the owners under a later version can see money go by an earlier one. Whether the version named was still the last is answered only per an anchoring, or as shown (rules 3.6, 13.4), and the climb up a series of splits gets less certain at each level that names no place.
- **A work that points nowhere cannot be paid.** Under rule 10, what is owed to an agreement whose version names neither a service agreement nor a receiver has nowhere to go, and is not shown.
- **A service is a hand.** Rule 10 says what a verifier reads: the two pointers, the address, the fee, the shares, the service's word that it paid on. Nothing in the grammar makes a service divide as the shares say, or pay at all. A discharge that does not match the work's shares is shown as not matching, and anyone may count it against the service (K13); getting the money is another matter ("Enforce beyond what was posted"). Each owner's address comes from that owner's home, on the owner's word: an owner with no home, or no address on the rail used, cannot be paid on it.
- **Paid, and the author gone.** An open offer whose only author goes absent, with nothing that delivers by itself: whoever paid and got nothing can contest, and the contest stays on the record for anyone to read (K13). The record cannot give the money back, since nobody is left to sign. A buyer who wants that pays through an escrow service: a hand, under its service agreement, for a fee. Without one, the loss is the buyer's. Nothing in the grammar prevents an author from vanishing with what was paid; that is dealt with, if at all, in how services are built. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "MOR only offers ways to ring the alarm", and "MOR just tracks receipts".
- **Order without an anchoring is partial.** Citing shows only "after" (rule 13.3). Two acts that never cite each other have no order from the acts alone, and nothing shows "nothing later". Readers who accept different anchorings can get different answers; each answer says which anchoring it stands on.
- **Void is as sure as its cause.** A rule that ends rights on someone's statement rests on whoever made the statement (rule 7); one that ends them at a time rests on the anchoring the reader accepts (rule 13.4). Two readers with different anchorings can differ on whether a filling is void yet.
- **Reputation is as wide as the acts one holds.** K13 answers only from what the one asking holds, per place or as shown. Two people holding different acts compute different standing for the same signer, and honest forks (below) count against honest people.
- **Without a place, no scarcity and no freshness.** The same thing can be given twice in two branches, the latest act can be withheld, silence cannot be counted. Rule 6.3 makes this visible; it does not cure it.
- **Ownership is as wide as its place.** Two parties who accept different places cannot settle who holds a thing between them. The same thing sold in two separate agreements is seen by neither agreement's rules (`situations-1.md`, F5).
- **Closed world hurts the one who did not lodge.** Under rule 6.2, a party's own act that never reached the place does not count, even if it is genuine.
- **Honest forks.** A signer who loses track of their last act (a lost device, a restored backup) and signs again produces a fork that looks exactly like a lie. Honest drift and lying look the same from outside. From draft 5 the signer can end it with a join (rule 12.3), if they still hold their key; what others relied on in the branch the join drops stands, shown as disputed (rule 12.5).
- **A fork of Terms holds the agreement where it was.** Under rule 3.7, while two replacements of the same Terms both stand, the Terms before them stay in force: neither branch's change takes effect, including a new receiver (rule 10.4). If the agreement named no arbiter, and those whose signatures a join needs will not sign one, it stays there; the grammar shows it and picks no branch.
- **A join does not undo.** What others relied on in a dropped branch stands, shown as disputed (rule 12.5). A join or an arbiter settles which branch goes on; it cannot take back what a stranger did relying on the other.
- **Withdrawing before in force.** Between a first assent and the last, the first signer holds an open option against themselves. Withdrawal is a race that only a place, or an `in_force` rule with a deadline read from a place, settles (and see P10).
- **Closing an open offer.** The same race, between the closing version coming into force (rule 9.5) and a stranger's filling the spot. Without a place, which came first is only as shown.
- **Secret votes need a teller.** K7 requires trusting whoever counts.
- **Identity ends in acquaintance.** Who vouches for the voucher (K2) ends in someone knowing someone; the grammar cannot express that. Spots of kind fits rest on it.
- **The record holds no value.** Every act about value is a claim or an instruction. The gap between "the record says 100" and "there are 60" must be checked continuously (K5, K8), not once.
- **Those who cannot sign.** A contributor who has no key (the long dead, the unknowing) can be named in words but is no party, and nothing can arrive for them (`shower-cmips-1.md`, 7.3).
- **No default way out.** By rule 4, an agreement that names no power to change itself changes only by everyone's assent again. And since no agreement ends (rule 3.9), none can be left by ending it: the last version keeps binding until those its Terms require sign a new one. If a party is gone and no successor was named in advance (#37, #38), it is stuck. The way out is designed in advance or not at all; this draft chooses that over any default that would let someone be bound without their signature.
- **Ordering and chaining show patterns.** Rules 5 and 6 make the shape of an agreement (who acted, how often, in what order) visible to whoever sees the acts or the place, even when the words are concealed. K13 reads exactly those patterns.

### 9.4 Readings of his words that he should confirm

These are Claude's readings, written into draft 2 (and, for item 2, rewritten in draft 3; items 5 to 7 are new in draft 4, items 8 to 13 in draft 5, items 14 to 21 in draft 6, and items 22 to 35 in draft 7) so that it can be read whole. Each could be wrong.
