## 0. In plain words

**The central idea: an agreement can be a party.** People sign an agreement. That agreement can then stand as one party in another agreement, as a person would. Being named asks nothing of it: it need not sign to be pointed at, and what reaches it is passed on by its own terms. Where it does sign, it signs as its own terms require: a small work by all its owners, a collective by a vote or a named delegate, as its rule says. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "The key concept is « agreement as a party » then it’s what you build with it that changes". Everything else in this text is there so that this can be read safely by a stranger. What people build with it is theirs:

- a **work**: an agreement among those who made something, saying who owns it and in what shares;
- a **service**: someone who does a job for a work (dividing its money, holding a payment in escrow) under a **deal**, which is an agreement between the service and the work as a party, signed by the service and by the work, as the work's own terms require; the work names that deal as its receiver;
- a **delegation**: an agent acting for an identity, or for an agreement as a party, which are the same thing in two flavours;
- a work that stands on other works names them as parties, so that one split pays into the next: a **sequence** of agreements, each one a **leg**;
- a **collective**: nothing separate. "A collective is simply an agreement that decided to define many aspects of how it can govern itself", and "all agreements are collectives if there are shares, whether loose or hyper defined". An agreement with shares defines as much or as little of its own governance as it chooses, and the grammar has no other notion of a group.

People agree in order to defend themselves against the other party's future self, and their own. Honest drift and lying look the same from outside, so every written agreement is written for a third person who may turn up later: a newcomer, an heir, a judge, a buyer. The least two people need to write down is exactly the least that third person needs to see.

**Agreements does not rule.** It is a grammar: the words people use to write down their own rules, and the few ways those words must be read so that a stranger reads them the same way. What people agree (a price, a vote, a split, a deadline) is theirs, written in cMIPs they choose. The grammar only makes sure that what they wrote can be checked by anyone, later.

Most of what an agreement needs is already provided by the layers around Agreements: words that cannot shift (State), a mark that binds someone (Sign), getting the words to someone (Deliver), placing them before or after something else (Order), and power over a thing (Control). Agreements does not redefine those. It adds only what those five cannot give on their own, and every addition here must answer two questions:

- **Which lie does it expose that signed, ordered text cannot already expose?**
- **Whom does it protect who did not choose it?**

One more test stands over the whole text, in the words of Nobody, allegedly: "Building blocks over solutions." In plain words: the grammar gives blocks (an act, a party, a spot, a condition signed in advance, a name by fingerprint), it uses one block that belongs to all of MOR (the report), and people build their solutions from them, in cMIPs. Where this text would itself solve something a cMIP could build from those blocks, it gives the block and stops.

Those tests leave fourteen grammar rules for a verifier. Everything else an agreement needs is handed to cMIPs as thirteen tasks, each asking one question. What fits neither is written down as a cost, openly, instead of being patched with another rule.

In one sentence: **Agreements fixes what was agreed and who is bound by it, and says honestly what it does not know; everything about the world, about time and about value is somebody's signed word, reported as such.**

**Two words that are kept apart.** A **chain** is the versions of one agreement, each replacing the one before: one agreement's own history. A **sequence** is several agreements linked to one another, what reaches one being passed on to the next: a payment passing on, the baker's rights passing on.

The fourteen grammar rules, in plain words (rule 1 is the central one):

1. **An agreement as a party.** An agreement may name another agreement as one of its parties. It need not sign to be named, and no group has to be formed: a collective is only an agreement with shares. Where it does sign (a work signing its deal with a service), it signs as its own terms require. An agreement names who receives for it: an identity, or another agreement. What is owed to it goes to its receiver; where that receiver is another agreement, that agreement is the next leg, and passes on what reaches it by its own terms. This is how one split pays into the next. How a service is paid, what it charges and how it divides are not the grammar's: they are the cMIP of each leg.
2. **Whole.** Whatever the agreement relies on is named by its fingerprint. Anything named otherwise is not part of it.
3. **Author.** The agreement says who wrote its words. Whoever wrote them carries their unclearness.
4. **In force.** Signing first does not bind you alone. Your signature counts once the signatures the agreement asks for are all there. Terms stop being in force when Terms replacing them are in force, and new Terms replace old ones only if they carry the signatures the old ones require. Two replacements of the same Terms, each carrying them, are a fork (rule 12): while it stands, the old Terms stay in force. An agreement never ends: it only gets a new version, signed as its Terms require; until then, the last version keeps binding. Nothing counts until every party has signed, and until then a party may cancel its own assent by a counter signature; for a party that is an agreement, "signed" is whatever its own Terms require.
5. **Own signature.** Nobody is bound by an act they did not sign, except through an agreement they did sign that gives that power, and the act must say so at the time. Whoever delegates, an identity or an agreement, can end the delegation by default; an agreement may add signed conditions to that, and the grammar puts no limit on what they say: a delegation only the agent may end is not "never" but unknown, and anyone can read it.
6. **Own chain.** Each person's acts in one agreement point at their previous one. Two acts pointing at the same previous one prove that person told two stories: a fork (rule 12).
7. **Place to look.** An agreement may name one ordered place. Then "nothing later" and "nothing else" have answers, and only what is found there counts, for every reader of that agreement. It may name a clock in the same way. Without one, every such answer is "as shown", never "latest".
8. **Say who said it.** Whatever concerns the world, time or value is reported with the name of whoever stated it. An agreement never makes it true. An act may name the settlement it is tied to: that is where the Money layer's proof of a payment plugs in.
9. **Unchecked is not passed.** What a verifier could not check, it reports as unchecked.
10. **Spots.** An agreement may keep a place for someone not yet known: whoever proves they did X, received Y, hold Z, or fit W. A fifth, open kind is for those nobody could anticipate. A spot is earned per proof: it may be filled many times, each time by the proof that earned it, and its cMIP says whether it holds one or many, and whether it still takes new people. Who fills a spot is always reported with the proof and who confirmed it. Whoever earned a filling has exactly the rights the version it was earned under offers, conditions included; no later version changes them without the holder's own signature. A version may name one condition, signed in advance, under which what applied no longer applies: the condition itself says whether it stops the rights, the offer, or both. A filling is then **in force**, or **no longer in force, by its own condition**, the condition named beside it. Going on afterwards is a new version, the offer renewed, reusing values already in the chain or not.
11. **Legs of a sequence.** Where a result depends on a sequence of agreements (one click climbing through several splits), each leg chooses its own cMIPs; nothing is chosen for the whole. So that the whole can still be read by someone who lacks one leg's cMIP, a leg may call for reports on the passing on done for it. The report is a block of MOR's shared language, which this text uses and does not define: a task may carry a call for a report, or not; the call says from whom and where. A report on passing on says what was received, what was passed on and to whom, and what was kept apart from what is still owed. Every leg is the same exchange: whoever carried out the obligation reports that they did, and whoever received confirms by a report of their own. A receiver who holds money for others then has the next obligation, within their own agreement, and the same exchange happens there. No leg is made to call for reports, and nobody is made to answer; a reader sees, before paying, which legs called. A report travels one leg, and no further. Seeing beyond one leg is for data cMIPs focused on agreements, and only where the people seen agreed to it in an agreement they signed. That is in Agreements only, never in the other layers.
12. **Forks.** Where a line of acts splits in two and only one act may follow (a person's own acts, an agreement's Terms, any act that names the one before it), that is a fork. It is shown, never chosen. It ends only by a join, signed by whoever could sign the step where the branches part, that names every branch and says which continues or merges them. An agreement may give, in advance, a power to sign that join. What others relied on in a branch that is dropped still stands, shown as disputed.
13. **Now is what an act cites.** An act is read against the versions and acts it names, and a later version does not reach back to change what it did. Every act names what it relied on: a payment names the version of the work it read. An act that names another came after it, so order emerges wherever chains cite each other, with no clock. Citing never shows "nothing later", or what time it was: for those, every reader uses the place and the clock the agreement names; only where it names none does each reader accept the anchoring it is willing to accept, and the answer says which.
14. **The baker.** A finished work is baked: sealed in an envelope that says who baked it. An agreement for a baked work counts only if the baker is one of its parties, or one of its parties holds the baker's rights through the work's own sequence of agreements, which starts with the baker. A stranger who copies the bytes has no such sequence; to sell the work as their own they must edit it and bake it again, and that is another envelope.

The way out is the reason for rule 5: nothing ties a person except their own signature, and nothing changes an agreement except what its own signers agreed in advance. An agreement never ends; it only gets a new version, signed as its own Terms require. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "Terms rule, because they were agreed by all signers". The cost is equally plain, and is written in section 7: an agreement that names no way to change it can change only when everyone signs again.

And the same reason keeps the shower free. ("The shower" is the model in which each work pays, by choice, the works it stands on.) In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "Mor won’t force the shower, it will sustain it". No grammar rule makes a work pay the works it stands on. Rule 1 makes it possible (a work may name the works it stands on as parties), rule 11 lets the whole climb be read wherever its legs call for reports and are answered, by whoever those legs agreed to be read by, rule 8 makes it visible, and the choice stays with those who sign.

## 1. Purpose

The central idea of the grammar is that an agreement can be a party to another agreement (rule 1). Works, services, collectives and their deals are built with it; none of them is a separate thing in the grammar.

Agreements defines a grammar:

- the acts in which agreeing is written (Terms and Statement);
- the grammar rules by which a verifier decides, from acts alone, **who is bound by which Terms, and what is shown, unknown or contradicted**;
- the tasks that cMIPs fill so that people can agree on anything, by rules they choose: votes, sales, shares, splits, agents, deadlines, witnesses, judges, payments.

Agreements does not decide what anyone must agree. It gives the words and the reading; the rules people agree on are theirs, written as cMIPs filling its tasks.

Agreements does not define how words are fixed, how signatures work, how acts travel, how a shared order is kept, or how value is held. It names those operations (State, Sign, Deliver, Order, Control) where it needs them.

Agreements does not decide whether anything happened in the world. It records who said so.

### Two properties, not rules

Two of the research's 49 tasks are not tasks but qualities every operation must have (`agreement-grammar.md`, finding 1):

- **Durable** (#9 Keep): what an act fixes is still there and unaltered when needed.
- **Verifiable** (#10 Check): an outsider holding copies can test it.

Agreements relies on both and adds no rule for them. One consequence is a design constraint on everything below: **every grammar rule here is checked from copies of acts alone**, plus the answers of a named place where rule 7 applies. No rule needs a particular server, keeper, platform or software author to remain. Reputation follows the same constraint (task K13).

## 2. Definitions

Each word here means one thing throughout this text.

- **Act**: a signed statement in the core's sense. Published or signed again, never updated. The fields the core gives every act (who signed, the signature, the act's own fingerprint) are not repeated here.
- **Fingerprint**: the identifier the core gives to fixed bytes. "Named by fingerprint" means referred to by that identifier and nothing else.
- **Grammar rule**: one of the fourteen rules of section 4. It says how acts are read. It never says what people must agree. Written "rule 1" to "rule 14".
- **Task**: one question the grammar asks and does not answer itself. A task says what an answer to it accepts, what it produces, and whom it makes you trust. The thirteen tasks are K1 to K13 (section 5). The grammar rules may require that some task be answered; they never answer it.
- **cMIP**: a document, named by fingerprint, that answers one task, for some kind of thing or situation. Anyone may write one. Many cMIPs may answer the same task in different ways (one for land, another for shares). Choosing a cMIP is how people choose their own rules.
- **The research's 49 tasks** (#1 to #49): the questions an agreement may have to answer, from `agreement-tasks.md`. They are a checklist of what can go wrong, not this text's tasks. Where each one lands in this text is recorded in `companion-3.md`.
- **Terms**: an act that states what is agreed. Always capitalised and singular ("a Terms act", "these Terms"). Stating Terms is not agreeing to them.
- **Statement**: any other act made within an agreement. Its **kind** says what it is (see task K11). Two kinds are defined here: **assent** and **counter signature**.
- **Assent**: a Statement by which its signer agrees to one Terms act.
- **Counter signature**: a Statement by which its signer cancels its own earlier assent to one Terms act. It is available only until every party has signed (rule 4.10).
- **Agreement**: the first Terms act together with every Terms act that replaces it, directly or through others, and every Statement made within them. Its identifier is the fingerprint of the first Terms act (the **root**).
- **Version**: one Terms act of an agreement. The first is the root; each later one replaces the one before (rule 4.8). The **last version** is the one in force (rule 4.6; while a fork of Terms stands, the one both branches replace, rule 4.7).
- **Chain**: the versions of one agreement, each replacing the one before: one agreement's own history. The word is used here for nothing that links one agreement to another (but see section 8.3 for three places where it is still used of other things).
- **Sequence**: agreements linked to one another, so that what reaches one is passed on to the next: a payment passing from a work to the works it stands on, the baker's rights passing from one agreement for a work to the next. Never called a chain.
- **Leg**: one agreement of a sequence, with the cMIPs its own Terms name. The choice of cMIP is made per leg (rule 11.1).
- **Report**, **call**: words of MOR's shared language, used here and not defined (section 8.1). In short: a task may carry a **call** for a report, or not; the call says from whom a report is expected, and where; only the signers of the act that holds the task can make it. A **report** is a signed act that answers a call: it carries the call it answers, the acts it relied on, and its signer. Anything more is the task's to say. In this text a report travels one leg, and no further (rule 11.6).
- **Leg report**: a report that answers a call carried by the Control task (K8) of a leg (rule 11.3). What it carries beyond what every report carries is in K8's row (section 5).
- **Never ended**: an agreement never ends. It only gets a new version, under the Terms' own rule (rules 4.8, 4.9), and goes on as its last version. This text has no defined term for ending.
- **Party**: one named in Terms. Named in one of three ways:
  - as a **signer**: a person, by the key the Sign layer gives them;
  - as a **role**: a spot for someone not yet known, linked to a signer later (rule 10);
  - as an **agreement**: another agreement, by its root (rule 1).

  Signers, and signers who fill a role, are the parties who may be bound. An agreement named as a party need not sign to be named, and where it has not signed it is not bound. Where it signs, it signs as its own Terms require (rule 1.2). What its signing binds its own people to is not said (P17).
- **Work**: an agreement whose Terms say who owns something and in what shares. A plain word for a use of the grammar, not a separate thing in it: every rule reads a work as it reads any agreement.
- **Shares**: what an agreement's Terms say each party's part is. The grammar gives them no form: they are read by the rule (task K4) the Terms name for dividing. An agreement with shares defines as much or as little of how it governs itself as it chooses (powers, rule 5); the grammar has no separate notion of a collective.
- **Service**, **deal**: a service is a signer who does a job for an agreement (dividing what it is paid, holding a payment); a hand, in the research's sense (task K8). Its deal is an agreement between the service and the agreement it serves, named as a party. A deal is signed on both sides: by the service, and by the agreement it serves, as that agreement's own Terms require (rule 1.2). Plain words for a use of the grammar, as "work" is: what makes an agreement a deal, and what is read from it, are the service's cMIP's (section 5).
- **Spot**: a role's place among the parties, before and after it is filled. It has a **kind**: **done**, **received**, **holding**, **fits** or **future** (rule 10). Whether it still takes new identities is said by its cMIP, from the version in force (rule 10.5).
- **Fill**: a spot is filled when the cMIP its role names (task K12) says that a signer is linked to it by a proof: the acts that earn it. Each filling is tied to the proof that earned it. A spot may be filled many times; whether it holds one filling or many is its cMIP's (rule 10.2).
- **Rights**: what a version offers whoever earns a filling under it, with every condition that version puts on it. The grammar does not say what they are, or read them (rule 10.6).
- **Receiver**: whoever Terms name, in the field `receiver`, as receiving on the agreement's behalf: an identity (one of the agreement's own parties) or another agreement, by its root (rule 1.3).
- **Author**: a party whose words the Terms are.
- **Power**: an entry in Terms saying that a Statement of a given kind (or a replacing Terms act, or a join) binds the parties when a given rule says yes.
- **Delegation**: Terms in which one party, the **delegator**, gives another, the **agent**, a power to make acts for it. The delegator may be an identity or an agreement (rule 5.5).
- **Rule**: a cMIP answering task K4, named by fingerprint, with its parameters. It is the people's own rule, not a grammar rule. It computes; it trusts nobody.
- **Settlement**: the signed evidence, from the layer that holds value (Control; the Money layer), that a payment was made. Agreements names it and does not define it. The Statement field `settlement` is the socket where it plugs in (rule 8.5).
- **Line**: acts of which each names the one before it: one signer's acts within an agreement, by `previous` (rule 6); an agreement's Terms, by `replaces` (rule 4), which is its chain; and any other acts the grammar reads that way (rule 12.1).
- **Fork**: two acts that each count as the next act after the same act of one line (rule 12.1). The act both follow is the **shared act**; each of the two, with what follows it in the line, is a **branch**.
- **Join**: an act that ends a fork by naming every branch and saying which continues, or by merging them (rule 12.3).
- **Dropped**: a branch is dropped when a join ends its fork and it is not the branch that continues (rule 12.3).
- **Bound**: a person is bound by an act when rule 5 says so.
- **In force**: Terms are in force when rule 4 says so. A filling, and the rights it holds, are in force from when the filling is earned, and **no longer in force** when the condition its own version names says so (rule 10.7).
- **Condition**: what a version states, in signed words and in advance, as changing what applies under it. These grammar rules read one directly: the rule (task K4) a version names, by fingerprint, in `condition`. That rule itself says what it stops: the rights the version offers, the offer to new identities, or both (rule 10.7). Every other condition is read where its version's cMIP puts it (rules 5.5, 10.6).
- **Place**: an ordered place to look, provided by the Order layer, which can say of an act whether it is there and at what position, and whether anything is later.
- **Name** (or **cite**): an act names another act, or a version, when it carries its fingerprint in one of its fields. What an act names is what it is read against (rule 13).
- **Anchoring**: whatever a reader accepts as saying that nothing is later than an act, or what time it was. A place is an anchoring, and so is a clock. The place and the clock an agreement's Terms name are the ones every reader uses for that agreement (rule 7.4); a reader accepts an anchoring of its own choosing only where the agreement names none (rule 13.4).
- **Clock**: what an agreement's Terms name, in the field `clock`, as saying what time it was for that agreement. Named here, not defined (section 8.1).
- **Bake**, **envelope**, **baker**: a finished work is baked when its bytes are sealed in an envelope, which says who baked it: the baker. The core's words, named here and not defined (section 8.1). An envelope is named by its fingerprint (rule 14).
- **Verifier**: whoever applies these grammar rules to a set of acts. A verifier answers questions; it does not decide disputes.
- **Answers**: a verifier's answer is one of:
  - **shown**: the acts held establish it under these grammar rules;
  - **not shown**: the acts held do not establish it;
  - **fork**: two acts are a fork (rule 12): two acts by one signer name the same previous act (rule 6.2), or two Terms acts each replace the same Terms (rules 4.7, 4.8);
  - **no longer in force**: a filling counted, and still is one, but the rights it holds are no longer in force, by its own condition: the one its own version names (rule 10.7). Always given with that condition named beside it. It is not the answer for an act that never counted: that is **invalid**.
  - **invalid**: an act never counted as what it claims to be: the acts held show that these grammar rules do not let it count (a replacement without the signatures required, rule 4.8; Terms for a baked work with neither the baker nor a sequence from the baker, rule 14.3). The word is meant to mean here what it means in the other layers (P19). It is not **not shown**, which says only that the acts held do not establish something.
  - **unchecked**: the verifier could not apply a rule it needed (rule 9).

  Answers about what is latest, what else exists, or what time it was, carry a qualifier: **per anchoring**, with the anchoring named (rule 13.4), written **per place** where the anchoring is the place the Terms name and **per clock** where it is the clock they name; or **as shown** (meaning: among the acts this verifier happens to hold). An answer that rests on what someone relied on in a dropped branch also carries the qualifier **disputed** (rule 12.5).

  Of reports, a verifier also shows what the shared language's block asks it to show, in that block's words, which are not answers of this text: for an act that two reports name, **matched**, **mismatched** or **one side only**; for a call with no report, **unanswered**; for a task with no call, **no report called for** (rule 11.4).

| `continues` | no; only in a join | one of the fingerprints in `joins`: the branch that continues; absent, the join itself merges the branches | rule 12 |

An assent's `content` is empty: its `terms` field is what it agrees to. A counter signature's `content` is empty too: its `terms` field names the Terms whose assent it cancels (rule 4.10).

A join (rule 12.3) of a fork of Terms is a Terms act; a join of a fork in one signer's line is an act of that signer, Terms or Statement. `joins` is what makes an act a join.

A report (rule 11) is an act of the shared language, and this text gives it no fields of its own. What a leg report carries is in K8's row (section 5). How a report is written as an act, and whether it is a Statement of this text, is the block's to say (section 8.1).

## 4. Grammar rules

Each grammar rule is given in plain words, then precisely, then with its two answers and the reasoning. MUST, SHOULD and MAY are used as in RFC 2119. No SHOULD appears: every rule that survived the two questions turned out to be either required or a free choice.

These rules say how acts are read. None of them says what people must agree.

### Rule 1. An agreement as a party

**Plain words.** An agreement can name another agreement (a song, a library, a drawing, anything with its own agreement) as one of its parties. It need not sign to be named, and no group has to be formed. The grammar has no notion of a collective apart from this: an agreement with shares is all a collective is, and it defines as much or as little of how it governs itself as it chooses. Works, services and their deals are built in the same way.

An agreement named as a party can also sign, as a person would. Then it signs as its own Terms require: a small work by all its owners, a collective by a vote or a named delegate, as its rule says. Nobody outside it signs in its place.

An agreement names who receives for it: its **receiver**. That may be an identity (one of its own parties) or another agreement, named by its fingerprint. There is one mechanism, not two. Whatever is owed to an agreement goes to its receiver. Where the receiver is itself an agreement, that agreement is the next leg: what reaches it is passed on by its own terms.

Paying through several splits is the first thing people will build with this. A work says who its owners are and what share each has. A service that divides money makes a deal with the work, and the work names that deal as its receiver: its own deal with the service, never the service itself, nor an offer the service makes to everyone. That deal is signed on both sides: by the service, and by the work, as the work's own Terms require. The payer pays the work and needs to know nothing else. An owner that is itself a work is paid in the same way, and so one split pays into the next. All of that is built, by cMIPs: where the work is paid, what the service charges, how it divides, what happens to a late payment. The grammar gives only the blocks under it: the receiver that may be an agreement (this rule), the payment that names the version it read (rule 13), the report a leg may call for (rule 11), and who said each thing (rule 8).

**Precisely.**
- 1.1 Terms MAY name a party as an agreement, by its root fingerprint.
- 1.2 **Named, and signing.** Naming an agreement as a party asks nothing of it: it need not sign to be named, it is not counted by the default of rule 4.1 (rule 4.4), and where it has not signed it is not bound by any act of these Terms, and neither are its people. A party named as an agreement MAY sign: an assent to these Terms, where their `in_force` rule asks for it (rule 4.4). Its signing covers what those Terms carry, a call among them (rule 11.2). It signs as its own Terms require: every one of its signers, or what its own rule accepts (a vote, a named delegate). A verifier reads what they require from that agreement's Terms in force (rule 4.6); where it lacks them, the answer is **unchecked** (rule 9). By which acts that signing is shown, and what it binds that agreement's own people to, are not said here (P17).
- 1.3 Terms MAY name a `receiver`: one of their own parties, named as a signer or as a role; or another agreement, by its root fingerprint.
- 1.4 **Receiver now.** Whatever a payer, an act or a rule of one agreement gives or owes to a party named as an agreement, it gives or owes to that agreement's **receiver now**: the `receiver` named by the version of that agreement which the act that gives or owes names (rule 13.1). Where that act names no version, a verifier MUST take the receiver from that agreement's Terms in force, per that agreement's own place, qualified **per place** with that place named, or, where it names none, per the anchoring the reader accepts, or **as shown** (rules 4.6, 7, 13.4). Which Terms of that agreement are in force is answered by rules 4.5 to 4.8: while a fork of its Terms stands, the receiver now is the one named by the Terms both branches replace, and the verifier MUST show the fork with that answer and MUST NOT choose a branch (rule 4.7). Where the receiver so named is one of that agreement's own parties, the answer is that party. Where it is another agreement (1.3), that agreement is the next leg of a sequence: what reaches it is passed on as its own Terms and the cMIPs they name say, and is read across legs only as rule 11 says; these grammar rules read nothing more of it. If the version read names no receiver, or the spot named is not filled, the answer is **not shown**. If the verifier lacks that agreement's acts, it is **unchecked** (rule 9).
- 1.5 A rule (task K4) MAY take as an input a receiver now, so answered, or what a leg report states (rule 11.3).

**Lie exposed.** "I receive for that work", said by someone who once did and no longer does, or never did; and "that work was paid", when it was paid to a past receiver. Signed text in the paying agreement cannot say where another agreement is paid; only that agreement's own Terms can, and rule 1 sends the verifier there and labels each answer with where it came from. And (1.2) "the work agreed", said of a signing that the work's own Terms did not let those who signed make for it.

**Protects.** The people of the work pointed at, who did not sign the pointing agreement and need not: they are not bound by it (1.2), and what it owes their work reaches them by their own agreement's words, which no payer can change. Also the one who pays, who needs to know one thing, the work, and nothing of who its owners are. And the people of an agreement that does sign: it signs only as their own Terms require, so no one of them, and no service, signs for all of them.

**Reasoning.** Asked whether a party can be another agreement, Nobody, allegedly: "I think it is essential… it ensures a series of split is possible.", and: "The new agreement points to the other agreement as one party, no collective involved". So an agreement can be named as a party without being a group that must sign, and where it has not signed it is not bound. Its words are pinned (its root is a fingerprint), and what it says is read from its own versions, which is why rule 2, rule 7 and rule 13 together suffice. Without a receiver, something could only be "owed to an agreement", which is not a person, and the step from there to a person was made outside any rule; the receiver is that step, inside the grammar.

On whether a split needs a field of its own beside the receiver: "I mean, receiver still exists, it happen to also accept agreements hashes as input, no?" So there is one field, which takes an identity or an agreement's root (1.3). On what a work names for a split: "Almost, the target is not the split service but the agreement with the split service, not some kind of public offer." That is why the plain words above say a work names its own deal. What makes an agreement such a deal, what a verifier reads from it, what shows that a work was paid, and how a pay-out is matched against the shares, were written into this rule in earlier drafts; they are a solution built on the blocks, and are now the cMIP of the service (task K8; section 5). The grammar keeps the blocks.

On collectives: "A collective is an agreement, more than an ID, we gave it ID capabilities (keys) and then removed them. So, agreements as parties is nothing new"; and of the whole: "The key concept is « agreement as a party » then it’s what you build with it that changes". So this rule is named for the idea, not for one use of it, and the grammar defines no collective: governance is whatever powers an agreement's own Terms give (rule 5).

*Where it signs.* Earlier drafts went further, and said that an agreement named as a party never assents or signs. That was more than his words. Of the counter signature he had said: "Which for an agreement as party means, all signature or whatever satisfies the collective agreement". The MOR lead put the consequence to him: under that rule, in a deal with a service, only the service could make a call. He described the deal instead: "So, I am assuming everyone on the work signed an agreement that pointed to a receiver. So, the agreement with the service is signed by all, the report about the payment only comes from the receiver." And then, of the sentence "an agreement as a party never signs": "Also, if you keep that quote a collective becomes nearly impossible to build." So 1.2 no longer says it. An agreement signs as its own Terms require. "A small work by all its owners" is his "signed by all"; "a collective by a vote or a named delegate, as its rule says" is the MOR lead's wording for his "whatever satisfies the collective agreement". The exchange is about a deal. It does not say that every agreement named as a party must sign: "no collective involved" still holds for a work that is only pointed at. So the default of rule 4.1 is left as it was, and what else is not said is listed in P17.

### Rule 2. Whole

**Plain words.** Everything the agreement uses is pinned by fingerprint. A link to something that could change is not part of the agreement.

**Precisely.**
- 2.1 The `body`, every piece in `relies_on`, and every cMIP named anywhere in a Terms act MUST be named by fingerprint.
- 2.2 Anything a Terms act refers to in another way is not part of those Terms. A verifier MUST NOT read it as part of them.

**Lie exposed.** "The document we referred to said something else", and "there was a side piece". A signature over words that point at something changeable proves only that the pointer was signed. (#34 Whole, #1 Fix.)

**Protects.** Every reader who arrives later: newcomer, heir, buyer of a part, judge. Also the party who signed without fetching what was referred to. None of them chose how the reference was written.

**Reasoning.** A cMIP can be revised; a document can be edited; a name can come to mean something new. Naming cMIPs by fingerprint is what lets an agreement be read correctly after its cMIP's author is gone, which is the good-ancestor requirement applied to meaning. Rule 2 does not prove that nothing else exists outside the Terms; that is rule 7. An agreement named as a party is named by its root, which is a fingerprint; what it says *now* is not pinned by rule 2, and is read by rule 1.

### Rule 3. Author

**Plain words.** The agreement says whose words these are. If the words turn out to be unclear, the unclearness falls on them.

**Precisely.**
- 3.1 `authors` MUST name one or more of the parties named as signers or roles.
- 3.2 The authors are the parties who bear the failure of the words to mean one thing (#4 Align). A cMIP answering task K10 (Judge) MUST accept `authors` as an input.

**Lie exposed.** "We wrote this together" or "I meant something else by that word", said by the one who did write it. Signatures alone cannot show who wrote the words: every party signs the same Terms.

**Protects.** The party who accepted words they did not write, above all whoever takes up an open offer or fills a spot (rule 10), who never had a chance to change the words.

**Reasoning.** Whether two people meant the same thing is an inner state at a past moment; no operation reaches it (`agreement-grammar.md`: Mind). The research's answer is "do not try to prove; assign in advance who bears the failure", and the open offer shows the assignment: whoever writes the words carries their ambiguity. Rule 3 makes the assignment readable; what a judge does with it is the judge's task. It assigns Align only. Able (#3) and Free (#5) are not assigned by any rule; see section 7.

### Rule 4. In force

**Plain words.** Signing first does not bind you alone. Your assent counts only once all the assents the agreement asks for are present.

Terms stop being in force when Terms that replace them are in force. New Terms replace old ones only if they carry the signatures the old Terms require: everyone's, unless the old Terms gave a power to fewer. New Terms without those signatures replace nothing, and the old Terms simply go on. If the agreement names a place, the place says which Terms are the latest; if it names none, a verifier can say only which are latest among the acts it holds, "as shown". If two Terms acts each replace the same Terms and each carries those signatures, that is a fork (rule 12): a verifier shows both, and never picks one. While the fork stands, neither branch is in force: the Terms both replaced stay in force, and everything that rests on the Terms in force (who receives now, who fills a spot, how a rule divides) is answered from them, until a join ends the fork.

An agreement never ends. It only gets a new version: one with new conditions replaces the last one as any version does, carrying the signatures the last one requires. What happens to the work and its shares is in the new version's conditions; the grammar does not list the ways, and has no word for ending. Until such a version is in force, the last version stays in force and keeps protecting everyone in it: whoever acts against it alone, without the signatures it requires, has made nothing that replaces it.

Nothing counts until every party has signed. Until then an assent is no more than an offer held out to the others, and whoever gave it may take it back, by a **counter signature**: a signature that cancels the party's own earlier one. It is available only until every party has signed, and not after; from then on only a new version changes anything. Where one of the parties is itself an agreement, "signed", for that party, is whatever its own Terms require: all its signatures, or whatever satisfies its own rule.

**Precisely.**
- 4.1 Terms are in force when the assents their `in_force` rule requires are present. If `in_force` is absent, they are in force when there is an assent to them in the name of every party named as a signer.
- 4.2 Terms that name a party by role MUST carry `in_force`.
- 4.3 An assent binds its signer only while the Terms it names are in force.
- 4.4 A party named as an agreement is not counted by the default of 4.1. An `in_force` rule MAY ask for its signing; it has then signed when what its own Terms require is present (rule 1.2).
- 4.5 Terms stop being in force when Terms that replace them (rule 5.2, 4.8) are in force. While a fork of them stands (4.7), no Terms that replace them are in force.
- 4.6 Which Terms of an agreement are in force is an answer about what is latest. A verifier MUST take it per the agreement's place, qualified **per place** (rule 7.2); or, where no place is named, per the anchoring the reader accepts, with it named, or else **as shown** (rules 7.3, 13.4). It MUST NOT give it as latest without saying what the answer stands on.
- 4.7 Where two Terms acts each replace the same Terms (4.8), and each has the assents its own `in_force` requires (4.1), they are a fork (rule 12); a join is not one of them (rule 12.3). A verifier MUST answer **fork** for which Terms replace them, show both branches, and MUST NOT choose between them, by position or in any other way. Until the fork ends (rule 12.3), the Terms both branches replace stay in force, and a verifier MUST answer from them every answer that rests on the Terms in force: among others, a receiver now (rule 1.4), who fills a spot (rule 10.2), and what a rule the Terms name produces (rule 5, task K4). It MUST show the fork with each such answer, per place or as shown.
- 4.8 A Terms act replaces the Terms its `replaces` names only if it carries the signatures those Terms require for their replacement (rule 5.2): an assent to it in the name of every party bound by them, or what a power for replacement in them requires. A Terms act that does not carry them replaces nothing: the Terms it names stay in force, and it is not a branch of a fork. A verifier answers **invalid** for it as a replacement, per place or as shown, since an assent it does not hold may exist. For a delegation, whose signatures end it is said by rule 5.5.
- 4.9 Terms stop being in force only as 4.5 says: when a version replacing them is in force. No act ends Terms, or an agreement, without replacing them, and a verifier MUST NOT answer that Terms or an agreement have ended. An agreement only gets a new version: a verifier reads every replacing version alike (rules 4.8, 5.2), whatever its conditions say becomes of the work and its shares; what they say is theirs (rule 2, task K1). Every version replaced stays an act of the agreement, readable as it was (rule 2).
- 4.10 **Counter signature.** No assent binds anyone, and nothing in Terms counts, until those Terms are in force (4.1, 4.3). A counter signature is a Statement of kind `counter_signature`, by the signer of an assent, whose `terms` names the Terms that assent names, and which follows that assent in the signer's own line (rule 6.1). It cancels that assent, and no one else's: an assent so cancelled is not counted by 4.1. A counter signature is available only until every party has signed, which these grammar rules read as: until those Terms are in force (4.1). One made for Terms that are in force changes nothing: Terms in force change only as 4.5 and 4.9 say. Where a party is an agreement, "signed", for that party, is whatever that agreement's own Terms require: every one of its signers, or what its own rule accepts. A verifier reads that from that agreement's Terms in force (rule 4.6); where it lacks them, the answer is **unchecked** (rule 9). This is the reading of rule 1.2. A party named as an agreement is not counted by the default of 4.1 (4.4), so the reading has effect here only where the Terms' own `in_force` rule asks for that party's signing. Whether such a party can itself make a counter signature, and by which act, is not said (P17). Whether the Terms were in force before the counter signature is an answer about what is latest: per place, or as shown (4.6). A later assent by the same signer to the same Terms is counted as any assent is.

**Lie exposed.** "You signed, so you are bound", said by the party who never signed. Signed text shows that one side signed; it cannot by itself show that the signature was conditional on the other's. And (4.5 to 4.7) "these Terms still hold", said of Terms already replaced, and "these are the Terms now", said of one of two replacements while the other is hidden. Each Terms act is signed and looks valid on its own; only the chain of replacements, read per place or as shown, says which stopped, and two branches of it convict each other as a fork does. And (4.8) "these are our new Terms", said by some parties over the heads of others whose signatures the old Terms required. And (4.9) "this agreement is over", said by one party to escape what its last version still says, and "I can do this alone now", said against a version that requires others' signatures. And (4.10) "you signed, so you are held to it", said to a signer who took their assent back before the others had signed; and "I had cancelled my signature", said of Terms that were already in force.

**Protects.** The first to sign, who did not choose to be bound alone, and who is not left holding an open offer against themselves for as long as the others choose to wait (4.10). And whoever reads the agreement later and is shown an old version, or one branch of two: the payer looking for the receiver now, the newcomer, the judge. And (4.7, 4.8) the party left out of a replacement they never signed, and everyone who would otherwise be stuck with no Terms in force while a fork stands. And (4.9) everyone in the last version, which keeps binding until those whose signatures it requires agree a new one.

**Reasoning.** "Each party must hold the other's commitment, not their own" (#7 Exchange). Exchange may run one way until the other side acts, which is why 4.2 requires Terms with a spot to say how acceptance is recognised instead of falling back to a default that cannot apply to a role.

*When Terms stop.* A rule that says when Terms come into force and nothing about when they stop gives two answers to everything that rests on "Terms in force". Nobody, allegedly, on old Terms stopping: "that’s where a whole lot of special cases were born if I remember right", and, asked whether Terms stop being in force when Terms replacing them are in force, two competing replacements being a fork that is shown and never chosen: "Yes, less likely to bloat and overdefine." So rules 4.5 to 4.7 are one rule, read three ways: what stops (4.5), where the answer comes from (4.6), and what two answers mean (4.7). With 4.3, an assent to replaced Terms stops binding its signer once the replacing Terms are in force; the signer is then bound by the replacing Terms only through rule 5.2.

*Who can replace.* On a case of two replacements each signed by two of three parties: "Both term 2a and 2b are invalid as they do not carry the required signatures that were in term 1." So 4.8: a replacement replaces only with the signatures the replaced Terms require; where those are everyone's, two replacements signed by different two-thirds are not a fork at all, and the first Terms stay in force. Asked whether, for a real fork (the Terms let fewer than all replace them, or the same people sign two replacements), the Terms both branches replaced stay in force until it is settled: "It does, and more sense than anyone ending up stuck in the first one". So 4.7 keeps the fork shown and unchosen, and answers from the Terms both branches replaced. How a fork ends is rule 12, for every kind of act.

*Never ended.* Asked whether ending an agreement is a replacement by Terms that say "ended": "An agreement never ends, it can resolve. It’s a new version of the agreement with new conditions signed by all signers of previous version"; and, asked whether that needs everyone who signed the previous version even where the Terms let fewer change them: "Terms rule, because they were agreed by all signers". So ending needs no act, field or answer of its own: it is a replacement whose conditions say what becomes of the work and its shares, signed as 4.8 already says. What 4.9 adds is that nothing else stops Terms: there is no "ended" for a verifier to compute. His example shows why: owners who all agree to make a work public and free, and one of them then selling it alone; the version that made it free is still in force, and "the previous agreement says « sorry can’t do this alone, the other two signatures are needed »". ("Resolve" was his word of the moment and is not a term of this text.)

*Counter signature.* Asked whether an assent may be taken back until the Terms are in force: "Yes, a counter signature is a good idea. Nothing counts until all parties have signed,". Asked what he meant by it: "Yes, I meant it as a signature that cancels the previous one, this becomes unavailable once all parties have signed. Which for an agreement as party means, all signature or whatever satisfies the collective agreement". So the counter signature is the party's own signature, cancelling its own earlier one, and it is a second defined kind of Statement. "Once all parties have signed" is read as "once the Terms are in force": by default that is every party named as a signer (4.1), and where the Terms carry their own `in_force` rule (as every agreement with a spot must, 4.2), it is what that rule asks (section 8.3). His second sentence is the first place where he says that an agreement as a party signs. Earlier drafts kept beside it a rule that such a party never does; that rule is dropped, on his later words (rule 1, reasoning). 4.10 says how "signed" is read for such a party where Terms ask for it. What is still not said is in P17. Withdrawing is still a race against the last assent (section 7).

The grammar reads the counter signature itself, instead of leaving it to a cMIP, because Terms with no `in_force` rule name no cMIP that could.

### Rule 5. Own signature

**Plain words.** Nobody is bound by anything they did not sign. The only exception is an agreement they did sign that gives someone a power over them, and then the act must name that agreement at the time it is made, not afterwards.

A grant of authority is a delegation: someone lets an agent act for them. The one who delegates may be a person, or a business, a collective, which is an agreement: then there is no "you" any more, there is the agreement as a party, and an agent acting for it is the same delegation in another flavour. Whoever delegates can end the delegation, at any time: that is the default. The agreement may add signed conditions to that (a period of notice, a fixed term, a cause), and then it ends as they say. The grammar does not defend the agent's job: pay, notice and severance are for cMIPs the parties choose. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "we’re not Law."

The grammar puts no limit on what such a signed condition may say. Even a delegation that only the agent may end is not "never": nobody knows whether the agent will ever use that freedom, so the honest answer is "unknown". And it is legible: the condition stands in the signed Terms, for anyone to read before relying on the delegation.

**Precisely.**
- 5.1 A person is bound by an act if, and only if:
  - (a) they signed it in their own name, or
  - (b) the act's `for` names them, its `under` names Terms by which they are bound and which are in force, those Terms give a power for acts of this kind, and that power's rule, applied to the acts held, says yes.
- 5.2 A replacing Terms act (one with `replaces`) binds a party only through 5.1: by that party's own assent to it, or by a power for replacement in the Terms it replaces. Its `replaces` is read as its `under`.
- 5.3 An act with no `for` and no `under` is made in its signer's own name only. It MUST NOT later be counted as made for anyone else.
- 5.4 Where the person named in `for` is bound only through Terms that have been replaced, a verifier MUST answer from rule 7: per place if the Terms name one, otherwise as shown. (Whether Terms have been replaced is answered by rules 4.5 to 4.8; Terms are not ended otherwise, rule 4.9.)
- 5.5 **Delegation.** Terms in which one party, the **delegator**, gives another, the **agent**, a power to make acts for it are a delegation. The delegator may be named as a signer or as an agreement (rule 1.1): these grammar rules read the two as the same delegation. By default the delegator can end it at any time: a version of those Terms that removes or narrows the power replaces them (rule 4.8) when it carries the delegator's part alone, without the agent's. For a delegator named as a signer, that is its own assent. For a delegator named as an agreement, it is what that agreement's own Terms say (their rule). The Terms of a delegation MAY state conditions on that ability (a notice, a term, a cause); such a version then replaces them only as those conditions say. A verifier reads them from those Terms, in the form their cMIP gives (rule 2, task K1); where it cannot, the answer is **unchecked** (rule 9). These grammar rules put no limit on what such a condition may say, and a verifier reads every one alike. Under a condition that leaves ending to the agent alone, a version carrying the delegator's part alone replaces nothing (rule 4.8). A verifier MUST NOT answer that such a delegation can never be ended: whether the agent will end it is **not shown**, and the verifier shows the condition as the Terms the delegator signed state it. These grammar rules give an agent no protection of their own: pay, notice, severance and the like are cMIPs the parties choose. Once a delegation has been so ended, 5.4 says how an act made under it is read.

**Lie exposed.** "She bound you", "the group decided", "I was acting for him" (said after the fact), "I was still authorised". And (5.5) "you cannot take your authority back without me", said by an agent whose delegation states no such condition; "this can never be taken back", said of a delegation whose signed condition leaves ending to the agent; and "I can still end it alone", said by a delegator who signed that only the agent may. A signature shows who signed; it cannot show that the signer could bind someone else. The pointer in `under` lets anyone check the power against the principal's own signature. (#27 Authorise, #28 Scope, #29 Name, #30 Decide, #14 Admit, #15 Bind, #47 Amend, #48 End.)

**Protects.** The person bound through someone else: the principal, the outvoted member, the absent party. Also the counterparty of an agent, who can check the power before relying on it.

**Reasoning.** "A group deciding and a person acting for another are the same: a few bind others, within limits. Amending an agreement is one more decision of that kind" (`agreement-tasks.md`, what collapsed). So one rule covers agency, groups, judges, admission and amendment. A grant of authority is itself Terms: the principal is author and party, the agent is party, and the power names the kinds of act and the rule that limits them. Revoking it is a new version of those Terms (rule 4.9).

With no power named, a new version needs every party, which would let an agent block their own dismissal. On what a grant is, Nobody, allegedly: "Wait… act as you… but as a business… a collective… therefore an agreement, there is no « you » anymore, there is agreement as a party… an agent acts for an ID different flavor of the same delegation". So the grant is a delegation, and its delegator may be an agreement as well as an identity; nothing separate is written for a business. Asked whether the core guarantees only that a delegator can always take its delegation back, or a grant can be made irrevocable by its own terms: "I lean a. The default is delegator can end it, but with provision that an agreement can add conditions to its ability to do so." So 5.5 is a default, not a guarantee beyond the reach of what was signed: the delegator ends it alone unless signed conditions say how. And on protecting the agent: "Yes, we’re not Law. So, core defending employment rights is not a good idea. But, a cMIP should be able to do so if the parties need it." 5.5 is written only for a delegation by one delegator to an agent. A power shared among several (a vote, a judge's decision) is not read as a delegation each of them can end alone (rule 4.8).

Asked whether a condition amounting to "never" (a delegation that only the agent may end) is the one limit on conditions: "Dunno if the agent will ever act on its freedom… so it’s a I don.t know, not a never". The case was put to him as the one possible limit, and he did not take it as one. So 5.5 writes no limit and adds no check: a verifier reads such a condition as it reads any other. What it must not do is turn "unknown" into "never". What the delegator gives up by signing it is a stated cost (section 7).

The default is in the grammar, although a delegation's own Terms could give the same power to the delegator alone, because it is his stated default and it is the way out for whoever delegates.

A newcomer is bound (#15 Bind) by their own assent to the Terms in force when they join; their assent names exactly that Terms act, so they are not bound by a version they never saw, except through powers in it they accepted. Filling a spot (rule 10) does not bind anyone either: the signer who fills it is bound only by their own assent, under 5.1.

There is no default power. An agreement that names no power to replace itself can be replaced (rule 4.9) only by every party's own assent. This is the conservative way out, and its cost is in section 7.

### Rule 6. Own chain

**Plain words.** Each person's acts in one agreement are a single line: each one points at that person's previous one. If two acts point at the same previous one, that person has told two stories, and that is proven without a judge.

**Precisely.**
- 6.1 Every act within an agreement MUST name in `previous` its signer's previous act within the same agreement, or none if it is the signer's first.
- 6.2 Two acts by one signer within one agreement that name the same `previous` (or that both name none) are a fork. A verifier holding both MUST answer **fork** for that signer, and MUST NOT choose between the branches. The fork ends only as rule 12 says.

**Lie exposed.** Showing one history to one person and another history to another (equivocation), and hiding an act in the middle of one's own line. Without a single shared order, two signed acts can each look like the latest; the pointer turns the contradiction into proof (`agreement-grammar.md`, second test: "a fork convicts itself").

**Protects.** The stranger who is shown only one branch, and any party who has no place to look.

**Reasoning.** Rule 6 is checkable by anyone holding the two acts, with no outsider. It does not stop the lie, it makes it provable afterwards, which deters only where the liar is known and has something to lose (section 7). What a proven fork costs its signer is their standing with whoever sees it, which anyone may compute (task K13). It cannot reveal that a signer's line has a later act that was not shown; only rule 7 answers "nothing later". What a fork removes from later answers, while it stands, is open (P9); how it ends is rule 12.

### Rule 7. Place to look

**Plain words.** An agreement may name one place where its acts are lodged in order. Then the questions "is this the latest?", "is this all of it?" and "who holds this now?" have answers, and anything not lodged there does not count. Without a place, nobody can prove that nothing else exists, and a verifier must say so. The place an agreement names is the one everyone uses for that agreement, and so is the clock it names. Only where it names none may a reader accept an anchoring of its own choosing for those questions; the answer then says which.

**Precisely.**
- 7.1 Terms MAY name a `place`.
- 7.2 Where the Terms in force name a place, an act within the agreement counts for any answer about what is latest (including which Terms are in force, rule 4.6), what is whole, who holds what, whether a period has passed, or whether something was made public, only once the place shows it, and at the position the place gives it. A verifier MUST take those answers from the place, qualified **per place**.
- 7.3 Where no place is named, a verifier MUST qualify every such answer **as shown**, or **per anchoring** where the reader accepts one, with it named (rule 13.4). It MUST NOT give such an answer as latest or whole without saying what it stands on.
- 7.4 The place the Terms in force name is the one every reader uses for that agreement, whether a party to it or not: a verifier MUST NOT give an answer of 7.2 for that agreement per another anchoring. Terms MAY also name a `clock`. Where the Terms in force name one, what time it was, for that agreement, is answered per that clock by every reader, qualified **per clock** (rules 8.2, 13.4). What a clock is, and how it is identified, is for the layer that gives it (section 8.1).

**Lie exposed.** "There is nothing later", "there is no side piece", "nobody else holds it", "you were told" (by publication), "the deadline has passed". A document cannot prove an absence; only a single place to look can (`agreement-tasks.md`, what collapsed: Current, Whole, Alive). (#33 Current, #34 Whole, #11 Title, #12 Sum, #13 Transfer, #32 Notify, #36 Wait.)

**Protects.** Everyone who arrives later and must rely on "nothing else exists": the newcomer, the buyer of a part, the heir, the judge, the counterparty of an agent whose delegation may have been replaced.

**Reasoning.** This is the closed-world rule ("what is not in the register does not count"), which the research calls a design decision, not a discovery. It is made local: each agreement names its own place, so "a thing's ownership is exactly as global as its witness set". Rule 7 is a MAY because a bilateral agreement about deeds barely needs a shared order; the research finds that one is needed for exactly two things, scarcity and freshness. Rule 7.3 is the price of not naming one, stated to the reader instead of hidden. Changing the place is a replacement of Terms and goes through rule 5. Rule 1 asks of another agreement which of its versions is in force; that answer is per *that* agreement's place, or, where it names none, per the anchoring the reader accepts (rule 13.4).

On whether a reader may set aside the place or the clock an agreement names, Nobody, allegedly: "Whatever the agreement states as reference clock is the clock everyone has to use for that agreement." So 7.4: the place and the clock an agreement names are everyone's for that agreement, and a reader's free choice of anchoring is what is left where it names none (rule 13.4). His sentence names the clock; the place was in the question it answers, and the two are read together.

### Rule 8. Say who said it

**Plain words.** Whether a thing was delivered, whether it was what was promised, how much there was, whether someone is alive, what time it was, whether money moved, who filled a spot, what a leg passed on, how often something was seen: an agreement can record who said so, never make it so. A verifier always reports who said it. Where an act is tied to a payment, it may name the settlement: the Money layer's own proof, plugged in here and reported on the word of whoever gave it.

**Precisely.**
- 8.1 For any answer about the world (a deed done, a thing's quality or quantity, an event, a person's presence or absence), about time, or about value under Control, a verifier MUST report who stated it: the one who performed, the one who received, a named witness, the controller, or the place.
- 8.2 A time written inside an act is reported as its signer's claim. Only a place gives a position. What time it was is answered per the clock the agreement names (rule 7.4); where it names none, only per an anchoring the reader accepts, with it named (rule 13.4).
- 8.3 A verifier MUST NOT report value as moved on the strength of an Agreements act alone. An act can instruct Control; whether Control obeyed is the controller's or the receiver's signed word.
- 8.4 Who fills a spot (rule 10) and what a leg received and passed on (rule 11) are answers about the world. A verifier MUST report them with whoever confirmed them.
- 8.5 **Settlement.** A Statement MAY name a `settlement` it is tied to: the evidence, from the layer that holds value, that a payment was made. This field is the socket where that layer's proof plugs into Agreements; what a settlement is, is that layer's to say. A settlement named is reported on the word of whoever gave it (8.3). What is done with it (counting only what led to a settlement, dividing what one shows) is a rule's (task K4).

**Lie exposed.** A claim laundered into a fact: "it was delivered" when only the deliverer said so; a backdated act; "you were paid" when only the payer says so; "I am the one who did it" when only the claimant says so. Signed text proves that the signer said it, and nothing more; this rule stops a verifier's answer from saying more than that.

**Protects.** The receiver, who did not choose to be declared paid or served, and every reader who would otherwise take a verifier's summary as fact.

**Reasoning.** The research's largest residue is truth: whenever an agreement touches the world, "someone must say what happened, and their signature proves only that they said it". And "the record cannot hold value": every entry about value is a claim or an instruction, neither is the value. Rule 8 does not rank sources; whether a receiver's acknowledgment (#20) or three witnesses suffice is a rule (task K4) chosen in the Terms. On the settlement, Nobody, allegedly: "The settlement proof is provided by the Money layer but needs to plug somewhere no?" The field is that somewhere, and the grammar says nothing of what must be done with it: a rule may ask for it, as in his words "Show me referees that lead to settlement only" (he meant "referrers"), or not, since "Advertisement industry can still build a model that still rewards pure attention, if they wish."

### Rule 9. Unchecked is not passed

**Plain words.** If a verifier lacks something it needs to check (a cMIP it does not have, a place it cannot reach, the acts of an agreement named as a party), it says "unchecked". It never says "fine" or "broken" instead.

**Precisely.**
- 9.1 Where a verifier cannot apply a rule it needs, because it lacks a cMIP named by fingerprint, lacks an act named by fingerprint, lacks the acts of an agreement named as a party, or cannot get an answer from a named place, it MUST answer **unchecked** for every answer that depends on it.

**Lie exposed.** "My software says it checks out", from software that could not check. Signed text cannot expose what a verifier silently skipped.

**Protects.** Anyone relying on someone else's verification, and the parties of an agreement whose cMIP has become rare.

**Reasoning.** Because every agreement can name any cMIP, verifiers will always meet ones they do not have. Without rule 9, the convenient path is to treat the unknown as passing. With it, an agreement written today stays honestly readable when its cMIPs are old. Rule 9 alone would leave a whole sequence unchecked wherever one leg's cMIP is missing; rule 11 is what keeps the sequence readable there, where that leg called for reports and was answered, for a reader who holds those reports (rule 11.6).

### Rule 10. Spots

**Plain words.** Some participants are not known when the agreement is written. The agreement keeps a spot for each of them, and says what earns it: having done something, having received something from the one before, holding something, or being someone who fits a description. Whoever proves it fills the spot, once for each proof: the same spot can be earned again by a later proof, and each filling is tied to the proof that earned it. Whether a spot holds one person or many is for the spot's cMIP to say. For those nobody could foresee in any of these four ways, a fifth, open kind of spot lets a cMIP say both who the participant is and how they are confirmed. The grammar does not say how anything is proven: the cMIP the spot names does. The grammar names no use of a spot. It only says that the spot, and its cMIP, are fixed in advance, and that each filling is reported with its proof and whoever confirmed it.

An open offer does not end. It can stop taking new people: by a new version, or by a condition signed in advance ("open until June", "first 100 buyers"). Whether a spot still takes new people is for its cMIP to say, from the version in force. Whatever it says, everyone who already filled the spot keeps their filling, so that they can still point to a valid agreement that says so.

Whoever earned a filling has the rights the agreement offered when they earned it: exactly those, with whatever conditions that version put on them (a time limit, a creator's statement, anything its cMIPs read). The grammar does not care what the conditions are. No later version changes them unless the holder signs it.

A version can name one condition, signed in advance, under which what applied no longer applies. The condition itself says what it stops: the rights, the offer to new people, or both. When it stops rights, a verifier says so: a filling, and the rights it holds, are **in force**, or **no longer in force, by its own condition**, and the condition is always named beside the answer. That is not the same as an act that never counted, for which "invalid" is the word. Whether the holder is still a party depends on how things are set up: the version says, and the grammar defines nothing more. And nothing stops the agreement from going on afterwards: a new version can offer again, the offer renewed, and it can plug in values already in the chain (the earlier payment, the earlier proof) or not.

**Precisely.**
- 10.1 A role in `parties` MUST name:
  - its **kind**, one of: **done** (whoever proves they have done X), **received** (whoever proves they have received Y), **holding** (whoever proves they are holding Z), **fits** (whoever proves their identity fits the conditions in W), or **future**;
  - its `what`: the X, Y, Z or W, by fingerprint (rule 2), except for kind future (10.4);
  - the cMIP answering task K12 that says who fills it, by fingerprint.
- 10.2 A spot is filled, by a signer, when its K12 cMIP, applied to the acts held and the place's answers, names that signer on the strength of a **proof**: the acts that earn the spot. A spot MAY be filled many times; each filling is tied to the proof that earned it. Whether a spot holds one filling or many is said by its K12 cMIP. A verifier MUST answer each filling with its proof, each act reported by who signed it (rule 8.4), qualified per place or as shown (rule 7). The role and its cMIP are read from the Terms in force; while a fork of Terms stands, from the Terms both branches replace (rule 4.7).
- 10.3 Each signer who fills a spot is a party in it from then on. They are bound only by their own assent (rule 5). Filling a spot does not bind any other party.
- 10.4 A role of kind **future** names no `what`: its K12 cMIP defines who the participant is as well as how they are confirmed. It is for participants none of the four kinds can describe in advance. No verifier can check that a future spot was used only for them; whether it was is the authors' word, carried by them (rule 3).
- 10.5 **New identities.** Whether a spot still takes new identities is said by its K12 cMIP, applied to the version in force (the role as that version states it, and what the rule in its `condition` says, 10.7), the acts held, and the answers of the place and the clock the agreement names (rule 7). These grammar rules define no act and no field for closing a spot or for opening it again: an offer stops taking new identities, or takes them again, by a new version (rule 4.8) or by a condition signed in advance, as that cMIP reads them. A verifier MUST give that answer with its cause, as the cMIP gives it, per place, per clock, per the anchoring named or as shown (rules 7, 13.4). Whatever that answer is, a filling already earned stays a filling, shown with its proof (10.2), and keeps the rights of the version it was earned under (10.6). Where the verifier lacks the cMIP, the answer is **unchecked** (rule 9).
- 10.6 **Rights.** A filling has exactly the rights offered by the version it was earned under, with every condition that version puts on them. The version a filling was earned under is the version its proof names (rule 13.1). What the rights and their conditions are is that version's (rule 2, task K1); these grammar rules do not read them. No later version changes the rights of a filling already earned unless it carries an assent to it signed by that filling's holder in their own name (rule 5.1(a)). A verifier MUST name, with each filling, the version whose offer it holds: the version it was earned under, or a later one only where it holds the holder's own assent to that later version.
- 10.7 **No longer in force, by its own condition.** A filling, and the rights it holds (10.6), are in force from when the filling is earned. Terms MAY name in `condition` a rule (task K4), by fingerprint (rule 2), with its parameters: one condition, signed in advance, under which what applied under that version no longer applies. The rule itself says what it stops: the rights that version offers, the offer to new identities, or both; these grammar rules do not say which, and a condition that stops one does not by that stop the other. Where the rule named by the version a filling was earned under (10.6), applied to that filling, the acts held and the answers of the anchoring, says that its rights have stopped, a verifier MUST answer **no longer in force** for that filling, and MUST name the condition beside it, with what met it, as the rule gives it: an act, reported by who signed it (rule 8), or a time, per the clock the agreement names (rule 7.4), or, where it names none, per the anchoring named or as shown (rule 13.4). Such a filling stays a filling, shown with its proof (10.2): what applied before no longer applies, and nothing is undone. Where the rule says that the offer has stopped, that is read by the spot's K12 cMIP (10.5). These grammar rules define nothing more for it. Whether the holder of such a filling is still a party in the spot (10.3), and whatever else follows, are as the version it was earned under states; a verifier reads them from that version (the rule it names in `condition`, and its Terms in the form their cMIP gives, rule 2) and MUST NOT answer beyond what it states. Nothing stops a later version: one made as rule 4.8 says MAY offer again, and MAY rest what it offers on values already in the chain, named by fingerprint (rules 2, 13.2), or on none. Who fills a spot under it is said by its own K12 cMIP (10.2), and a filling under it has the rights that version offers (10.6); the earlier filling is still answered **no longer in force**, under the version it was earned under. A verifier MUST NOT answer **no longer in force** under a rule the filling's own version does not name (10.6). It MUST NOT give that answer for an act that never counted (Terms never in force, a replacement that replaces nothing, a claim that fills no spot): that is answered **invalid** where the acts held show it never counted, and otherwise **not shown**. Where the verifier lacks the rule, the answer is **unchecked** (rule 9).

**Lie exposed.** "I am the one who did it", "it was passed to me", "I hold it", "I am one of them", said after the fact, with a confirmer chosen after the fact. Without a spot fixed in advance, the one who pays decides afterwards who earned it, and the claimant cannot check that the test was the one promised (`late-roles.md`: "The payer as sole confirmer appears in the affiliate and bounty cases"). With it, the test is pinned by fingerprint before anyone claims, and the answer always says on whose word the spot was filled. And (10.5) "the offer was withdrawn, so your filling is gone", said to someone who earned the spot while it was open. And (10.6, 10.7) "the terms have changed, so what you bought is gone", said by an author over a later version the holder never signed; and "that licence never counted", said of one that counted and then stopped, or "it still holds", said of one whose own version says it has stopped.

**Protects.** Whoever earns a spot and was not there to sign: the referrer, the heir, the holder, the later contributor, the beneficiary. Also every other party, who agreed in advance to the test a stranger must pass. And (10.5) whoever filled a spot before it stopped taking new people, who keeps a valid agreement to point to. And (10.6, 10.7) whoever paid for or earned rights, who keeps exactly what was offered when they earned it; and the later reader, who is told whether a filling never counted, or counted and stopped, and why.

**Reasoning.** On participants who arrive later, Nobody, allegedly: "some participants are not tied to a specific ID when the agreement is drafted, they are tied to a role that will be linked to an ID in the future." And the four kinds are his: "Prove that you have done X and earn spot 1. Prove you have received Y and earn spot 2. Prove that you are holding Z and earn spot 3 (relays for example) Prove that your ID fits the conditions in W and earn spot 4". `late-roles.md` finds that about eighteen recorded roles link to a person in only four ways: "by **doing** something, by **receiving** from the previous holder, by **holding** an object, or by **being** someone who fits a description"; his four spots name the same four. The kinds are in the grammar, although the fifth could carry them all, because a kind says what sort of answer a verifier is reporting (a deed, a chain, a holding, an identity) and so whom it rests on; *how* each is proven is the K12 cMIP's question. The fifth kind exists because the research shows roles no agreement anticipated (the unknowing source, the rescuer, the owed but unfindable, the contributor recognised afterwards, the early user), where "someone with judgment decides afterwards". His words for it: "Maybe a customizable “future participants” with the cMIP specifying how that participant is defined and confirmed". A future spot lets an agreement name that judgment in advance, by its cMIP.

Asked whether a spot is earned per proof, not once: "Yes, it leverages the above discussion as intended I think". So 10.2 ties each filling to its proof, and the same spot can be earned again by a later proof without a new rule. Asked where holding stops and being seen starts: "I don’t think it should be defined, what matters is “HOLD” with cMIP defined what the proof is, no?" So no use of a kind is named here.

On withdrawing an open offer: "The offer does not end, it is simply not open to new IDs anymore. Whoever bought the rights should still be able to point to a valid agreement that says so". The second sentence is what the grammar keeps (10.5, last part; 10.6). How an offer stops taking new identities, or takes them again (a mark in a new version, a date, a count of buyers), is something a spot's cMIP can compute from the version in force, so the grammar writes no act and no field for it (section 5).

Asked whether an offer's author could sign that earlier licences no longer count: "Whoever paid the agreement has the rights the agreement offer." Asked whether that version can itself limit the rights: "That version could have conditions tied to time, creators statements… bla bla bla… core does not care". So 10.6 says only whose offer a filling holds (its own version's), and reads none of the conditions. He then asked that the agreement be able to give a verifier what it needs to answer that rights have stopped; 10.7 gives it that say, through one rule named in advance. Asked directly what stopping changes: "The question depends on how things are setup. Void simply means, watever applied before this does not apply anymore… it is updating the agreement within its conditions. Nothing stops the agreement to be updated again. So, offering again, and plug in values already in the chain (or not) is an option". Then: "So techincally void is the wrong term… updated within its specified conditions (some updates stop the offer, some don’t)". Read as a whole: there is no state to be in or to leave. A condition the version signed in advance has been met, and what applied before no longer applies. So there is one notion, a condition, which may or may not stop the offer, and 10.7 has one field for it. The wording "in force" and "no longer in force, by its own condition" is the MOR lead's, chosen for him: "void" is not a term, and neither is "updated", since nothing in MOR is updated, only published or signed again. Coming back is not leaving a state: it is the agreement offered again.

### Rule 11. Legs of a sequence

**Plain words.** One click may have to climb: a payment reaches a work, part of it goes on to the works that work stands on, and part of that goes on again. Each agreement on the way is a leg. Each leg chooses its own cMIPs: one work may use one split service, the next another, and nothing has to be chosen for the whole climb. That freedom has a price. A reader who lacks the cMIP of one leg could not read what that leg did, and so could not tell whether the click reached the people beyond it.

What lets the whole be read is a block that belongs to all of MOR, not to Agreements: **the report**. In short (this is a summary; the block is the shared language's, section 8.1): some work is worth hearing back from, and most is not. So any task may carry a **call** for a report, or not. The call says from whom a report is expected (the one who did the work, the one it was done to, or both) and where each report is expected. Only those who signed the act that holds the task can make the call, and it binds nobody else. A **report** is a signed act that answers a call. Whatever the task, it carries three things: the call it answers, the acts it relied on, and who signs it. Each side signs only its own half, and an act that both halves name ties them together. A call nobody answered is shown as unanswered, and a task with no call as "no report called for": neither is read as anything else.

Agreements uses that block in one place. Passing money on is the Control task (K8). The Terms that hold it may carry a call. Most often those are a service's deal with a work. The work signed that deal as much as the service did, so the call in it is the work's people's as much as the service's. They can also be a work's own Terms, where one of its owners receives for the others. A **leg report** is a report that answers that call. What it says beyond the block is K8's to say: what was received, what was passed on and to whom, and what was kept, apart from what is still owed, each with its amount.

No leg is made to call for reports, and nobody is made to answer a call. But a call is written in signed Terms. So a reader sees, before paying, which legs called for reports. Which calls were answered is seen by those the reports go to ("One leg", below).

**Every leg is the same exchange.** Whoever carries out an obligation reports that they did. Whoever received confirms, by a report of their own; on some ways of paying, that confirmation can be automatic. Sometimes the one who received holds money for others: a band member who receives for the whole band. Then it does not end there. That person now has an obligation of their own, to pass the money on within the band's own agreement, and the same exchange happens there: they report that they did, and each bandmate confirms. No call is attached to a receiver as such. A call is always attached to a task, in the agreement under which that task is carried out.

**One leg.** A report travels one leg, and no further. It answers the call of its own leg, and goes where that call says, to the people who signed it. The grammar carries it no further, up or down: nobody outside a leg can call for that leg's reports, and no report is made to carry another leg's reports along. Sometimes someone further away does want to see. A partner who wants to check that a split among people was really carried out cannot trust the one who received; it can trust only the end of the waterfall. That can be built, but it is not the grammar's: it is for data cMIPs focused on agreements, and only where the people who would be seen agreed to it. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "People have to AGREE to be under surveillance, in an agreement they signed." And it stays in Agreements. It is never to be extended to the other layers of MOR: "Or surveillance become cheap and omnipresent."

With that, a reader who holds each leg's reports can follow a click both ways: down from the payer, and up from a contributor. Who holds them is what "one leg" settles: the signers of each leg hold their own, and anyone else only where that leg agreed. From a leg, the reader goes to where that leg's call says its reports are expected. Two reports are tied by the act they both name: for a payment, the settlement. The one who paid names it as passed on, the one who received names it as received, and neither needs to know of the other's report. And because each states its amounts, two reports that disagree are shown side by side. Whether a leg passed on what its own terms required is still that leg's cMIP's to say; the report only makes the whole readable.

**Precisely.**
- 11.1 **Legs.** Where what one agreement gives or owes reaches another agreement (a party named as an agreement, rule 1.1; a receiver that is an agreement, rule 1.3), and so on from one to the next, those agreements are a **sequence**, and each is a **leg** of it. The cMIPs a leg uses are the ones its own Terms name (rule 2). Nothing in one leg's choice binds another leg, and nothing is chosen for a sequence as a whole. Legs MAY name the same cMIPs; none is required to.
- 11.2 **The call.** Terms hold the Control task (K8) where they name a cMIP that answers it (rule 2); or where they name as `receiver` one of their own parties (rule 1.3) and give a part to any other party, so that this receiver holds for the others and passing on to them is its obligation under those Terms. Terms that hold the task MAY carry a call for it, in `calls`, as the report block of the shared language gives it (section 8.1): from whom a report is expected (the doer, the receiver, or both), and where each report is expected. For K8, the **doer** is whoever carried out the passing on (the controller: a service, or a party that receives for the others), and a **receiver** is whoever something was passed on to; where that is an agreement, the receiver who reports is that agreement's receiver now (rule 1.4), and no other of its people. The call is made by the signers of those Terms, and binds no one else. In a deal, those signers are the service and the agreement it serves, which signs as its own Terms require (rule 1.2): the call is that agreement's people's as much as the service's. A call is attached to a task, in the Terms under which that task is carried out; none is attached to a receiver. These grammar rules make no Terms carry a call, and make no one answer one. A call is part of signed Terms and is read as they are (rules 2, 4.6), by anyone, before any payment is made.
- 11.3 **Leg report.** A leg report is a report that answers such a call. As every report does, it carries the call it answers and the acts it relied on, each by fingerprint, and its signer. Among the acts it relied on are the version of the leg's Terms it passed on by (rule 13.2) and each settlement it speaks of (rule 8.5). Beyond that, it carries what K8's row says (section 5). It is its signer's word (rule 8.4), and each signer reports its own half only. What K8's row asks of a leg report MUST be readable without any cMIP the leg names: a verifier that lacks a leg's cMIPs still reads its leg reports, and reports what they state as stated by their signers. Whether what was passed on is what that leg's Terms and cMIPs require is answered by those cMIPs, and is **unchecked** where the verifier lacks them (rule 9); that does not make the leg report itself unchecked.
- 11.4 **Reading the whole.** A verifier asked about a result that depends on a sequence MUST read it leg by leg, from the reports it holds (11.6), and give each leg's part with who signed it (rule 8.4).
  - (a) **Where to look.** For each leg, the verifier reads, from the Terms that hold its Control task, whether they carry a call, and where the call says each report is expected. It looks for that leg's reports there. Where a leg report states something as passed on to an agreement, what received it is that agreement's receiver now (rule 1.4). Where that is another agreement, that agreement is the next leg, and its reports are looked for where its own call says: not within the agreement the report names. Where it is one of that agreement's own parties, holding for the others, the next leg is that agreement itself, with that party as its doer (11.2, 11.5), and its reports are looked for where its own call says.
  - (b) **The tie.** Two reports that name the same act are tied by it: for a payment, the settlement by which one side passed on and the other received (rule 8.5). Neither report names the other. At each leg these are the doer's report and a receiver's confirmation, which answer the same call (11.5). As the block asks, the verifier shows, for each such act, **matched**, **mismatched** (both shown, neither chosen), or **one side only**.
  - (c) **Amounts.** Two leg reports that name the same settlement match where they state the same amount for it (K8's row). Where they do not, the verifier MUST show both, with the amount each states, and MUST NOT choose between them.
  - (d) **Silence.** As the block asks, the verifier shows a call with no report as **unanswered**, and Terms that hold the Control task and carry no call as **no report called for**. Neither is read as anything else: not as kept, not as failed. From such a leg, nothing ties what lies beyond it to what reached it: as part of this result, the legs beyond are **not shown**, though their own reports are read as any are. **Unanswered** is said only by a verifier that reads where the call says its reports are expected. A verifier that does not, because that leg's reports travel no further than the leg (11.6), MUST NOT show the call as unanswered: for it, that leg's reports are **not shown**. Where the verifier lacks an act a report names, or the Terms of a leg, that part is **unchecked** (rule 9).
  - (e) **Before paying.** Asked which legs of a sequence called for reports, a verifier answers from their Terms alone (11.2), leg by leg, per place or as shown (rule 4.6), and **unchecked** where it lacks them. It answers in the same way which legs' Terms name a cMIP by which their signers agreed to be read beyond their leg (11.6).
- 11.5 **The same exchange at every leg.** At every leg the exchange of reports is the same. The doer's leg report states that it carried out the passing on. A receiver's leg report confirms what it received. How a receiver's report comes to be made is its own business and its way of paying's: on some ways of paying it can be automatic (P20). A receiver that holds for others (11.2), or that is the hand of the next leg, then has that leg's obligation: it passes on under that leg's own Terms, and reports there as its doer, under that leg's call where those Terms carry one. These grammar rules attach no call to a receiver, and read no report as reporting on another report: each report speaks for its own signer, of its own leg.
- 11.6 **One leg.** A report answers the call of its own leg (11.2) and is expected where that call says, by the signers of the Terms that carry it. These grammar rules carry it no further. A call MUST NOT be read as expecting a report on a task held by other Terms than the ones that carry it. No one who did not sign a leg's Terms can call for that leg's reports (11.2), however far upstream of the payment they stand. No report is read as carrying another leg's reports onward. Reading reports beyond one leg (a partner reading the end of the waterfall, a request from further upstream) is not these grammar rules': it is for cMIPs about data, focused on agreements, which the Terms of each leg so read name by fingerprint (rule 2). A verifier MUST NOT read the people of a leg as having agreed to be read beyond their leg unless Terms they signed say so (rule 5.1). What such a cMIP is, and which task it answers, is not said here (section 8.2). This provision is of Agreements only: it is no part of the report block, and no other layer of MOR is to have it (section 8.1).

**Lie exposed.** "It all went through", said by one side alone; "we passed it on", said where nobody on the other side can contradict it, or in a form that only readers of one leg's cMIP can read; "you never asked", said by a leg whose own signers did; "that was our fee", said of money that is still owed; "only the service can ask", said to the people of a work that signed the deal; and "you agreed to be watched", said from upstream to people whose signed Terms name no such thing. Each leg's own record is signed and may be complete in its own form; to a reader without that leg's cMIP it shows nothing, and rule 9 would then answer "unchecked" for everything beyond it. A call written in signed Terms shows who asked to hear back; a report in a form every reader reads shows what each side said; and the settlement both name shows where two sides agree and where they do not.

**Protects.** Those further along a sequence, and the payer, who did not sign the legs before them: they can read, before paying, which legs called for reports, and which agreed to be read further. The people of a work, whose deal is theirs as much as the service's. The people of every leg, who are seen from beyond their own leg only where they agreed to it in an agreement they signed. Every signer, whom no one outside their own act can oblige to report. Each leg, which keeps its free choice of cMIP: no leg has to adopt another's in order to be read. And whoever is still owed, whose money is not shown as someone's fee.

**Reasoning.** What must hold, in the words of Nobody, allegedly, is: "can I with a single click reward all the people that contributed to the service or good via MOR". A click that climbs is a result that depends on a sequence of agreements. When the details of paying were handed to cMIPs (rule 1, section 5), the MOR lead's doubt was that "paid", read wholly by each service's cMIP, would be unreadable wherever a reader lacks one leg's cMIP. Nobody, allegedly: "I actually thought about this a few days ago… yes, the whole chain of payment and also the chain of agreements that dictates does not require to always use the same cMIPs, the choice of cMIP stands on the leg, not the whole. So I think you are right, when the result depends on a chain of agreements, informing everyone of the whole where needed has its value." (He later said of his own words that "sequence is better" than "chain of agreements"; this text follows that.) So 11.1 puts the choice on the leg.

*Must every leg report?* On his "where needed": "When needed could be all the time that we did not check". To the proposal of letting a test that builds cMIPs on top of the grammar answer it: "Good idea, test by building on top, I like it". The test was built by the Dubsar Suite lead (`docs/click-sketch-report.md`, branch `claude/click-sketch`): three split services, a band member three legs from the click. It showed that one click can reach everyone, but only if every leg on the way reports; that a payer could not tell, before paying, whether it would; and that the report, as it was then written in this rule, could not be followed as its own words said.

*The report is not Agreements'.* Asked, after that test, what is truly useful in a leg report: "We need to be able to track it both directions, as exercise. The agreements need to point to the right places to receive the confirmations." To a field for it, proposed by the MOR lead: "« where my reports are found » feels too broad", and then: "Tasks may or may not call for a report. Therefore, a task can if needed specify where it expect its report from." Asked whether that is a rule for all of MOR: "Yes, I lean so too. It is elegant, and can be used wherever it is needed." Asked whether a report carries only what it answers, what it relied on and who signs: "Seems enough yes". Asked who can ask for a report: "Who signed the act that holds the task, no?" So the report is a block of the shared language, written down by the MOR lead as a proposal, and this rule is only its use: which task carries the call here (K8), who the doer and the receiver are for that task, and what that task adds to a report. He gave the same rule again in another window, on the question whether a leg that receives for others should be required to report: "Excellent question, turned into an MOR rule that extends to all tasks, a task may or may not request a report, if it does it states where it expect it."

*How the block answers the three flaws the test found.*
- **Where the next report is.** As first written, this rule followed each entry that named an agreement to a report made within that agreement. But a work paid through its deal is reported on within the deal, so the climb stopped at the first deal. The block's answer: every call says where its reports are expected, so a reader follows a leg to where its call says (11.4(a)), and never has to guess from the agreement a report names.
- **How one report is linked to the next.** As first written, each report had to name the report before it, and nothing told the next service which report its money came from: the settlement arrives first, the report later. The block's answer: halves are tied by an act both name. For a payment that act is the settlement, which both sides hold. Neither report needs to know the other (11.4(b)).
- **How a disagreement is seen.** As first written, a report named acts and no amounts, so two reports could not be seen to disagree on how much. The block's answer: everything beyond its three parts is the task's to define. K8 defines amounts as part of a leg report, so two reports that disagree are shown side by side (11.4(c)).

The same answer serves a fourth thing the test found: money held back and still owed looked exactly like a fee kept. K8's row asks for the two apart.

*No leg is made to report.* His words are "may or may not" and "if needed", and the block obliges nobody outside the act that holds the call. So this rule makes no leg call for reports and makes nobody answer. What it gives instead is sight: a call is in signed Terms, readable before paying, and silence is shown as silence. What that leaves uncovered is a stated cost (section 7).

*Who makes the call in a deal.* "Who signed the act that holds the task" was, in a deal, the service alone, for as long as an agreement named as a party never signed. He described the deal otherwise: "The service signed with work and pointed to the receiver." And: "So, the agreement with the service is signed by all, the report about the payment only comes from the receiver. It’s in that agreement that a request for surviliance can be cooked." So the work signs its deal, as its own Terms require (rule 1.2), and the call in it is made by the work's people as much as by the service (11.2). On the work's side, the report about a payment comes from its receiver, and from no other of its people.

*Every leg is the same exchange.* The click test found a work whose receiver was one of its owners: that owner held the other's money, and no report was asked of anyone. Of such a person he said: "So, he is reporting his leg, fulfilling his obligation." The MOR lead proposed that a call might be attached to a receiver. His answer described something simpler: "The service fulfilled its obligation, reported that it did so. The band receiver confirmed it via report (this report can be automatic on certain rails). Now, the band receiver needs to fulfill his obligation. So the same exchange of reports happen within the band." So 11.5 adds no new thing. The one who receives for others is the doer of the next obligation, within their own agreement; 11.2 lets that agreement carry the call, and the proposal of a call attached to a receiver was withdrawn by the MOR lead.

*One leg.* He then asked his own question: "Why would the service care? Obligation fulfilled on their end. A partner, that wants to verify that a human split was executed would want to see it." And: "The partner cannot trust the receiver, it can only trust the end of the waterfall." So: "the answer is it depends, and if it depends it needs to be built to accommodate both". He let the idea run as far as it would go ("Yes, upstream of payment can request, and anyone upstream of them should be able to do so too."), and said why: "Yes, it’s a huge stretch on purpose. To see where it snaps. I think the grammar to support it should be there, in agreements only. Never extended to the other layers. Or surveillance become cheap and omnipresent. People have to AGREE to be under surveillance, in an agreement they signed." Asked how far a confirmation travels: "One leg, that’s all it can do. For everything else’s it’s DATA cMIPs focused on agreements". So 11.6: in the grammar a report travels one leg. Both needs are met, each in its place: the service, whose obligation ends at its own leg, by the grammar; the partner who wants the end of the waterfall, by data cMIPs that the people concerned agreed to in an agreement they signed. What the grammar gives for the second is only what it already has: Terms name their cMIPs by fingerprint (rule 2), and nobody is bound by what they did not sign (rule 5). And it is written here, in Agreements, and nowhere else.

What a leg report says is in this text, in K8's row, and not in a cMIP, for the reason the shape was first kept: what must be read by those who lack a leg's cMIP cannot be defined by that cMIP (11.3).

### Rule 12. Forks

**Plain words.** Some acts come in a line, each pointing at the one before: a person's own acts in an agreement (rule 6), an agreement's Terms, each replacing the last (rule 4). Where two acts both claim to be the next step after the same one, and only one should be, the line has split: that is a fork. A fork is never chosen by anyone's software: a verifier shows both branches. A fork ends by a **join**: one act that names every branch and says which one continues, or merges them into one, signed by whoever could sign the step where the branches part. Because the problem can always arise, an agreement may decide in advance how it will be solved: it may give someone a power to sign the join, under a rule of its own choosing, so no single way is forced. Either way, what other people did relying on a branch that is dropped still stands, and is shown as disputed. Until a fork ends, it stays shown; for Terms, the Terms before the fork stay in force (rule 4.7). This is one rule for every act that can fork; there is no list of cases.

**Precisely.**
- 12.1 A **line** is a set of acts of which each names the one before it, so that only one act may follow each: one signer's acts within an agreement, by `previous` (rule 6.1); an agreement's Terms, by `replaces` (rule 4.8); and any other acts these grammar rules read as following one another. A **fork** is two acts that each count, under these grammar rules, as the next act after the same act of one line (rules 4.7, 6.2). That act is the **shared act**; each of the two, with every act that follows it in the line, is a **branch**.
- 12.2 While a fork stands, a verifier MUST show every branch it holds and answer **fork** where rules 4.7 and 6.2 say, and MUST NOT choose between the branches, by position or in any other way. A fork stands until 12.3 ends it.
- 12.3 **Join.** A join is an act of the same line that follows the shared act (its `previous`, or for Terms its `replaces`, names the shared act), and names in `joins` the first act of every branch. If it names one of them in `continues`, that branch continues and the line goes on from that branch. If it names none, the join merges the branches and the line goes on from the join. A join ends the fork only when it carries the signatures an act following the shared act in that line requires: for one signer's line, that signer's (rule 6); for Terms, those the shared Terms require for their replacement (rule 4.8). A join is not a branch. Every branch the join does not continue is **dropped**.
- 12.4 **A power to sign the join.** Terms MAY give, in advance, a power (rule 5) to sign the join of a fork: an entry in `powers` whose rule (task K4) says who may sign it, and which join it accepts. A join signed under such a power carries the signatures 12.3 asks for, and ends the fork as 12.3 says. For a fork of Terms, the power is read from the Terms both branches replace, which stay in force (rule 4.7); it is a power for replacement (rules 4.8, 5.2), which its rule may limit to joins. For a fork in one signer's line, it is a power to make that act for the signer (rule 5.1(b)). How the join is decided is the power's rule's: it may accept only the join it computes from the acts and the place, whoever signs it, or accept the join a named person signs (a judge, task K10), or anything else; these grammar rules force no single way. A verifier MUST report such a join with the power it was signed under and whoever it rests on (rules 5.1, 8).
- 12.5 **What others relied on stands.** An act signed by someone who signed none of a dropped branch's acts, and that rests on an act of that branch (it names it, in `terms`, `under`, `answers` or `relies_on`, or was made within or under Terms of that branch), keeps the effect these grammar rules gave it. A payment that names a version of that branch as the one it read (rule 13.2) is such an act. A verifier MUST show that act, and every answer resting on it, as **disputed**.
- 12.6 A fork that no join has ended stands: of Terms, as rule 4.7 says; in one signer's line, as rule 6.2 says.

**Lie exposed.** "This branch is the real one", said by whoever a branch suits, to whoever is shown only that branch; and "we settled it", said of a fork by people who could not have signed the step where it parted. Each branch is signed and looks valid alone; only the two together show the fork, and only the shared act shows who could end it.

**Protects.** Whoever relied on a branch without causing the fork: the payer, the newcomer, the other party who answered an act (12.5). Every party, who is not left stuck in a fork with no way out, because the way out (a join, signed by those who could sign the step, or under a power given in advance) is fixed before anyone needs it. And the reader, who is shown the fork and every branch instead of one picked for them.

**Reasoning.** Nobody, allegedly, asked: "Can we attempt  to solve incompatible forks (a fork that should not exist) for all sorts of acts that need it?" Asked whether one shape fits all acts (shown, never chosen; ended only by a join that names every branch and says which continues, or merges them; signed by whoever could sign the last act both branches share; what others relied on in a dropped branch standing, shown as disputed): "I think it does. Let’s focus on unresolved forks…" So rule 12 is one rule, written for a line of any acts, with no list of cases; rules 4.7 and 6.2 say only where the grammar meets a fork, and how a fork of Terms is read while it stands. 12.3 reads "whoever could sign the last act both branches share" as whoever could sign the next act after it in that line: the same signer for one signer's line, and for Terms those whose signatures rule 4.8 requires.

On forks that nobody joins, to the proposal that an agreement name in advance who or what settles them: "Resolver makes sense, the problem potentially exists, so deciding in advance how to solve it makes sense, and this way does not force a single way to do it." ("Resolver" was his word of the moment.) Deciding in advance needs no block of its own: the join is already there (12.3), and so is a power given in advance to make an act that binds the parties (rule 5). So 12.4 only says that the two meet: an agreement may give a power to sign the join, and that power's rule is where "how to solve it" lives, as a cMIP answering task K4 (section 5). No single way is forced, because the rule is whatever the agreement names.

### Rule 13. Now is what an act cites

**Plain words.** After any act, other acts pile up: payments, thanks, fillings, new versions, each made for its own reason and tied to its own people. No clock runs over them. But every act names other acts, and each naming is a knot: an act that names another certainly came after it. Where chains name each other, order emerges by itself. So an act's "now" is simply what it names. It is read against the versions and acts it cites, and a later version does not reach back to change what it did. That is why every act names what it relied on: a payment names the version of the work it read. An act is never read as having relied on something it did not name. Citing can show "after". It can never show "nothing later", or what time it was. For those two, every reader uses the place and the clock the agreement itself names. Only where the agreement names none does each reader accept the anchoring it is willing to accept, and every such answer says which anchoring it stands on, or is given as shown.

**Precisely.**
- 13.1 An act is read against the versions and acts it names, in any field that holds a fingerprint (`terms`, `under`, `replaces`, `previous`, `answers`, `joins`, `relies_on`, `settlement`). A report is read in the same way, against the call and the acts it names (rule 11.3). A version that replaces one an act names does not change what these grammar rules read that act as having done. Whether the act's signer is bound from then on is rules 4.3 and 5, unchanged.
- 13.2 An act that relied on a version or an act, of its own agreement or of another, names it: a Statement in `relies_on` where no other field names it, a Terms act in `relies_on` (rule 2). A verifier MUST NOT read an act as having relied on a version or act it does not name. A payment to a work names the version of the work agreement it read; how a settlement carries that name is for the layer that gives the settlement (section 8.1).
- 13.3 **Order from citing.** An act that names another, directly or through acts that name it, came after it. A verifier MAY give that order as **shown**, from the acts alone, with no place and no qualifier. Between two acts of which neither so names the other, no order is shown from the acts alone.
- 13.4 **Anchoring.** Whether anything is later than an act, and what time it was, are never shown by citing. For an agreement whose Terms in force name a place, every reader takes "nothing later" from that place; for one whose Terms in force name a clock, every reader takes the time from that clock (rule 7.4). A verifier MUST NOT give, per a reader's own anchoring, an answer that the place or the clock the agreement names gives. Only where the agreement names none does each reader accept the anchoring it chooses. A verifier MUST give each such answer **per anchoring**, with the anchoring named (**per place** or **per clock** where it is the one the Terms name), or **as shown**.

**Lie exposed.** "You paid the wrong one", said to a payer after the agreement changed behind them; "I was relying on the earlier version", said afterwards by someone whose act named nothing; "mine came first", said of two acts that nothing ties together; and "nothing came after", said on the strength of an anchoring the reader never accepted; and "by my clock it had not yet ended", said against the clock the agreement names. Signed text shows what an act names; without this rule a verifier could read the act against whatever version suited the one asking.

**Protects.** Whoever acts on a version they read and named: the payer above all, who did not choose when others would change the agreement and cannot know of a change not yet shown to them. And the reader, who is told what each answer about "latest" or about time stands on, instead of being handed someone else's clock.

**Reasoning.** If "now" were read when someone asks, whether a payer paid the right person could change answer after they paid. Asked whether every act should instead be read against the versions in force at its own position, Nobody, allegedly, answered with what really happens: "What happens after it got paid? A series of acts have been created for various reasons, tying to various id… chains crossing, knots forming, order emerging". Asked whether that means free order (each citation a knot that says this came after that; an act's now is what it cites; a place needed only for nothing later): "Yes, I think so. Then, anyone accept the anchoring they are willing to accept." So 13.1 and 13.3 take order from what the acts themselves carry, which any holder of copies can check (section 1), and 13.4 leaves the two things citing cannot give to an anchoring. That every act names what it relied on is written as a rule of reading (13.2: what is not named was not relied on), because no verifier could check a duty to name. Time is written into 13.4 because rule 8.2 already says a time inside an act is only its signer's claim, so that an anchoring is the only thing left to answer it. Which anchoring wins is rule 7.4: what an agreement names binds every reader of it; "anyone accept the anchoring they are willing to accept" is what holds where it names none.

### Rule 14. The baker

**Plain words.** A finished work is baked: its bytes are sealed in an envelope, and the envelope says who baked it. Anyone can copy those bytes and write a fresh agreement that claims to sell them. So the first thing to look at is the envelope: who baked it? An agreement for a baked work counts only if the baker is one of the parties it names. Or, where the baker has sold her baker's rights, if one of its parties holds them through the sequence of the work's own agreements, which starts with the baker. A stranger with no such sequence has nothing to sell. To sell the work as their own, a thief is at least forced to edit it and bake it again, and that is another envelope, with another baker.

**Precisely.**
- 14.1 Terms MAY name an `envelope`: the fingerprint of the envelope of a baked work those Terms are for (rule 2). What an envelope is, and who it says baked it, are read as the core gives them (section 8.1). The baker is reported as the envelope states it (rule 8).
- 14.2 Terms that name an envelope count as an agreement for that work only if:
  - (a) the baker is one of their parties, bound by them (rule 5.1); or
  - (b) one of their parties, bound by them, holds the baker's rights through a sequence of agreements for the same envelope: the first meets (a); each passes the baker's rights to a party of the next, as its own Terms and cMIPs say (tasks K4, K11, K12); and the last is these Terms. Each agreement of the sequence names the one before it (rule 13.2).
- 14.3 A verifier asked whether Terms count as an agreement for a baked work MUST read the envelope, the baker it states, and, under (b), every agreement of the sequence. Where the Terms name neither the baker among their parties nor a sequence, the answer is **invalid**: they never counted as an agreement for that work. Where they name the baker or a sequence and the acts held do not establish (a) or (b), the answer is **not shown**. Where the verifier lacks the envelope or an agreement of the sequence, it is **unchecked** (rule 9). A verifier MUST give that answer with every answer about that work that rests on those Terms (a receiver, a filling, a leg report).
- 14.4 A work edited and baked again has another envelope and another baker. These grammar rules read it as any baked work, and do not read it as the first.

**Lie exposed.** "This work is mine to sell", said by someone who copied its bytes and wrote an agreement of their own around them. That agreement is whole, signed and in force on its own terms; nothing in it shows that its signers had nothing to sell. Only the envelope says who baked the work, and only the sequence from the baker says who holds her rights now.

**Protects.** The baker, and whoever holds from her, who did not sign the stranger's agreement and may never see it. And the buyer, who can check, before paying, that the one who sells is the baker or holds from her.

**Reasoning.** Someone who copies a work's bytes and sells them in a fresh agreement of their own is seen by no other rule: that agreement is valid on its face. Nobody, allegedly: "That’s when we look at the envelope of the work. Who baked it? So, the thief should be at least forced to edit it and bake it again." Then: "So, the baker has to be one of the parties the agreement for that envelope states." Asked whether a buyer who bought the work outright counts through the transfers that start with the baker: "The baker, through the chain of agreements of the work has sold her bakers rights, no?" (His "chain of agreements" is what this text calls a sequence.) So rule 14 is those three sentences: the envelope is where a verifier looks (14.1), the baker must be a party (14.2(a)), or the sequence from the baker must reach one (14.2(b)). How rights pass from one agreement to the next is each one's own business, read through the tasks it names. "Envelope", "bake" and "baker" are the core's words; this text names them and does not define them. The rule covers the stranger's sale only. The same baker selling the same work twice, in two sequences, is still seen by neither (section 7, "Ownership is as wide as its place").

## 5. Tasks handed to cMIPs

A task is one question the grammar asks and does not answer (section 2). A cMIP answering a task accepts what the task says and produces what the task says; anyone may write one, and a cMIP may answer a task for one kind of thing only (one cMIP for land, another for shares). Where a task belongs to another layer's operation, it is named and not defined.

| K12 | Spot | Who fills this spot, by which proof, and on whose word; and does it still take new identities? | the role (its kind and `what`) as the version in force states it, what the rule in that version's `condition` says, the acts held, the answers of the place and the clock | each filling: the signer who fills it and the proof that earned it (the acts it rests on); or none; whether the spot holds one filling or many (rule 10.2); whether it still takes new identities, and why (rule 10.5); for kind future, also what defines the participant | whoever the cMIP names as confirming: for done, a witness or the counterparty; for received, the previous holder; for holding, as its cMIP says; for fits, those who vouch (K2); for future, as its cMIP says |
| K13 | Standing | What do one signer's acts show about them, to anyone who looks? | a signer, any acts and place answers the one asking holds | whatever measures the cMIP defines (proven forks, acknowledgments given and received, spots filled), computed from signed acts only, qualified per place or as shown | none: anyone can recompute; nobody keeps it |

**Why these, and why so few.** The research found that the same few operations answer the 49 questions, and that a "silent sixth operation", applying a stated rule to known inputs, needs no trust because anyone can redo it. So the tasks sort by whom they make you trust: nobody (K1, K4, K11, K13), a witness of the world (K2, K3, K5, and K12 for most kinds), a place (K6), a teller (K7), a hand (K8), a recipient (K9), a judge (K10). Each task is one question; many cMIPs may answer each one, pointed at different objects. Nobody, allegedly: "I think more tasks means less defining for each one. And clearer mapping for cMIPs." That holds inside each row too: a cMIP for share registers and a cMIP for votes both answer K4 without either defining the other.

**Why K13 is a task of its own.** Nobody, allegedly: "reputation tracking no platform owns or computes is important." K4 is one agreement's rule; reputation reads a signer's acts across many agreements. K13 says what such a reading may rest on (signed acts and place answers, nothing a platform alone holds), and that its answer is the same for anyone who holds the same acts. Which measures matter, and how they are weighed, is the cMIP's.

### 5.1 Five things built on the blocks, and the task each plugs into

Nobody, allegedly: "Just be mindful to not attempt to solve something the cMIPs will." The five things below are solutions people will build. The grammar keeps only the block under each; the rest is a cMIP's, answering the task named. None of this is a rule: it says where each thing plugs in.

| What is built | The block the grammar keeps | Its cMIP plugs into | What that cMIP says |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Paying through a sequence**: a service that takes a work's payments and divides them | a receiver that may be an agreement (rules 1.3, 1.4); an act that names the version it read (rule 13.2); a call for reports on the passing on, and the leg report that answers it, at every leg in the same way and for one leg only (rule 11; the block is the shared language's); who said it (rule 8) | **K8** (Control), the service being a hand; with K1 to read its deal and K4 to compute the division | what makes an agreement the work's deal with the service (and not the service itself, nor an offer it makes to everyone); what is read from the deal (where the work is paid on each way of paying, the fee and who bears it, what becomes of a late payment); what must agree for a work to count as paid; how a pay-out is matched against the shares and the version the payment named; where each owner is paid |

| **Closing, reopening, closing by itself**: an offer that stops taking new people, or takes them again | a spot read from the version in force (rules 10.2, 10.5); earned rights kept (rule 10.6) | **K12** (Spot) | what in the version in force means the spot takes no new identities (a mark in a new version, a date, a count of buyers), and what opens it again |

| **Settling a fork in advance** (what earlier drafts called an arbiter or resolver) | the join (rule 12.3); a power given in advance (rules 5, 12.4) | **K4** (Rule), as the rule of the power to sign the join; with K10 where that rule names a judge | who may sign the join, and which join is accepted |

Escrow is built in the same way as the first row: a service's cMIP (K8), under its deal; which agreement its deal is with, and how a contest reaches it, are that cMIP's. What protects an agent in a delegation (rule 5.5) is likewise a cMIP the parties choose.

The blocks under it are ones the grammar already has: Terms name their cMIPs by fingerprint (rule 2), and nobody is bound by what they did not sign (rule 5). Which task such a cMIP answers is not said, and no task is added for it (section 8.2).

## 6. Open parameters

These are values the grammar rules depend on and this draft does not fix. The numbers are kept from earlier drafts so that the records still point at them; P3, P10, P13 and P14 were answered there and are not listed (`history.md`).

- **P1. Default for `in_force`.** Rule 4.1 sets it to "every party named as a signer". Whether a different default is wanted is open.
- **P2. Depth of powers through powers.** Rule 5 lets Terms named in `under` themselves be bound through other Terms (an agent of an agent; a group within a group). Whether a verifier must follow any depth, or a limit applies, is open.
- **P4. One place or several.** Rule 7 names one place. Whether Terms may name several places with a K4 rule over their answers (plural witnesses of order) is open.
- **P5. What a verifier holds.** Rules 6 and 9 depend on which acts the verifier holds. Whether a verifier answers from a set it is given, or must also ask the place for more, is open. K13's answers depend on it most.
- **P6. Pools.** In plain words: some ways of paying cannot pay each share at the moment it is earned. Money must first gather in one pot, and be divided later. Nobody, allegedly: "Sadly some rails will need pools… this headache won’t be easy." This draft does not solve it. What is open, all of it:
  - who holds a pool, and on whose word it is said to hold what it holds (rule 8.3: only the holder's or controller's word);
  - when a pool is divided, and by which rule;
  - how a claim on a pool is counted when each false claim takes from the real claimants (`late-roles.md`: "each fake claim takes from real claimants, and the payer has little reason to resist");
  - what "tied to a settlement" (rule 8.5) means for a count paid out of a pool, where one settlement covers many counts;
  - what a leg report (rule 11.3) names as received where what it passes on comes out of a pool;
  - what happens to a pool's share that no one has yet claimed (the owed but unfindable).

  **What the click test showed** (`docs/click-sketch-report.md`, branch `claude/click-sketch`, its 3e; a test built on draft 10): one report for a batch of payments works, since it names them all as received. But "how much of *my* click reached the band" is then arithmetic the reader does pro rata, which nobody states; the sketch marks it "not stated". Left open. Whether one report may answer several calls at once is open in the block as well (section 8.1).
- **P7. What happens to a filling.** A spot may be filled many times, each filling tied to its proof (rule 10.2). Still open: whether a filled spot can pass to another, and whether a later claimant can displace an earlier one.
- **P8. A receiver that is an agreement.** Rule 1.4 reads an agreement named as receiver as the next leg, and reads nothing more of it. Open: to what depth one receiver may point onward to another, and what a loop answers. **What the click test showed** (its 3f): when a song names as a party the film that stands on it, money goes round until fees and rounding eat it. It ended in the sketch only because the services round down and charge fees, and the reader needed a rule of its own to detect the return. The grammar says nothing of what a loop answers. Left open.
- **P9. What a fork removes.** Rule 6.2 says a verifier "MUST answer **fork** for that signer". Whether that is for the two acts only or for every later answer involving the signer, and whether a signer can make their own assent uncountable by forking on purpose, is open. It is answered for Terms only (rule 4.7): while a fork of Terms stands, the Terms both branches replace stay in force.
- **P11. Dates.** A place gives positions, not dates. Terms may name a `clock`, and every reader uses it for that agreement (rules 7.4, 13.4). Still open: what a clock is and who gives it (P16).
- **P12. One act for many.** `for` names one person. Whether one decision or judgment binding many is one act or one per person is open.
- **P15. Rights outside fillings.** Rule 10.6 is about fillings. Whether a party named as a signer, or an agreement named as a party, also keeps exactly what its version offered against a later version made under a power is not said (rules 4.8, 5.2).
- **P16. The clock.** Not said: what a clock is and which layer gives it; whether the place may also be the clock; and, where two versions of one agreement name different clocks, which one a `condition` rule (rule 10.7) reads. This text says "the Terms in force", as rule 7.2 does for the place.
- **P17. An agreement that acts, or signs.** *Settled in draft 12,* from the exchange "Who signs the deal, and who can call", and no further than it goes: an agreement named as a party signs, where it signs, as its own Terms require (a small work by all its owners, a collective by a vote or a named delegate, as its rule says); the rule that it never assents or signs is dropped (rules 1.2, 4.4); a deal between a work and a service is signed by the work and by the service, and a call in it is made by both (rule 11.2). *Still not said:*
  - by which acts of its own people an agreement is shown to have signed (an assent by each owner, one act carrying the result of a vote, a delegate's signature), and where those acts sit: in the agreement signed, or in its own;
  - how an act names an agreement in `for`, which holds a person, and by which act an agreement delegates (rule 5.5);
  - what an agreement's signing binds its own people to. Rule 5.1 binds a person by their own signature, or through Terms they signed that give the power; rule 1.2 says only that an agreement that has not signed is not bound;
  - whether the default of rule 4.1 should count a party named as an agreement. It does not, as before: a work that only points at the works it stands on needs none of them to sign ("no collective involved"). So Terms that need an agreement's signing must say so in their own `in_force` rule, and nothing else in the grammar tells a party that must sign from one that is only pointed at;
  - what is read where an agreement's own Terms state no rule for signing as a party. This text says every one of its signers (rule 1.2), from his "signed by all";
  - whether such a party can itself make a counter signature (rule 4.10).
- **P18. The baker's sequence.** Rule 14 says the sequence starts with the baker and that each agreement passes the baker's rights as its own Terms say. Not said: what in an agreement counts as passing them (a version, a Statement of some kind, a filling of kind received); whether the agreements of that sequence call for reports (rule 11); what is read for a work with several bakers; and how Terms that sell a baked work's bytes without naming its envelope are read (rule 14 reads only Terms that name one).
- **P19. Where "invalid" stops and "not shown" starts.** "Invalid" is meant as one word across the layers. This text draws the line so: invalid, where the acts held show that an act cannot count; not shown, where they only fail to show that it counts. Whether that is the line the other layers draw is not known to this text (section 8.1).
- **P20. What a leg report carries.** K8's row says what a leg report carries beyond what every report carries: what was received, what was passed on and to whom, what was kept, and, apart from it, what is still owed, each with its amount (rule 11.3). Not said: how an amount is written so that every reader reads it alike (an amount on a way of paying is the Money layer's to give; a right is something else); whether money shown as still owed is later shown as paid, and by what; and whether one report may cover many payments (P6). **A person who receives for others**, what the click test showed (its 3g): a work whose receiver is one of its owners passes no report, and none is asked. That owner then holds the others' money, unseen: in the sketch a library's first owner received for both owners, and the second was shown in no run. *Answered in draft 12* (rules 11.2, 11.5): every leg is the same exchange. Whoever paid that owner reports that they did; the owner confirms by a report of their own; then the owner has the next obligation, within the work's own agreement, and reports there as its doer, each other owner confirming. No call is attached to a receiver. *Still not said:* how a receiver's report is "automatic" on some ways of paying (who signs it, and whether a settlement can itself stand as the confirmation: the Money layer's, section 8.1); and whether a work whose owner receives for the others holds the Control task with no cMIP named for it, as rule 11.2 reads it (section 8.2).
- **P21. One condition.** New in draft 10. A version names one rule in `condition` (rule 10.7). Not said: whether a version may name several (one for each spot, or one for rights and one for the offer), or whether the one rule's parameters must carry all of it.

## 7. Stated costs

Cases that do not fit the grammar. None is closed by a rule.

- **Minds.** Whether a party was able (#3) or free (#5) cannot be shown by any act. K3 can make it more likely and record what was done; nothing assigns its failure. Only Align is assigned (rule 3).
- **Conceal (#39).** Terms can be kept private and their fingerprint lodged in a place; nothing can show the words were not told to someone, and telling cannot be undone.
- **Enforce beyond what was posted (#45).** Words can order a release; something must obey. Against value not under Control, force lies outside the protocol. A proven liar (rule 6) loses only what they posted and their standing with those who see the proof (K13). Against a stranger who vanishes, the proof is worth nothing.
- **Deeds lean on witnesses.** The more an agreement is about services and acts in the world instead of value under Control, the more it rests on K5, whose statements prove only that the witness said them.
- **Spots lean on their confirmers.** Each kind of spot has its own recorded fraud (`late-roles.md`): doing is faked "by simulating the act or stealing credit"; receiving "by forging the chain"; holding "by forging the object or issuing more than exists"; being "by false or multiplied identities". Rule 10 fixes the test in advance and rule 8.4 reports who confirmed; neither makes the confirmer honest.
- **Future spots are the authors' word.** Whether a future spot was used only for participants no one could anticipate cannot be checked (rule 10.4). Authors who use one for an ordinary participant, to escape the four kinds' clearer tests, are not stopped by the grammar.
- **Counted attention leans on whoever counts, and the grammar names no one.** Being seen is counted by whoever a spot's cMIP names (section 5.1). The grammar itself no longer requires an agreed witness: an agreement whose cMIP names none, or names the payer, has counts that rest on whoever states them, and inflated attention (`late-roles.md`: "Roles counted by attention suffered fabrication of the event itself") is then exposed only as far as that cMIP goes. A rule that counts only what led to a settlement leaves out someone truly seen whom no payment yet stands behind; a rule that rewards pure attention rests every count on the one who counts.
- **"Now" is what was read, and no more.** Under rule 13, a payer who names the version they read has paid as that version says, even if the agreement had already changed without their being able to know. That protects the payer; it also means the owners under a later version can see money go by an earlier one. Whether the version named was still the last is answered only per an anchoring, or as shown (rules 4.6, 13.4), and the climb up a sequence gets less certain at each leg that names no place.
- **A work that points nowhere cannot be paid.** Under rule 1, what is owed to an agreement whose version names no receiver has nowhere to go, and is not shown.
- **A deal is as complete as its words.** Who bears a service's fee, and what becomes of a payment that arrives after the work has named another receiver, are whatever the deal states, read by its cMIP. A deal that says nothing on them leaves them unsaid; the grammar supplies no answer.
- **A service is a hand.** Nothing in the grammar makes a service divide as the shares say, or pay at all. The grammar reads only its signed word of what it received, passed on, kept and still owes (rule 11), and only where its deal called for that word. Two reports that state different amounts for the same settlement are shown side by side, neither chosen, and anyone may count it (K13); getting the money is another matter ("Enforce beyond what was posted"). An owner who receives for the others is a hand in the same way (rule 11.2): nothing makes them pass it on.
- **A sequence is readable only as far as its legs call for reports, and are answered.** Rule 11 makes no leg call for reports, and makes nobody answer a call. From a leg whose call is unanswered, or which carries none, nothing ties what lies beyond it to the click: the people beyond are not shown, though they never chose that leg (in the click test, one silent service hid a producer and a whole band). And a call binds only the signers of the Terms that carry it: a work cannot make the works it stands on, or their services, report. What a payer has is the sight of it: before paying, which legs called for reports, read from their signed Terms; afterwards, which calls were answered. And a report is its signer's word: it makes the whole readable, not true.
- **One leg, and no further.** By the grammar each report goes to its own leg (rule 11.6). A payer, or a partner, who wants to know whether a split among people at the far end was carried out reads it only where every leg on the way agreed, in an agreement they signed, to a data cMIP that carries it. Where they did not, the far end is not shown to that reader, whatever was reported there; and the one who received is the only one who says it was passed on. That is the price of not making surveillance cheap. The other side of it: where the people of a leg did sign such a cMIP, they are read as far as they signed. The grammar reads their signature, not whether they were free to refuse it ("Minds").
- **Paid, and the author gone.** An open offer whose only author goes absent, with nothing that delivers by itself: whoever paid and got nothing can contest, and the contest stays on the record for anyone to read (K13). The record cannot give the money back, since nobody is left to sign. A buyer who wants that pays through an escrow service: a hand, under its deal, for a fee. Without one, the loss is the buyer's. Nothing in the grammar prevents an author from vanishing with what was paid; that is dealt with, if at all, in how services are built. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "MOR only offers ways to ring the alarm", and "MOR just tracks receipts".
- **Order without an anchoring is partial.** Citing shows only "after" (rule 13.3). Two acts that never cite each other have no order from the acts alone, and nothing shows "nothing later". Where an agreement names no place and no clock, readers who accept different anchorings can get different answers; each answer says which anchoring it stands on. Where it names them, every reader uses them (rule 7.4), and the answers are as good as that place and that clock.
- **No longer in force is as sure as its condition.** A condition met by someone's statement rests on whoever made the statement (rule 8); one met at a time rests on the clock the agreement names (rule 7.4), or, where it names none, on the anchoring the reader accepts (rule 13.4): then two readers with different anchorings can differ on whether a filling's rights are still in force. Beyond that the grammar defines nothing (rule 10.7): a version that says nothing more leaves it unsaid. And going on afterwards takes a new version, signed as the Terms require (rule 4.8), which those who must sign it may refuse.
- **Reputation is as wide as the acts one holds.** K13 answers only from what the one asking holds, per place or as shown. Two people holding different acts compute different standing for the same signer, and honest forks (below) count against honest people.
- **Without a place, no scarcity and no freshness.** The same thing can be given twice in two branches, the latest act can be withheld, silence cannot be counted. Rule 7.3 makes this visible; it does not cure it.
- **Ownership is as wide as its place.** Two parties who accept different places cannot settle who holds a thing between them. The same thing sold in two separate agreements is seen by neither agreement's rules. A stranger's sale of a baked work is seen: Terms for an envelope count only with the baker, or a sequence from the baker (rule 14). The baker, or a holder, who sells the same work twice in two sequences is still seen by neither.
- **The baker is as the envelope says.** Rule 14 rests on the envelope and on whoever it names as baker, as the core gives them. It does not stop anyone from editing a work and baking it again under their own name: that is another envelope, and whether it owes anything to the first is not read by this grammar. It reads only Terms that name an envelope (P18).
- **Closed world hurts the one who did not lodge.** Under rule 7.2, a party's own act that never reached the place does not count, even if it is genuine.
- **Honest forks.** A signer who loses track of their last act (a lost device, a restored backup) and signs again produces a fork that looks exactly like a lie. Honest drift and lying look the same from outside. The signer can end it with a join (rule 12.3), if they still hold their key; what others relied on in the branch the join drops stands, shown as disputed (rule 12.5).
- **A fork of Terms holds the agreement where it was.** Under rule 4.7, while two replacements of the same Terms both stand, the Terms before them stay in force: neither branch's change takes effect, including a new receiver (rule 1.4). If the agreement gave no power to sign the join (rule 12.4), and those whose signatures a join needs will not sign one, it stays there; the grammar shows it and picks no branch.
- **A join does not undo.** What others relied on in a dropped branch stands, shown as disputed (rule 12.5). A join settles which branch goes on; it cannot take back what a stranger did relying on the other.
- **Withdrawing before in force.** Between a first assent and the last, the first signer holds an open option against themselves. They can close it: an assent may be cancelled, by a counter signature, until the Terms are in force (rule 4.10). It is still a race, between the counter signature and the last assent, that only a place, or an `in_force` rule with a deadline read from a place or a clock, settles.
- **An offer that stops taking new people.** The same race, between whatever stops the offer (a new version coming into force, a date, the hundredth buyer) and a stranger's filling the spot. Who came in time is per the place and the clock the agreement names, and without them only as shown (rule 10.5).
- **Secret votes need a teller.** K7 requires trusting whoever counts.
- **Identity ends in acquaintance.** Who vouches for the voucher (K2) ends in someone knowing someone; the grammar cannot express that. Spots of kind fits rest on it.
- **The record holds no value.** Every act about value is a claim or an instruction. The gap between "the record says 100" and "there are 60" must be checked continuously (K5, K8), not once.
- **Those who cannot sign.** A contributor who has no key (the long dead, the unknowing) can be named in words but is no party, and nothing can arrive for them.
- **No default way out.** By rule 5, an agreement that names no power to change itself changes only by everyone's assent again. And since no agreement ends (rule 4.9), none can be left by ending it: the last version keeps binding until those its Terms require sign a new one. If a party is gone and no successor was named in advance (#37, #38), it is stuck. (One exception: a delegation ends by its delegator alone, by default, rule 5.5; not where its signed conditions say otherwise.) The way out is designed in advance or not at all; this draft chooses that over any default that would let someone be bound without their signature.
- **Ordering and chaining show patterns.** Rules 6 and 7 make the shape of an agreement (who acted, how often, in what order) visible to whoever sees the acts or the place, even when the words are concealed. K13 reads exactly those patterns.
- **An agent has what was signed, and no more.** By default a delegator ends a delegation at any time, and the grammar gives the agent no notice and no pay (rule 5.5). An agent who wants either has it written, as signed conditions or as a cMIP beside the delegation. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "we’re not Law." The same holds for the delegator: the grammar limits no signed condition, so a delegator who signs that only the agent may end the delegation has given up ending it alone, for as long as the agent chooses, and if the agent is gone, for good. The grammar shows the condition; it stops no one from signing it.

## 8. What is still open

Nothing in this section is decided. Sections 6 and 7 are part of what is open too: the parameters this text does not fix, and the costs it does not close.

### 8.1 What this text needs from the other layers, and does not have

- The core's act format: whether it already gives fingerprints, a per-signer line of acts (which would make rule 6 redundant), acts signed by several people at once, and field names.
- What the Order layer's place can answer: in particular whether it can sign "nothing later than this", and how a place is identified without a server address.
- What the Sign layer does when a key is replaced or lost, since that decides whether rule 6 sees a fork.
- How cMIPs are identified in practice, so that "named by fingerprint" in rule 2 can be met.
- **The report, a block of the shared language** (proposed in `docs/lead/proposals/report-block-1.md`, branch `claude/lead-records-2`: the MOR lead's proposal, not a decision, not yet written into any spec, its wording his to change). This text uses it (rule 11, task K8) and does not define it. What it needs from it: that a task may carry a call for a report, or not; that the call says from whom and where; that only the signers of the act holding the task can make it; that a report carries the call it answers, the acts it relied on and its signer; that two halves are tied by an act both name; and that "unanswered" and "no report called for" are shown as such. And what the block has not yet said: how a call is written, where it sits in the act that holds the task, and how a report names it by fingerprint; how "where each report is expected" is written without a server address; how a report is written as an act, and whether it is a Statement of this text; whether a call is per task, or per act holding several tasks; whether one report can answer several calls at once (P6); whether "unanswered" ever ends; and its words ("call", "report", "matched", "mismatched", "one side only", "unanswered", "no report called for"), which are the MOR lead's, as technical wording. The proposal was written before the exchange "A person who receives for others". Its sentence that "the trail can be walked both ways: down from a payer, up from a contributor" does not yet say that a report travels one leg; whether the block itself says so, for every layer, is the MOR lead's to write.
- **Seeing beyond one leg: in Agreements only** (rule 11.6). In plain words: the way for people to agree to be read further than one leg exists in this text and must exist nowhere else. What this text needs from the shared language and from every other layer: that none of them carries a report further than its leg; that none lets anyone who did not sign an act call for reports on it; and that none takes up, for its own tasks, what rule 11.6 leaves to agreements. In the words of Nobody, allegedly: "Never extended to the other layers. Or surveillance become cheap and omnipresent." And what this text does not have: what a data cMIP focused on agreements is, which task it answers, and how Terms say that their signers agreed to one.
- What a settlement is, as the layer that holds value gives it, so that the `settlement` field (rule 8.5) names the right thing; and what a payment is there: how it names the version of the work agreement it read (rule 13.2; that is Money's to say, and this text only reads the name).
- How that layer writes an amount, so that the amounts in a leg report (task K8) can be read by everyone alike (P20).
- The research's own open question: how much a proven liar must stand to lose, and where it is kept, for an evidence-only record to be enough. Rule 6 depends on the answer for its force; K13 is where that loss would be read.
- Whether the verifier's answers (shown, not shown, fork, no longer in force, invalid, unchecked) are already named in another layer, so that the words match; in particular whether "invalid" there means an act that never counted (P19), and whether another layer already uses "in force" of a right.
- What the core calls an envelope, baking and a baker (rule 14): whether an envelope has a fingerprint of its own, how it states who baked it, and whether a work may have several bakers.
- What a clock is, which layer gives it, and how it is identified without a server address (rule 7.4).
- How an agreement named as a party is shown to have signed: which acts of its own people show it, and whether the core has an act signed by several people at once (rule 1.2; P17).
- What a receiver's "automatic" report is on a way of paying, as the layer that holds value gives it: who signs it, and whether a settlement can itself be the receiver's confirmation (rule 11.5; P20).

### 8.2 For Nobody, allegedly: left open by draft 12's changes

Draft 12 is draft 11 with three changes: an agreement as a party signs (rules 1.2, 4.4); every leg is the same exchange (rules 11.2, 11.5); a report travels one leg (rule 11.6). They come from the exchanges "The same rule, in the Dubsar Suite lead's window", "A person who receives for others" and "Who signs the deal, and who can call". Each item below is where a change touches something those exchanges do not cover. Each is left as draft 11 has it, or says the least that lets the text be read, and says so. Where the ledger's arrived-at lines say more than the exchanges is listed in `history.md` (Part E).

**Three items of draft 11's list that these changes touch: what is answered, and what stays open.**

1. **Who makes the call in a deal** (draft 11's item 4). *Answered:* the work signs its deal, as its own Terms require, and so does the service. The call in a deal is made by both, and is the work's people's as much as the service's (rules 1.2, 11.2). *Stays open:* by which acts the work's signing is shown, and what it binds its people to (P17). And which deal he meant. His words are "The service signed with work". This text writes it for a deal as it defines one: between a service and the work it serves. In the click test's case, the service that pays a band member has its deal with another work, further up, and the band signed nothing with it. Whether the band then signs something with that service too is not said.
2. **The tie between two legs** (draft 11's item 6). *Answered, for the usual case:* at every leg the one who passed on reports and the one who received confirms. Those two reports answer the same call, the leg's own, so the tie is between two halves of one call, as the proposal has it. One leg is linked to the next by the one who received: the receiver of one leg is the doer of the next (rule 11.5). *Stays open:* rule 11.4(b) still ties any two reports that name the same act, whichever call each answers, as draft 11 has it. That now matters only to a reader who holds the reports of two legs, and by rule 11.6 such a reader exists only where both legs agreed. Whether reading across legs should leave the grammar altogether, for data cMIPs, is not said.
3. **Is sight enough?** (draft 11's item 2). *What changes:* before paying, a reader still sees which legs called for reports, and now also which legs agreed to be read further (rule 11.4(e)). After paying, the grammar shows a reader the reports of the legs they signed, and no others: one leg. Whether the click reached the people at the far end is no longer the grammar's to show. It is for data cMIPs, where every leg on the way agreed. His words: "the answer is it depends, and if it depends it needs to be built to accommodate both". *Stays open:* whether that is enough for the wish ("can I with a single click reward all the people") is his. It is written as a cost (section 7, "One leg, and no further").

**An agreement as a party signs.**

4. **Pointed at, or signing.** In plain words: a film that names a song as a party does not need the song's owners to sign; a deal does need the work to sign. The exchange covers the deal only. So the default of rule 4.1 still does not count a party named as an agreement, and Terms that need its signing say so in their own `in_force` rule. Whether the grammar should itself tell the two apart is not said (P17).
5. **How the signing is shown, and what it binds.** Not said: which acts show that a work signed (each owner's assent, one act for a vote, a delegate's signature), and what its signing binds each of its people to (P17). "A vote or a named delegate" is the MOR lead's wording, from the brief.
6. **"the report about the payment only comes from the receiver".** Read as: of the work's people, only its receiver reports on a payment (rule 11.2). It is not read as taking away the service's own report, which he describes in the other exchange ("The service fulfilled its obligation, reported that it did so").
7. **Bound.** Draft 11 said an agreement named as a party is never bound. Draft 12 says that only of one that has not signed. For one that has, nothing is said (P17).

**Every leg is the same exchange.**

8. **May, or must.** His words: "So the same exchange of reports happen within the band." That describes what happens. Draft 12 keeps the earlier "may or may not": a band's own Terms may carry a call, and nothing makes them. If he meant that such a leg must report, rule 11 changes.
9. **Where a work with no service holds the task.** Draft 11 read "the act that holds the task" as Terms that name a cMIP answering K8. A band whose member receives for all may name none. Rule 11.2 now also reads a work as holding the task where its receiver is one of its own parties and others have a part. That is Claude's reading, the least that lets "within the band" be read.
10. **"Automatic on certain rails".** Not said: who signs an automatic report, and whether a settlement can itself be the confirmation. It is the Money layer's (section 8.1; P20). This text says "ways of paying" where he says "rails".
11. **A receiver confirms under a call it did not sign.** A band member paid by a service further up did not sign that service's deal. Their confirmation answers its call all the same, as any receiver's does in draft 11: nobody is made to answer. Left as draft 11 has it.

**One leg.**

12. **What "one leg" covers.** His words: "One leg, that’s all it can do." Rule 11.6 reads three things into it: a call expects reports only on the task of its own Terms; nobody outside a leg can call for its reports; no report carries another leg's reports onward. That is Claude's reading, the least that lets it be checked.
13. **"Not shown", for a reader beyond the leg.** A reader who does not hold a leg's reports cannot honestly say its call is "unanswered". Rule 11.4(d) says "not shown" there. Claude's reading; the shown words are the block's (section 8.1).
14. **Data cMIPs.** "DATA cMIPs focused on agreements" are his words. Not said: what one is, which task of section 5 it answers, and how Terms say their signers agreed to one. No task is added.
15. **Who has agreed.** "People have to AGREE to be under surveillance, in an agreement they signed." For a small work, every owner signed. For a collective that signs by a vote or a delegate, a member who was outvoted signed only the collective's own agreement. Whether that is agreeing is not said. Rule 11.6 says "Terms they signed" and points to rule 5.1.
16. **"the grammar to support it should be there, in agreements only".** He said this before "One leg". Draft 12 reads the two together: the grammar gives one leg; beyond it, the only support in the grammar is what it already has (a cMIP named in signed Terms). If he meant a block of its own for seeing further, it is not written.
17. **"upstream of payment can request, and anyone upstream of them should be able to do so too".** Said inside what he called "a huge stretch on purpose". Draft 12 does not write it as a rule. It is read as something a data cMIP may give, where those seen agreed.
18. **Agreements only, and a block for all of MOR.** The report is the shared language's, so other layers may use one-leg reports. What is Agreements' alone is the way to agree to be read further. The proposal for the block does not yet say "one leg" (section 8.1).

**The rest.**

19. **The Dubsar Suite lead's list.** The first exchange records that the link by a shared act, amounts, and kept-or-owed are the MOR lead's proposals, "not yet his". Draft 11 wrote them because its brief asked. Draft 12 leaves them, and they are still to check.
20. **Words.** "Leg", "obligation", "waterfall", "surveillance", "upstream" and "data cMIPs" are his. "Confirms" is used for a receiver's report, from his "confirmed it via report". "Doer" is the proposal's. "The same exchange" is his.
21. **Words that may be typos.** Quoted as the ledger has them: "surviliance", "else’s", "happen", "become".
22. **Nothing was tested.** No test was run against draft 12, or against draft 11.
