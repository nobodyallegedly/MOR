# Roles filled after an agreement is made

Research report for Nobody, allegedly. 12 November 2026. Reports and compares; decides nothing.

## In plain words

Agreements have always named people they could not yet identify: "whoever brings a buyer", "whoever holds this ticket", "my heirs", "whoever finds the bug". The law and trade practice call these affiliates, bearers, successors, claimants and beneficiaries.

Across media, software, goods and services, I found about eighteen such roles. They link to a person in only four ways: by **doing** something, by **receiving** from the previous holder, by **holding** an object, or by **being** someone who fits a description.

Each way has its own characteristic fraud. Doing is faked by simulating the act or stealing credit for someone else's. Receiving is faked by forging the chain. Holding is faked by forging the object or issuing more than exists. Being is faked by false or multiplied identities.

The largest recorded frauds hit roles paid by counted attention (views, streams, clicks) and roles paid from a fixed pool that anyone could claim from.

## 1. The roles

| # | Role | What it is | Legacy practice | What it is owed |
|---|------|-----------|-----------------|-----------------|
| 1 | Referrer | Brought the buyer | Affiliate programmes, finder's fees, refer-a-friend | Commission on the sale |
| 2 | Curator, reposter, broadcaster | Put the work before an audience | Radio, playlists, reposts | Usually nothing from the creator; the broadcaster normally pays |
| 3 | Reseller, distributor | Bought to sell on | Wholesale, retail, resale markets | Margin; may owe the maker a resale share |
| 4 | Bearer, holder | Holds the ticket, note or receipt | Bearer bonds, warehouse receipts, tickets | Whatever the paper promises |
| 5 | Performer | Performs on a recording | Featured and session musicians | A statutory share of performance royalties |
| 6 | Cover artist | Re-records an existing song | Mechanical licences | Owes the songwriter; owns the new recording |
| 7 | Sampler, remixer | Builds on an existing recording | Sample clearance | Owes the original; shares in the new work |
| 8 | Sublicensee | Licensed by a licensee | Publishing, software, franchising | Use rights; owes fees upstream |
| 9 | Assignee | Was handed someone's right | Factoring, sale of debts and royalties | The original right, no more |
| 10 | Heir, successor | Takes over on death or dissolution | Wills, probate, company succession | The departed party's position |
| 11 | Bounty claimant | First to complete a posted task | Rewards, prizes, bug bounties | The posted sum |
| 12 | Open-offer acceptor | Did what a public offer asked | Advertised rewards, guarantees, warranties | What the offer promised |
| 13 | Third-party beneficiary | Named by description, not a signer | Insurance, trusts, "pay my creditor" | The benefit, sometimes a right to sue |
| 14 | Carrier, host | Delivers or stores | Shipping, hosting, ad delivery | A fee per delivery or per view |
| 15 | Later contributor | Joins work already under way | Open source, session work, crews | Credit, a share, or nothing |
| 16 | Class member | Fits a described group | Class settlements, compensation funds | A share of a fixed fund |
| 17 | Informer | Reports a fraud | Whistleblower statutes | A percentage of what is recovered |
| 18 | Toolmaker, intermediary | Provided the means used | Payment processors, app stores, patent pools | A fee per use |

Roles 3, 6, 7 and 8 both owe and are owed. That is the usual case for anyone in the middle of a chain.

Two concrete examples of how a split is fixed before the people are known:

- US law sets the performers' share of digital performance royalties at 45% for featured artists and 5% for a fund covering non-featured ones. Where several featured artists appear and nobody has filed a different split, the collecting body divides their share equally by head.
- An informer under the US False Claims Act gets 15 to 25% of the recovery if the government joins the case and 25 to 30% if it does not, falling to at most 10% when the claim rests mainly on public information.

## 2. The act that links a role to a person

| Role | Linking act | What proves it | Who confirms, other than the claimant |
|------|-------------|----------------|----------------------------------------|
| Referrer | Buyer arrives through their link, then buys | Tracking record | The seller, who is also the payer; sometimes a measurement firm |
| Curator, broadcaster | Plays or reposts | Play logs | The broadcaster's own logs; audit by a collecting body |
| Reseller | Buys, then sells | Invoices, registry | Each buyer and seller in the chain |
| Bearer | Presents the object | Possession | The issuer, on inspection |
| Performer | Took part in the session | Credits, session sheets | Label, union, collecting body |
| Cover artist, sampler | Releases the new work | The release itself | Rights owner or a collective |
| Sublicensee, assignee | Signs with the previous holder | The chain of documents | Previous holder; notice to the debtor |
| Heir | The party dies | Will, family records | A court or notary |
| Bounty claimant | Submits the result | The result itself | The sponsor, or a platform between them |
| Open-offer acceptor | Performs the condition | Evidence of performance | Often nobody until a dispute |
| Beneficiary | The triggering event occurs | Identity documents | Insurer or trustee |
| Carrier, host | Delivers | Delivery records, receipts | The recipient; a measurement firm |
| Class member | Files a claim | Proof of purchase, or a sworn statement | A claims administrator |
| Informer | Files first | The filing date and content | A court |

The open offer shows the weak point in its oldest form. English judges accepted in 1892 that an offer can go out to the whole world, binding the offeror only to those who come forward having performed the condition. The losing company had objected that it could not verify whether buyers had used the product as directed. That objection was overruled, not answered.

## 3. How each has been faked, and what held

**Referrer: stealing credit.** Two top eBay affiliates distributed free web widgets that planted eBay's tracking cookie on visitors' browsers, so later purchases looked like their referrals. One collected more than $28 million in commissions. A US court gave him five months in prison in 2014 after a guilty plea to wire fraud. The sales were real; only the cause was faked. What held: the payer's own investigation and criminal law, years later.

**Referrer: the tell.** In Uber's dispute with its ad agency, unpaid installs rose by nearly the same number that paid installs fell once spending stopped, which suggests intermediaries had been claiming customers who would have come anyway.

**Carrier and host: inflated delivery.** US prosecutors charged eight people in 2018 over the 3ve operation, which faked billions of ad views and cost advertisers over $29 million. A sister scheme rented more than 1,900 data-centre machines to load ads on over 5,000 counterfeit domains while imitating human behaviour. What held: outside security firms spotting the pattern, then seizure of the infrastructure.

**Performer and rights owner: inflated plays.** A North Carolina musician uploaded hundreds of thousands of AI-made tracks and had bot accounts play them, spreading plays thinly so no single song stood out. He pleaded guilty and agreed to forfeit about $8.09 million. Because streaming royalties come out of a shared pool, the money came from real rights holders. The collective that distributes US mechanical royalties flagged the catalogue as implausibly large.

**Curator and broadcaster: payment in the wrong direction.** New York's attorney general found in 2005 that Sony BMG had routed money to radio stations through middlemen and documented some payments with invented contest winners. The label paid $10 million; the underlying rule allows paid airplay only when it is disclosed on air. The remedy was disclosure, not prohibition.

**Bearer: more paper than goods.** The 1963 salad oil fraud cost lenders more than $180 million after tanks were rigged to look full and receipts were eventually forged outright; the American Express unit that had certified the receipts bore the liability and settled for $60 million. Fifty-one firms held receipts, and none could see how many existed in total. What held: the certifier paid.

**Heir: false identity.** The Tichborne claimant, who presented himself as a baronet's lost heir, lost in court, was convicted of perjury and served 14 years. The missing man's mother had accepted him. He could not speak French, the heir's childhood language. What held: knowledge only the real person would have, tested by adversaries.

**Bounty claimant: worthless claims at volume.** The curl project suspended its bug bounty from 1 February 2026 over machine-written reports. Its lead developer said the 20 reports handled so far that year contained no real vulnerability. The programme had paid over $100,000 since 2019. Nothing held; the offer was withdrawn.

**Class member: invented claimants.** Administrators judged 49% of claims fraudulent in one $7.8 million settlement and 47% in another. In a $45.5 million tobacco settlement, roughly 80% of 14 million claims were likely fake. Claim counts have in several cases exceeded the estimated size of the class.

**Reseller: unenforced resale share.** OpenSea stopped enforcing creator fees on new collections after 31 August 2023. A rival that did not enforce them had overtaken it in volume. No fraud was involved: when the role-holder could choose a venue that ignored the term, the term lapsed.

## 4. Roles no agreement could anticipate

- **The unknowing source.** Cells were cut from Henrietta Lacks in 1951 without her knowledge; her estate sued a supplier for unjust enrichment in 2021 and settled on confidential terms. A second company settled in 2026. The claim came seventy years later, by court, with no rule written in advance.
- **The rescuer.** Maritime law grants a reward, scaled to the value saved, to a volunteer who rescues a ship or cargo without any prior duty to do so. The law writes the open offer on everyone's behalf.
- **The owed but unfindable.** Streaming services handed the US mechanical collective $424,384,787 in royalties they had been unable to match to an owner. After a holding period the collective may share out what remains by publishers' market share. The role is known and the debt is certain; only the person is missing.
- **The contributor recognised afterwards.** Optimism's retroactive funding pays for work already done, on the premise that past usefulness is easier to agree on than future usefulness. A panel names the role-holders after the fact.
- **The early user.** Token airdrops reward past users by rule, and attract manufactured users. One project flagged 803,093 addresses as likely duplicates. Another excluded 516,960 of 1.29 million. The first offered self-reporters 15% of their allocation and paid others to report duplicates.

In every case here, someone with judgment decides afterwards: a court, a collective or a panel.

## 5. Patterns

**Four ways a role meets its person**

| Kind | Roles | Proof | Typical fraud |
|------|-------|-------|---------------|
| Doing | Referrer, curator, performer, carrier, bounty claimant, acceptor, informer, rescuer | Evidence of an event | Simulating the act; claiming another's act |
| Receiving | Reseller, sublicensee, assignee, heir | A chain back to the previous holder | Forged chain; the same right given twice |
| Holding | Bearer | Possession | Forged object; more issued than exists |
| Being | Beneficiary, class member, early user | A fact about the person | False identity; one person as many |

Holding is receiving with the chain collapsed into an object. So the eighteen roles reduce to three kinds, or four if bearers are kept apart.

**Three further distinctions cut across the kinds**

- **One seat or many.** A single-seat role (the heir, the first finder) invites races and impostors. A many-seat role (every referrer, every play) invites inflation.
- **Fixed pool or per-event payment.** Where claimants share a fixed pool (streaming royalties, class funds, airdrops), each fake claim takes from real claimants, and the payer has little reason to resist.
- **Owed, owing, or both.** Middle roles carry both, and the upstream debt is the part that lapses when unenforced.

**Who confirms**

Five confirmers recur: the payer itself, the counterparty to the act, a registry or collective, an outside measurer, and a panel or court afterwards. The payer as sole confirmer appears in the affiliate and bounty cases. The counterparty's own acknowledgment is the cheapest and appears least.

**What was faked, compared**

- Roles counted by attention suffered fabrication of the event itself.
- Roles counted by completed sale suffered theft of attribution; the event was real.
- Roles with no cost to claim suffered volume.
- Roles confirmed by a certifier shifted the loss to the certifier.

In the terms of the earlier task list, doing is a Perform that needs a witness, receiving is Transfer, holding is Title, and being is Identify.
