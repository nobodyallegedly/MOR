# The unwritten layer

*Companion document, version 2, 11 October 2026. Not part of the core. The redraft of "Who can earn on MOR" (version 6, which stays), under the title of the third film. Version 1, drafted earlier the same day as nine pairs of economies, was withdrawn before merge after a brainstorm with Nobody, allegedly: the companions are a light read, not a proof, and the people come first. Version 2 tells the same participants as stories, in two parts: today's economy made honest, then one participant leaving for a branch where the same roles chose the other way. A draft for the author's own passes. The drafting notes at the end are not part of the text.*

## Six written layers, and one unwritten

MOR has six written layers, six short rulebooks that will be frozen. There is a seventh that nobody writes down, because it is not a protocol layer at all. It is the economies people build on the six: who is paid, for what, by whom, and who gets to see it. In the author's words, "it's there with possibilities to explore various economic models but it is not a protocol layer".

This document proves nothing. The core document and "Don't ban greed, make it legible" do that. Here are only people, each with more than one way to be paid, choosing in the open. The core fixes three things and then steps aside: every reward comes from a payment someone chose to make, never from attention; every cut sits in a signed split that reaches everyone it pays; and leaving costs nothing but what one chose.

## Part one: the same economy, made honest

Nothing in this part is new. It is the film business as it runs today, moved onto MOR with its economic model untouched. Nothing is kinder. It is only legible.

### The film

Mara makes documentaries. Her new one took two years, a crew of four, and a production company that is itself an identity on MOR, a small firm with its own keys. The work claim binds the finished film to its creators. The film's agreement names the owners: the production company holds most of it, Mara a third. Teo, who edited it, Lina, who wrote the music, and Sam, who recorded the sound, are not owners. They were paid fees, as crews are. Each fee was a deal of its own, signed by both sides, and each is finished.

The production company's lawyer insisted on one thing the crew found exotic: the production deal names a keeper, a notary firm that records every act around the deal as it arrives, without reading any of it. "If this film sells, people will argue about who signed what, and when," the lawyer said. "The keeper is cheaper than the argument." The keeper charges a yearly fee for every deal that names it. Its product is its record and its reputation; it earns nothing from the film.

### The distributor

A distributor offers Mara and the production company a three-year deal: it takes seventy per cent of what the film earns until its advance is recouped, forty after. The terms are hard, and they are also exactly what they say. Mara's client shows her the plain text before she signs, and the recoupment waterfall that will divide every payment, which a split rule the distributor chose computes. The distributor holds a stake in its publication of the film, never in the film. The film's agreement is a party of the distributor's publication agreement, consenting by its own rule, which is every owner.

Money will pass outside in: a sale through the distributor's publication is split there first, the distributor's share and the film's; the film's share goes to the one pointer the film's agreement designates; then the film's own split divides it among the production company and Mara. The distributor pays that pointer and divides nothing beyond it. Its responsibility ends where the receipt shows the share arrived.

The distributor does its accounting through an accounting house, a split service named in its agreement by a grant. The house receives every payment, publishes a split for each one that sums exactly, pays each party out with a receipt, and takes its fee as a cut in the split, consented by the distributor who bears it. The distributor named a second house as a backup, tried only when the first fails for a payment. Mara sees every split that pays her, whole: the distributor's cut, the house's fee, the rail fees deducted from her payout, capped by the plan. She knew the seventy per cent going in. Now she sees it arrive, payment by payment.

### The pool

The distributor places the film in a streaming service. Viewers pay the service a monthly subscription; the service is a pool, which splits its income by minutes watched. Nothing about that changes on MOR except who can see it. The pool's split plan names the metric, minutes watched; the identity that measures it, a measurement house that is neither the pool's split service nor anyone the pool pays; and the payouts the metric produced. The pool splits by its stated rules, then the distributor's publication agreement, then the film's.

Mara's film is watched a great deal in its first month. Her share of each subscription is small, because the pool's rules say so, and the rules were published before she signed. A viewer, Jo, pays the subscription from a wallet that keeps Jo's own claim of the payment. If the pool ever signed a receipt for less than Jo paid, Jo's claim would show it. Nobody expects the pool to. The point is that nobody has to.

### The relays

The film's locked files sit on relays, which store and serve them without being able to read them. The distributor pays a relay operator by the gigabyte stored and served, under the operator's standing offer: a price, a service level, a promise to keep serving, which the distributor confirms or contests month by month. The relay operator's income is a fee. It competes on price and reliability with every other relay operator, and nothing it carries is its business.

Mara's identity lives on two homes run by two different operators, who hold her identity chain and sign receipts for every key change. She pays each a small yearly fee, for hosting over time, which is a promise and is sold as one: she confirms the year was served, or contests it. She declared the majority rule, so no single home decides for her.

### The clients

Jo watches in a free app. The app's developers are paid by nobody on MOR; they are paid by the streaming service, under a contract that is not on MOR at all. Mara's own client, the one she signs her deals in, is free too, maintained by two developers who sell support to production companies. The rules the clients run, the recoupment split rule, the payment rule, the media formats, were written by people who are paid nothing for them in this economy. Anyone can copy a specification, so nobody can charge for one. The split names the rules the payment ran under, and the plan carries no fee for any of them. That is a choice, visible as such.

### The critic

Ida writes about documentaries. She reposts Mara's film to her readers, and several of them subscribe to the pool that week. The repost is a reference. It pays nobody by itself, and the pool's rules have no share for it. Ida is paid by her newspaper, which pays her as it always has. Her taste moved money, and the record shows it moved nothing to her.

### Promises

The film premieres in a cinema. The cinema sells seats under a vow, an act naming in words something MOR cannot hash: a seat in row twelve, on the evening of the fourteenth. Each buyer signs onto it by paying, and after the evening each sale is confirmed or contested by that buyer and nobody else. The projector failed for ten minutes; three buyers contested; the cinema's refund terms, published with the vow, say what they are owed. The cinema earns from seats, as it always did, and its record of promises kept is now its own to show.

Rui, who wrote the subtitles, sells hours on a gig platform. Each job is a vow, confirmed by the client who signed onto it. The platform takes its cut in the split, which Rui sees. On MOR the platform cannot take a cut Rui did not sign, cannot hold Rui's history, and cannot keep Rui: Rui's identity, record and agreements leave with Rui. The platform still takes thirty per cent. Everyone can see that it does.

### The services around the money

A buyer in another country pays for the film on a rail the distributor does not accept. A conversion service carries the money across, paid under its own standing offer, with a receipt for every hop, so the route reads as one chain and a broken hop names itself.

The production company anchors its agreements to Bitcoin, through a service that batches many people's hashes into one anchor and charges per hash. The hash is in the batch or it is not; nobody's word is involved. Anchoring also decides who bears a theft: the company named a clock and anchors, so a thief who steals its everyday key cannot collect its older debts after the company locks the key out.

When a milestone dispute arose over the edit, the deal already named who would decide: an arbitrator, a retired editor both sides trust, given the keys to the cuts in question and paid to decide. A verification agency the distributor hired verified the archive footage and holds a signed position on the film, income without ownership, paid per sale. A reputation service, which reads signed contests and disclosed records and sells its judgements to buyers of film rights, has nothing on anyone in this story yet.

### What Mara sees

Three years later, Mara's client shows her the film's whole income, payment by payment, from both ends: every settlement, every split that paid her, every fee and who received it. She sees that the distributor's seventy per cent was seventy per cent; that the pool's minutes paid her what minutes pay; that the accounting house's fee and the rail fees came off exactly as the plan said; that Ida's reposts, which she can count, paid Ida nothing and her little; that the relay, the client developers and the people who wrote the rules were paid nothing from her film; and that the production company, which put up the money, took its share as agreed. Nothing is hidden. Nothing is unfair, in the sense that she signed all of it.

She decides it is not the economy she wants for the next film. The distribution deal ends as its terms say, at three years. She withdraws the film from the pool; past deals stand. Her identity, her stake in the film, every receipt and every agreement come with her. Leaving costs her nothing she did not choose, which is to say, a distributor's advance.

## Part two: the branch

MOR expects branches. Above the frozen core, people adopt different rules and different economies, and a branch made for one domain, speaking the same core, is simply where a community has chosen the other way. Mara moves to one made by and for independent film. Every kind of participant from part one is here. Each chose differently.

### The collective

For the next film, Mara founds a collective with Teo, Lina and Sam. The collective is one identity with its own keys, held by a threshold of its members, under a founding agreement all four signed. Nobody is paid a fee. Each holds a stake in the collective, and so a share of everything it earns, written in millionths, paid to whoever holds the stake at the time. The agreement gives Lina the money lane, so she chooses the rails and the split rule; Teo the publications lane. A manager they hire acts through a grant, within limits, and can be revoked.

They name a keeper, the same notary firm as before. Serious business is serious business in any branch, and the keeper does not care which economy a deal belongs to. They name an arbitrator for disputes over the cut, and an abandonment authority in case one of them stops answering, so no one's silence can freeze the rest.

When the film is half made, Teo needs money. He sells a quarter of his stake to Noor, an investor who buys into independent films. Noor inherits exactly that position; the others' shares do not move. Noor is now paid when the film is, and has no voice in the collective. Partnerships are stakes, and stakes travel.

### The relay that curates

Ola runs a video relay in the branch. It charges no fee. Instead, Ola watches the films offered to it, chooses the ones it will carry, and is written into each film's publication agreement with a small stake, the film's owners consenting. Ola's income is a share of every sale of every film the relay carries, nothing when nothing sells. The curation is the service: a film on Ola's relay has been chosen, and viewers in the branch know what that means. Across town, a text relay still charges by the gigabyte, because nobody takes a stake in a message. Same role, two economies, and each film's split shows which one it is in.

Ola's relay also signs a delivery record for every file it serves. The record is worth nothing on its own. It counts only when the viewer's wallet acknowledges it in the viewer's own claim of the payment, and then it earns the relay the role share the owners' plan offers for the relay that served the sale. A relay that claims deliveries it never made has no viewer's signature to show.

### The client that pays its builders

The collective publishes through a film client built by three developers. The client proposes a split plan to every owner who publishes through it: an app share for the sales the client brings, and small shares for the maintainers of the split rule, the payment rule and the video format the client runs. The owners sign the plan, or they do not; the client proposes, the owners decide, and the shares come out of the owners' split. The collective signs. Their reasons are the ones the client's developers wrote down: the people who maintain the rules that carry the money should be paid from it; a client whose builders are paid will still exist next year; and a split that pays its builders says something about who made it.

The developers of the video format earn from that plan without ever having touched the film. The rail that carried each payment earns a share too, evidenced by the receipt itself. A module developer who wanted a feature the client lacked offered a bounty, a share of future splits for whoever built it, and someone did. None of this is forced. In part one the same rules carried the same money and were paid nothing. Here the owners chose otherwise, and the split shows it.

### The repost as marketing

Ida is in the branch too. She reposts the new film, and this time the owners' plan has a role share for the referrer who led to a sale. When one of her readers buys, the reader's wallet names Ida's repost as the one it followed, in the reader's own claim, and the split pays Ida her share. No split service can name a referrer of its own; only the buyer's signature counts. Ida earns from the sales her taste drives, and the record shows exactly which. A chain of reposts, Ida's reposted by someone else, still pays only the one the buyer names. How several referrers might share one sale is not settled. [OPEN: F184]

An indexer in the branch, which helps viewers find films across relays, earns the same way: a referral share when a viewer's wallet names it, and a fee from the collectives that want to be found.

### Selling direct

The collective sells the film under its own standing offer: a price, a key delivered on payment, the deal complete when the key is delivered. There is no distributor. Half the branch's collectives name a split service that pays out at once; Lina chose payer-side splitting instead, where each buyer's wallet pays every owner directly, Ola's relay included, with no service between them. It costs the buyer several payments and shows the owners' pointers, and it means nobody holds the collective's money even for an hour.

Three publishers in the branch carry the film at once, each under its own publication agreement: a festival's catalogue, a teachers' library, a critics' collective. One takes a cut, one takes a stake in its publication, one takes nothing and sells its own commentary alongside. Each earns only from the sales made through its own publication, and Mara compares them by settlements, not promises.

A pool exists in the branch too, run as a cooperative by viewers who pay a monthly sum. It splits by its own stated rules: equally among the films its members watched that month, however long. Minutes are not the only metric, and a metric is not the only rule.

### Promises

The collective sells a live conversation with the crew after the film, under a vow: an hour on the evening of the twentieth, by stream. Buyers sign onto it by paying. The stream's key, delivered on the evening, sits in each sale as evidence; the buyer still confirms or contests, because the key proves access, not the hour. One evening the stream dropped after forty minutes. The key worked; the promise was not kept; the vow's terms say what each buyer is owed. The collective's record of promises kept is now part of what the film client shows beside its name.

Rui sells subtitles in the branch too, this time without the platform: a standing offer of hours, each job a vow, confirmed by the client. Rui's record of two hundred confirmed jobs came along from part one, because it was always Rui's.

### The services that remain

Services are services in any branch. A conversion service still carries money across rails for a fee. The anchoring service still sells proofs by the hash. A key-release service, which the collective hires when sales grow past what Teo can hand out by hand, delivers keys on its behalf through a grant and is paid per release. The arbitrator is paid to decide. A verification agency that verified the film's archive footage holds a position on it, and the collective offered it a stake instead, which it declined; it prefers income without ownership, so that its judgement is not its own investment.

The reputation service that had nothing on anyone in part one now has plenty: Ola's relay's record of films chosen, the critics' collective's splits, Rui's confirmed jobs, the cinema's three contests, and one distributor in part one whose splits a buyer's claims contradicted twice. It sells its judgements to anyone choosing whom to deal with. Its raw material is what the other participants signed.

### The guards

With real money arriving, the collective's keys matter more. It keeps a vault for large payments, whose entries change only with its offline key, and points its everyday flow at a custodial service that enforces a daily limit the core cannot. Mara moves her own identity to three homes, one of which refuses any key change without proof from a device she registered; the home told her plainly what losing that device would cost her. An auditor cosigns her homes' logs. She buys insurance against theft of her keys, from a service that reads the same records everyone else can. Each of these is a market, and each is a choice she did not have to make.

### The viewer

Jo followed Mara to the branch, and finds that the viewer earns nothing and decides a great deal. Jo's wallet names the repost Jo followed, acknowledges the relay that served the file, confirms the hour of conversation or contests it, and keeps Jo's own claim of every payment, which is equal evidence against anyone who under-reports it. Jo's wallet carries Jo's choices into every payment, and the client's developers chose to build it that way. A viewer who stays wholly anonymous, committing no key at all, can reward no relay and no critic. Jo chose a name.

## Two branches, one core

The distributor from part one can still buy the branch's film; Mara's old film still pays her under its old terms; Rui's record, Ida's identity, the keeper's notary book are the same in both. The two branches speak the same core, so an identity is the same identity in each, a text can be read in each, and an agreement made in one can be cloned into the other. What differs is what people chose: fees or stakes, builders paid or not, reposts as marketing or as nothing, a pool by minutes or a pool by heads, a service that holds the money or a wallet that pays everyone at once. Both are legible. Anyone can leave either.

## What nobody can earn from

- **Harvested data.** Everything is locked by default; relays carry sealed boxes, and keepers record who signed what, never the content.
- **Hidden attention deals.** The core has no built-in reward for views or clicks; a pool that splits by minutes names the metric, its measurer and the payouts.
- **Lock-in.** Identities, stakes, purchases and history are portable; nobody keeps users by holding their data hostage.
- **Hidden fees.** Only an agreement's own entry makes a fee owed; every cut is in the signed split, seen by whoever bears it before they sign; rail fees on payouts are capped by the plan.
- **Money sent where the owners never signed.** A split rule chooses among the addresses the owners signed, never invents one.
- **Promises no buyer can contest.** Each sale of a vow is pending, confirmed or contested by the buyer who signed onto it.
- **Silent income.** A receiver who signs no receipt, or a smaller one, is exposed by the payer's claim. Hiding income requires the payer's silence or collusion; the core promises exactly that much, and nothing more.
- **Debts that vanish.** A payout without a receipt, a refund unclaimed, a share nobody could deliver: each stays an open obligation, visible, until a valid claim appears.

## The invitation

Every participant in both parts is open to anyone, and most can start small: one relay, one home, one keeper, one curated list, one split service, one vow kept. "When I look at MOR I imagine a diversity of ethos and wish to see them compete. It's a building ground" (Nobody, allegedly). The branches are made by those who build clients, relays, services and rules, legible to anyone who wishes to look; the rest of us choose among them, and leave when we like, taking everything that is ours. The core is small and will be frozen. The unwritten layer is yours.
