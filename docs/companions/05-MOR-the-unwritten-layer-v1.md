## Six written layers, and one unwritten

MOR has six written layers: Identity, Envelopes, Text, Money, Agreements and Development, six short rulebooks, frozen once they are proven. There is a seventh that nobody writes down, because it is not a protocol layer at all: the economies people build on the six, who is paid, for what, by whom, and who gets to see it. In the author's words, "it is not actually a layer… it's there with possibilities to explore various economic models but it is not a protocol layer".

The core fixes only what no choice may break. Every reward comes from a settlement, a payment someone chose to make; the core itself rewards no view and no click. Every cut sits in a signed split that reaches everyone it pays, and nothing is taken from a payment without a signature. Leaving costs nothing but what one chose: identities, stakes, purchases and history are portable; a member leaves a collective alone and keeps their stake; a service is dropped by revoking its grant; a deal ends as its terms say, or by a new version every party signs.

Everything else is a choice, and the same core carries opposite choices with equal ease. Below are such choices, each a pair of economies that both run on MOR. Neither side is recommended. Where the core sets a limit on one side, it is said.

## The choices

### Rewarding the builders, or not

Specifications are public goods: anyone may copy one, and a copy without a fee would appear within a day. So no specification can force a fee. A module may state its fee, as information; only a fee entry in the owners' own agreement makes a fee owed, and a split that leaves an owed fee out is a wrong split, visible to everyone it pays.

One economy pays the builders: owners accept the fee terms of the rules their money runs through; the rail module that carried a payment takes a share, evidenced by the receipt itself; a client proposes a plan with an app share for the sales it brings and shares for the maintainers of the rules behind it, the owners signing the plan, whatever the client proposed, the shares coming out of their split; bounties promise a share of future splits for what you build; software pays its dependencies, each level paying its immediate parents, as the software case study shows.

The other pays the builders nothing: no fee entry, no builders' share, and the split shows it. Both run. In either, developers earn from what cannot be copied: running services, being the trusted and maintained original, bounties paid before building, shares clients choose to pass on. The core's limit on the first: a plain tip carries no module fees beyond the rail's own.

### Transparency to all, or information kept segmented

Everything on MOR is locked by default; public means the key travels with the act, and going public later is one act, publishing the key. The same agreements can run in the open or in compartments.

One economy offers transparency to all: a collective that publishes its founding agreement, its splits and its incoming flows; a label whose terms every band can read before signing and whose settlements anyone can compare; a newsroom that discloses a negotiation's record by publishing its keys. Reputation services live here, turning signed contests, disclosed records, payers' claims against silent receivers and sales made outside a claiming agreement into judgements people pay for.

The other keeps information segmented: a receiver whose incoming flows only its counterparties see, the default; a label that shows each band its own deal and nothing of the others; keepers, the notaries an agreement names, which record who signed what, never the content; a publication whose two agreements stay apart, what happens inside the work's agreement being the owners' business. The network is segmented in a second sense too: each agreement pays the next at the pointer it designates, and its responsibility ends there, so that "the point of failure or cheating is easily found" (Nobody, allegedly).

The core's limits on the segmented side: whoever bears a cut sees it before signing; every split reaches everyone it pays, whole, naming each fee and who received it; a collective keeps no secret from its own members, and anyone can see that it acts, never what; the payer's side of every payment is the payer's to publish. Segmentation keeps outsiders out, never a participant from what they pay or are paid from.

### Partnerships, or fees

A partnership is a stake: a share of a work, a publication or a collective, written in millionths that sum exactly, paid on every payment that reaches the agreement, to whoever holds it at the time. Stakes can be sold, so investors buy into works and catalogues, and a creator sells part of a stake to fund the next project. A position is income without ownership. A fee is a cut in the split, consented by whoever bears it, ending when the service ends.

Session musicians, editors, translators and producers holding stakes instead of one-off fees, a publisher holding a stake in its publication, developers paid in shares of what their software earns: one economy. A split service's cut, a key-release service paid per release, a publisher paid a flat cut, an arbitrator paid to decide: the other. Many mix the two; both are legible in the split.

The core's limits: a stake never shrinks without its holder's signature, member of the collective or not, and a departed holder is paid under the same terms as every other; a fee is owed only through the agreement's own entry, and a split that pays it unevenly, or leaves it out, breaks the plan. One honest limit on partnerships: the core ties a sale to the agreement of whoever sells, not to the work's stakes. A co-owner who sells alone under their own plan breaks the agreement in the open, not a core rule: clients show the sale as outside the claiming agreement, the other owners contest it, a keeper has the claim on record, and reputation follows.

### A publisher's cut, or selling direct

A lone seller's standing offer is itself the agreement between seller and buyer: price, words, deadline, refund terms. Owners sell through their own publication, and tips reach them from any wallet. That is selling direct.

A publication can also be a publisher's, a bundle of client, relays and marketing. Then it carries two agreements: the publication's, which sets the terms of use and divides the publication's income, a publisher's share among them; and the work's, one party of it, consenting by its own rule and dividing whatever reaches it. The work's agreement designates one pointer at which it accepts money, the same whoever pays; the publisher pays there, divides nothing, and its responsibility ends once the share arrives, the receipt the proof. A publisher holds a stake in the publication, never in the work. Several publishers can carry one work, each earning from the sales made through its own publication, so creators compare them by real settlements.

The same shape runs any chain: an agency paying a production company paying its crew; an album whose agreement has the songs' agreements as parties; a course of lessons by different teachers. Any agreement may be a party of another, consenting by its own rule and named by its founding line, so no later version escapes what it signed. A compilation made with its parts' consent is such a layer; made without it, it is a derivative, its own work, paying its sources by its own agreement, its published split showing whether it honours them.

The core's limits on the publisher's side: the layer above never divides the next layer's share; an agreement cannot be paid through a layer until it designates one pointer; no agreement is a party of itself; chains are bounded in depth.

### A pooled subscription, or paying per work

Paying per work is the standing offer: the buyer pays, the deal is complete when the key is delivered, and the purchase names the claim it pays under, so a wallet that reads only money can pay but cannot buy a claimed work.

A pooled subscription is a layer: the subscriber pays the pool, a settlement; the pool splits by its stated rules, then each publication's agreement, then each work's. The keys a subscription delivers are access to bytes, proven by data alone. How the pool divides is its choice, and it may follow plays, views or any metric a module reports, as streaming does today, named for what it is: the metric, the identity that measured it and the resulting payouts are in the split, and the measurer is none of the identities paid, nor the split service.

Attention can divide a payment; it cannot create one, since the core has no built-in reward for views or clicks. Curators, playlist makers and critics are paid under either model, from the pool's rules or from the sales their taste demonstrably drives.

### A collective, or a lone owner with a split service

A sole creator is simply their own split service: payments reach their pointer, nothing to divide. Several owners have two shapes to choose from.

A collective is one identity with its own keys, held under a founding agreement every member signed: by one holder, by a threshold, or by a custodian. Its work can be divided into areas, with grants for managers who act within limits. A member can always leave alone, giving up their voice and keeping their stake; removal, where the rules allow it, takes the voice, never the stake. Where members split into sides that will not agree, the collective forks: each side founds its successor, the works pass in the shares the fork names, and departed holders keep theirs in every successor. Labels, bands, newsrooms and developer groups are collectives.

A deal with a split service is lighter: the owners stay separate identities, each granting the service a key to receive on its behalf and each able to revoke its own, so leaving the service is a revocation, never a renegotiation. The service divides each payment exactly as the agreement says, pays everyone out with a receipt for every payout, and takes its fee as a cut in the split. Owners may name backup services, tried in order, payment by payment, the first tried again for the next payment, and every payer, a buyer or a layer above, follows the same order. Money goes only to an address the owners signed; where every attempt fails, it stays where it is, an open obligation of whoever holds it. Owners who want no dependence on a service's honesty choose payer-side splitting, the buyer's wallet paying each owner directly, at the cost of several payments and the owners' pointers shown to the buyer.

The core's limits: nobody joins a collective without signing; who decides changes only under a rule every party agreed, every party unless the founders agreed otherwise; the terms that can move a member's stake or voice, or decide a dispute, change only with every member's signature.

### Rewarding the pipe, or not

Between a work and its buyer runs a pipe: the relay that served the file, the client the buyer read it in, the repost that led to the sale. "The water has to reward everyone along the pipe if wished to" (Nobody, allegedly). The core offers the grammar and favours neither answer.

One economy pays the pipe. Helpers known in advance, a host, a mastering service, the formats and rules used, a community fund, take a named share in the plan, no evidence needed. Helpers known only at the moment of payment take role shares: the reposter who led to the sale, the relay that served the file, a service the payer chose. Their evidence is signed by someone other than the split service and the payee, and counts only when the payer's own claim vouches for it: the buyer's wallet says whom it followed and acknowledges the relay's delivery record, and a split service cannot name a referrer of its own. Curators, indexers and relays earn here, from the sales their work demonstrably drives. Whether a client carries its user's choices into the payment is the client developer's choice, "pay the pipe if you wish to", legible to anyone who looks at what the client does with a payment.

The other pays the poster alone. A performer's hat holds money given to the performer, who may share it afterwards. A tip needs only money and a payee pointer and carries no module fees; a repost pays nobody by itself; a lone poster with no agreement has no split plan, and a tip rewards nobody but them.

A cost, stated: the payer's client writes the evidence for payment-time roles, so a dishonest client, or a payer and a helper together, can acknowledge help that never happened, which hurts the owners and the helpers, never the payer; the owners' only bound is the size of the shares they offer, and legible is not the same as deterred. A payer who stays wholly anonymous can reward no relay and no service. How several referrers share one sale is still open. [OPEN: F184]

### Proven offers, or promised ones

Offers are of two classes, and the class follows what is sold.

A proven offer sells a delivery that leaves a proof any verifier can check from data alone: a key that decrypts to the promised hash, a hash in an anchoring batch, a rail swap's payment proof, a computation re-run to the same result, a bounty whose answer checks itself. Nobody confirms it; the proof is the confirmation. Key-release, conversion and anchoring services sell proven offers: the hash is in the batch or it is not.

A promised offer sells a vow: an act naming in words something MOR cannot hash, or cannot hash yet. A good, a seat, a stream, an hour of work, hosting over time, a service's own word on counts or quality. Every sale of a physical good or a service names a vow, unless its delivery is proven, and each sale is pending, confirmed or contested, by the buyer who signed onto it and nobody else. A vow holds promises, bundled as the seller chooses, in public: a season, a series, a single match. A contest names the sale that failed, so one bad match marks one buyer's sale, not the season's; what is owed back when part of a bundle fails is the bundle's terms, the core fixing no partial refund. A proof can sit inside a promised sale as evidence without replacing the buyer's word: a stream cut short is a key that worked and a promise not kept. A vow names what is promised to the buyer, never the event: a seat and a stream of the same match are two vows, under two agreements.

Gig work, live events, crafts, hosting, verification agencies selling their judgement as a signed position on a work, and the arbitrators the parties name and pay to decide, run on promises and the record of promises kept; the proven side needs nobody's trust.

The core's limits: a vow nobody signed onto carries no state; a service evidenced only by its own word is not paid. A cost, stated: a holder who resells a claimed piece under a fresh vow of their own escapes the marking of a sale outside the claiming agreement, since the core cannot tell that two vows describe one object. "We cannot police this" (Nobody, allegedly); provenance rules, registries and reputation can, and the fresh vow, signed by the reseller, is easy to find.

### Guarding yourself, or paying guards

An identity is a hash; its keys are only the pens that sign for it. Everything that protects it is the owner's choice, and the core states the consequences rather than choosing for everyone.

Self-reliance is one economy: an identity hosting its own home is trusted on its own signatures, and a thief holding its offline key wins at once; a single home; no vault; a stolen offline key like a stolen bitcoin key. It costs nothing and asks everything of the owner's care with keys.

Paid guards are the other: several homes run by different operators, under a rule the owner declares; homes that refuse a rotation without proof from the owner's registered device, and say what it costs if the device is lost, since such a home can refuse the owner as well as a thief; auditors cosigning each other's logs, so a stolen key cannot forge a receipt unnoticed; a vault whose entries change only with the offline key, with custodial flow services holding the everyday pointer and enforcing a rate the core cannot; services holding incoming money briefly before releasing it, alerts, insurance against theft; and anchoring, which decides who bears a theft: an owner names a clock and anchors, or bears the loss, which never falls on a payer who followed the published pointer. Each is a market.

The core's limits: every protection an owner chooses is removable by the owner's own offline key alone, and every protection others hold over a party needs that party's signature to change; a home's receipt counts only alongside the act it names, so a stolen operator key forges nothing that matters; clients never choose a device-bound policy by default.

## What nobody can earn from

- **Harvested data.** Everything is locked by default; relays carry sealed boxes, and keepers record who signed what, never the content.
- **Hidden attention deals.** The core has no built-in reward for views or clicks; a split that follows attention names the metric, its measurer and the payouts.
- **Lock-in.** Identities, stakes, purchases and history are portable; nobody keeps users by holding their data hostage.
- **Hidden fees.** Only an agreement's own entry makes a fee owed; every cut is in the signed split, seen by whoever bears it before they sign; rail fees on payouts are capped by the plan.
- **Money sent where the owners never signed.** A split rule chooses among the addresses the owners signed, never invents one; a pointer that bypasses the split service needs the service to sign publicly for an address not its own.
- **Promises no buyer can contest.** Each sale of a vow is pending, confirmed or contested by the buyer who signed onto it; a proof inside it is evidence, never a verdict.
- **Silent income.** A receiver who signs no receipt, or a smaller one, is exposed by the payer's claim. Hiding income requires the payer's silence or collusion; the core promises exactly that much, and nothing more.
- **Debts that vanish.** A payout without a receipt, a refund unclaimed, a share the owners' rules could not deliver: each stays an open obligation, visible wherever it was sent, until a valid claim appears.

## The invitation

Every pair above is open, and most of the roles inside them can start small: one module, one relay, one home, one keeper, one curated list, one split service, one vow kept. "When I look at MOR I imagine a diversity of ethos and wish to see them compete. It's a building ground" (Nobody, allegedly). The choice sits with those who build clients, relays, services and incentive layers, legible to anyone who wishes to look; users choose among what is built, and can leave one economy for another, taking their identity, their stakes and their agreements with them. The core is designed to be small and frozen once it is proven. The unwritten layer is still to be written, by whoever builds it. MOR is not "this is the protocol", but "this is what the protocol could be, with your help".

**Not claimed.** Each was in the brief or in version 6; the core does not support it as written, so the text says less.

1. *That every split is published to all.* The core delivers a split to every holder it pays and shows a cut to whoever bears it before signing; it publishes nothing to the world unless the parties do. Written as "a signed split that reaches everyone it pays".
2. *That builders earn a role share for every specification a payment ran through.* Only a rail Module's share has its evidence in the receipt; a split's list of the modules it ran under is the service's statement, never what decides. Written as fee entries plus the rail module's share.
3. *That the relay's delivery record is settled.* It exists in relay transport draft 3, not yet approved. The text names it as the evidence a payer's claim acknowledges, without saying the draft is approved.
4. *That transparency is a property of the core.* The core gives the means (publishing a key, publishing a claim, disclosing a negotiation) and makes nothing public by itself. Written as a choice.
5. *That attention is rewarded.* A split may follow a metric; the payment it divides is still a settlement someone made. Written as "attention can divide a payment; it cannot create one".
6. *That the depth of agreement chains is eight.* The number is a freeze parameter; the text says "bounded in depth".
7. *That leaving a deal costs nothing.* A deal ends as its terms say or by a new version every party signs; collectives, services, homes and relays can be left alone, deals cannot. The opening says so.
