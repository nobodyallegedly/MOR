# The unwritten layer

*Companion document, version 3, 12 October 2026. Not part of the core. The redraft of "Who can earn on MOR" (version 6, which stays), under the title of the third film, and now in the film's shape: no story and no named person; the economy we know first, eight other economies between, the one that rewards everyone along the pipe last, then a zoom out to many. Version 1 (nine pairs of economies) and version 2 (one filmmaker's story in two parts) were drafted on 11 October 2026 and withdrawn before merge; the film, finished on 12 October 2026 and voiced by Nobody, allegedly, took a different shape, and this document carries the detail the film has no room for. A draft for the author's own passes. The drafting notes at the end are not part of the text.*

## Six written layers, and one unwritten

MOR has six written layers, six short rulebooks that will be frozen. There is a seventh that nobody writes down, because it is not a protocol layer at all. It is the economies people build on the six: who is paid, for what, by whom, and who gets to see it. In the author's words, "it's there with possibilities to explore various economic models but it is not a protocol layer".

This document proves nothing. The core document and "Don't ban greed, make it legible" do that. The core fixes three things and then steps aside: every reward comes from a payment someone chose to make, never from attention; every cut sits in a signed split that reaches everyone it pays; and leaving costs nothing but what one chose. Everything below is what people have done, or could do, with the room that leaves.

## The one picture

Every economy here can be drawn as the same picture, and only the light changes.

On the left stand those who pay. In the centre is the work, locked or open. Just right of it is the split: the one place where a payment is divided, and the one place where every cut is written. On the right are those the work can pay: the maker, the crew, and at first a production company. Underneath runs the pipe: a keeper, a relay, a client and its builders, and the rules the money runs on.

Gold shows where money goes. Whoever is not paid stays in the picture, unlit. That is what legible means here: nobody disappears, and the absence of payment is as visible as the payment. Each economy below changes only what the gold reaches.

## The economy we know, made honest

Nothing in this part is new. It is the film business as it runs today, moved onto MOR with its economic model untouched. Nothing is kinder. It is only legible.

**The film and its owners.** A documentary, locked. A work claim binds it to its creators. Its agreement names the owners: a production company holding most of it, the filmmaker a third. The crew, an editor, a composer, a sound recordist, are not owners. They were paid fees, as crews are, each fee a deal of its own, signed by both sides, and each finished.

**The keeper.** The production company's lawyer insisted on one thing the crew found exotic: the production deal names a keeper, a notary that records every act around the deal as it arrives, without reading any of it. "If this film sells, people will argue about who signed what, and when. The keeper is cheaper than the argument." The keeper charges a yearly fee for every deal that names it. Its product is its record and its reputation; it earns nothing from the film.

**The distributor.** A distributor offers a three-year deal: seventy per cent of what the film earns until its advance is repaid, forty after. The terms are hard, and they are exactly what they say. The owners' clients show them the plain text before they sign, and the recoupment waterfall that will divide every payment. The distributor holds a stake in its publication of the film, never in the film. Money passes outside in: a sale through the distributor's publication is split there first, the distributor's share and the film's; the film's share goes to the one pointer the film's agreement designates; then the film's own split divides it among the owners. The distributor pays that pointer and divides nothing beyond it. Its responsibility ends where the receipt shows the share arrived.

**The accounting house.** The distributor does its accounting through a split service named in its agreement by a grant. The house receives every payment, publishes a split for each one that sums exactly, pays each party out with a receipt, and takes its fee as a cut in the split, consented by the distributor who bears it. The distributor named a second house as a backup, tried only when the first fails for a payment. The filmmaker sees every split that pays her, whole: the distributor's cut, the house's fee, the rail fees deducted from her payout, capped by the plan. She knew the seventy per cent going in. Now she sees it arrive, payment by payment.

**The pool.** The distributor places the film in a streaming service. Viewers pay a monthly subscription; the service is a pool, which splits its income by minutes watched. Nothing about that changes on MOR except who can see it. The pool's plan names the metric, minutes watched; the identity that measures it, a measurement house that is neither the pool's split service nor anyone the pool pays; and the payouts the metric produced. The pool splits by its stated rules, then the distributor's publication agreement, then the film's. A viewer pays the subscription from a wallet that keeps the viewer's own claim of the payment. If the pool ever signed a receipt for less than was paid, the claim would show it. Nobody expects the pool to. The point is that nobody has to.

**The pipe.** The film's locked files sit on relays, which store and serve them without being able to read them. The distributor pays a relay operator by the gigabyte, under the operator's standing offer: a price, a service level, a promise to keep serving, confirmed or contested month by month. The relay's income is a fee, and nothing it carries is its business. The filmmaker's identity lives on two homes run by two different operators, who hold her identity chain and sign receipts for every key change; she pays each a small yearly fee for hosting over time, which is a promise and is sold as one. The viewer watches in a free app whose developers are paid by nobody on MOR; they are paid by the streaming service under a contract that is not on MOR at all. The rules the clients run, the recoupment split rule, the payment rule, the media formats, were written by people who are paid nothing for them in this economy. Anyone can copy a specification, so nobody can charge for one; only a fee the owners write into their agreement is ever owed, and this agreement writes none. The split names the rules the payment ran under, and shows no share for them. That is a choice, visible as such.

**The critic.** A critic reposts the film to her readers, and several of them subscribe that week. The repost is a reference. It pays nobody by itself, and the pool's rules have no share for it. Her taste moved money, and the record shows it moved nothing to her.

**Promises.** The film premieres in a cinema, which sells seats under a vow: an act naming in words something MOR cannot hash, a seat in row twelve on the evening of the fourteenth. Each buyer signs onto it by paying, and after the evening each sale is confirmed or contested by that buyer and nobody else. The projector failed for ten minutes; three buyers contested; the cinema's refund terms, published with the vow, say what they are owed. The subtitler sells hours on a gig platform, each job a vow confirmed by the client who signed onto it; the platform takes its cut in the split, which the subtitler sees; it cannot take a cut she did not sign, cannot hold her history, and cannot keep her, since her identity, her record and her agreements leave with her. It still takes thirty per cent. Everyone can see that it does.

**Around the money.** A buyer abroad pays on a rail the distributor does not accept; a conversion service carries the money across, paid under its own standing offer, with a receipt for every hop. The production company anchors its agreements to Bitcoin through a service that batches many people's hashes into one anchor and charges per hash; the hash is in the batch or it is not, and nobody's word is involved. Anchoring also decides who bears a theft: the company named a clock and anchors, so a thief who steals its everyday key cannot collect its older debts once the company has locked the key out. When a dispute arose over a milestone of the edit, the deal already named who would decide: an arbitrator, given the keys to the cuts in question and paid to decide. A verification agency verified the archive footage and holds a signed position on the film, income without ownership, paid per sale. A reputation service, which reads signed contests and disclosed records and sells its judgements to buyers of rights, has nothing on anyone in this economy yet.

**What the owners see.** Three years later, the filmmaker's client shows her the film's whole income, from both ends: every settlement, every split that paid her, every fee and who received it. The seventy per cent was seventy per cent. The pool's minutes paid what minutes pay. The critic's reposts, which she can count, paid the critic nothing and her little. The relay, the client developers and the people who wrote the rules were paid nothing from her film. Nothing is hidden, and nothing is unfair in the sense that she signed all of it. The deal ends as its terms say. Her identity, her stake, every receipt and every agreement are hers to take wherever she goes next.

## Other economies

None of these is written in the core, and this is not a list of what is allowed. They are examples, drawn from the case studies, of what the same tools build when people choose differently. The picture is the same each time; only the gold moves.

**Gifts.** Elsewhere, nothing is locked. The work goes out with its key, open to everyone, and people pay because they want to. A payment needs nothing more than a wallet and the maker's pointer; a tip carries no fees but the rail's own. Some send gifts of different sizes; others take the work and pay nothing, and no record is made of them, since a gift that was not given is not an act. A lone maker with no agreement has no split: every gift reaches the maker, and a repost of the work pays nobody by itself. This is the performer's hat. The hat holds what was given to the performer, who may share it afterwards, or not.

**Paid before it exists.** Elsewhere, the audience pays before the work exists. The maker publishes a vow, in words: a film about the harbour, finished by the autumn, a key to it for everyone who paid. Each buyer signs onto the promise by paying, and each sale waits, pending. When the film exists it gets an ordinary work claim of its own, and the maker delivers what was promised; each buyer then confirms the sale, or contests it. One contests: the film came, but a year late, and the vow's refund terms say what a late film owes. The maker's record of promises kept is now part of the next vow's price. Money arrived through a promise, and the work filled in afterwards.

**Lineage.** Elsewhere, a work is free to reuse, and every reuse pays its parent. A musician publishes her stems under a standing offer: free to remix, a fifth of any income to her. A producer makes a remix, a new work whose claim names her stems as its sources. When the remix earns, the money goes to the producer's pointer and is split by the producer's agreement; if that agreement honours her offer, her share is paid, and if it does not, the producer's published split shows exactly that, and every client that reads splits can show a remix that took a free stem and kept the fifth. A remix of the remix names its own source, and each level pays only its immediate parent. Forking stops feeling like theft and starts paying the people it builds on, among people who keep their offers, and anyone who does not is seen not paying.

**The cooperative.** Elsewhere, those who do the work own the platform that sells it. Couriers in a city form a collective: one identity with its own keys, under a founding agreement every member signed, running its own matching app. Each job is a deal between a courier and a customer; the platform's matching fee is a cut in the split, as on any platform, except that the platform's stakes are the couriers', so the cut comes back to them by their shares. A courier who leaves takes her ratings, which were always her own signed record, and keeps her stake until she sells it. The platform at the gate is the same shape as the distant company's. The gold flows through it to the people behind it.

**The members' budget.** Elsewhere, members pool their money and decide together where it flows. A neighbourhood association collects a monthly sum from its members. Each round, every member casts a fixed number of points across the projects on offer, or delegates them, by a grant, to someone they trust on that topic. The budget follows the points, through a split plan in the association's agreement; each project's lead is paid its share as a simple payment with its own receipt, and every unit is visible to every member. The same shape runs a viewers' pool: a monthly sum, split equally among the films its members watched that month, however long. Minutes are not the only metric, and a metric is not the only rule.

**Paid on results.** Elsewhere, nobody is paid for showing. Two people recommend a work. A buyer follows one of them, buys, and the buyer's wallet names the repost it followed in the buyer's own claim of the payment. The owners' plan has a share for the referrer the buyer names, and that one is paid. The other showed the work to a thousand people and sold nothing, and is paid nothing, which the record shows as plainly as the sale. No service can name a referrer of its own; only the buyer's signature counts. A brand sells under the same rule: an agency is paid for the sales buyers say it led to, never for impressions. Where several reposts led to one sale, only the one the buyer names is paid; how several might share a sale is not settled. [OPEN: F184]

**The commons.** Elsewhere, the work is free for all, and those who depend on it fund its makers. A developer publishes a library under a free licence, open to everyone. A company sells an app built on it, among two hundred other libraries; the app's agreement names its dependencies as lineage, and its split carries a share for them, divided down the tree, each level paying its immediate parents. Whether it does is the app's own choice, and an app that names two hundred libraries and pays none of them publishes a split that says exactly that. A company that needs a feature publishes a bounty: an amount, held by a custodian until the work is accepted, split between whoever builds it and the maintainer who reviews it. Another pays one monthly sum for its whole dependency tree, divided by a rule it chose. Nobody is forced. The ones who pay are lit; the ones who do not are in the picture too.

**The closed circle.** Or the same tools, with the terms kept among those who signed. A studio and its partners run every economy above inside a sealed box: their agreements, their splits and their flows are encrypted to the parties, and an outsider reaches only the box. Less is visible here, and it is worth saying exactly what remains. Every party sees every split that pays them, whole, with each fee and who received it. Whoever bears a cut sees it before signing. A collective keeps no secret from its own members, and anyone can see that it acts, never what. The payer's side of every payment is the payer's to publish, so a partner who under-reports is exposed by the one who paid. Nothing more is promised. Closed means closed to outsiders, never hidden from those it concerns.

## Where the split rewards everyone along the pipe

And some reward everyone along the pipe. The author calls this one the shower, after a text of his on gratitude: standing under a shower and tracing the water back through everyone who helped it arrive. On MOR the water is a payment, and the pipe is everyone between the work and the one who paid for it. Here the owners wrote all of them into the split.

**The relay that chose the film.** A video relay charges no fee. It watches the films offered to it, chooses the ones it will carry, and is written into each film's publication agreement with a small stake, the owners consenting. Its income is a share of every sale of every film it carries, and nothing when nothing sells. The curation is the service: a film on this relay has been chosen, and viewers know what that means. Across town a text relay still charges by the gigabyte, because nobody takes a stake in a message. Same role, two economies, and each split shows which.

**The client that pays its builders.** The owners publish through a client whose developers propose a split plan to every owner who publishes through it: a share for the client, for the sales it brings, and small shares for the maintainers of the split rule, the payment rule and the video format the client runs. The owners sign the plan, or they do not; the client proposes, the owners decide, and the shares come out of the owners' split. These owners sign, for the reasons the developers wrote down: the people who maintain the rules that carry the money should be paid from it, a client whose builders are paid will still exist next year, and a split that pays its builders says something about who made it. The developers of the video format earn from the plan without ever having touched the film. The rail that carried each payment earns a share, evidenced by the receipt itself. A developer who wanted a feature the client lacked offered a bounty, and someone built it. In the economy we know, the same rules carried the same money and were paid nothing. Here the owners chose otherwise, and the split shows it.

**The critic, paid.** A critic reposts the film. The owners' plan has a share for the referrer who led to a sale, so when a reader buys and the reader's wallet names her repost, she is paid her share, and the record shows which sales were hers.

**The keeper, the same.** The deal names a keeper, the same notary as in the economy we know, at the same yearly fee, its record a little longer. It does not care which economy a deal belongs to. Serious business is serious business everywhere.

**The rest of the picture.** The film has no production company this time. The filmmaker and her crew founded a collective, one identity with its own keys held by a threshold of its members, and nobody is paid a fee: each holds a stake in the collective and so a share of everything it earns. The agreement gives the composer the money lane, so she chooses the rails and the split rule, and the editor the publications lane; a manager acts through a grant, within limits, and can be revoked. When the editor needs money mid-production he sells a quarter of his stake to an investor, who inherits exactly that position and no voice. Three publishers carry the film at once, each under its own publication agreement: one takes a cut, one takes a stake in its publication, one takes nothing and sells its own commentary alongside, and the owners compare them by settlements, not promises. The collective also sells direct, under its own standing offer, the key delivered on payment; the composer chose payer-side splitting, where each buyer's wallet pays every owner and the relay directly, with no service between them, at the cost of several payments and the owners' pointers shown to the buyer. With real money arriving, the collective keeps a vault for large payments, whose entries change only with its offline key, and points its everyday flow at a custodial service that enforces a daily limit the core cannot; the filmmaker moves her identity to three homes, one of which refuses any key change without proof from a device she registered, having told her plainly what losing the device would cost; an auditor cosigns her homes' logs; she buys insurance against theft of her keys. Each of these is a market, and each a choice.

**The viewer.** The viewer earns nothing and decides a great deal. Her wallet names the repost she followed, confirms the promise of a live conversation with the crew or contests it, and keeps her own claim of every payment, which is equal evidence against anyone who under-reports it. Her client's developers chose to build a wallet that carries her choices into the payment. A viewer who stays wholly anonymous, committing no key at all, can reward no critic and no relay. This one chose a name.

**Two limits, stated.** The reward reaches only those written into a split the owners signed. Nothing here is automatic: a relay, a builder or a critic is paid because the owners wrote them in, and in the economy we know the same owners wrote them out. And the water does not yet reach everywhere one might wish: a chain of reposts pays only the one the buyer names, and how several referrers might share one sale is still open. [OPEN: F184]

## Many economies, one core

Pull back from the keeper and the picture repeats, lit in a different pattern each time: the ones above, and dozens more that nobody has drawn yet. None of them is written in the core, and all of them are built with the same tools. They are not sealed off from each other. An identity is the same identity in every one of them; a text can be read in every one; an agreement made in one is in the same form as an agreement made in any other, and can be cloned across. A distributor from the economy we know can buy a film from the collective that rewards its relay; a courier's record from the cooperative is her record wherever she next works; old deals keep paying under their old terms when their parties move on; the same conversion services carry money between rails, and the same keeper takes deals from all of them. Money moves between economies as easily as within one, because a payment is a payment, and a split is a split, in all of them.

Each is legible: its terms signed by those they bind, its cuts in the split, its exits open. Anyone can leave any of them, taking what is theirs.

## What nobody can earn from

- **Harvested data.** Everything is locked by default; relays carry sealed boxes, and keepers record who signed what, never the content.
- **Hidden attention deals.** The core has no built-in reward for views or clicks; a pool that splits by minutes names the metric, its measurer and the payouts.
- **Lock-in.** Identities, stakes, purchases and history are portable; nobody keeps users by holding their data hostage.
- **Hidden fees.** Only an agreement's own entry makes a fee owed; every cut is in the signed split, seen by whoever bears it before they sign; rail fees on payouts are capped by the plan.
- **Money sent where the owners never signed.** A split rule chooses among the addresses the owners signed, never invents one.
- **Promises no buyer can contest.** Each sale of a vow is pending, confirmed or contested by the buyer who signed onto it.
- **Silent income.** A receiver who signs no receipt, or a smaller one, is exposed by the payer's claim. Hiding income requires the payer's silence or collusion; the core promises exactly that much, and nothing more.
- **Debts that vanish.** A payout without a receipt, a refund unclaimed, a share nobody could deliver: each stays an open obligation, visible, until a valid claim appears.

## The invitation

Every participant in every economy above is open to anyone, and most can start small: one relay, one home, one keeper, one curated list, one split service, one vow kept. "When I look at MOR I imagine a diversity of ethos and wish to see them compete. It's a building ground" (Nobody, allegedly). The economies are made by those who build clients, relays, services and rules, legible to anyone who wishes to look; the rest of us choose among them, and leave when we like, taking everything that is ours. The core is small and will be frozen. The unwritten layer is yours.
