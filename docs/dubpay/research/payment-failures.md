# Payment failures in Nostr and Lightning, and how users got out

Date: 2026-11-20

## Summary

- Documented money losses came mostly from services that held users' money or all-powerful wallet credentials: closures with deadlines, hacked account servers, stolen node credentials.
- Where users held their own keys, services closed or went offline and the funds stayed put.
- No ecash mint theft and no forged-zap campaign was found. That is absence of evidence, not safety.
- Zap receipts are statements by a payment service, not proof. Several apps skipped the checks; found by review, not by attack.
- Split payments cannot be all-or-nothing. Unpaid receivers are never told.
- Escrow on Lightning makes the judge the holder of the money. Disputes outlast the hold.
- Warning ranged from none to about a year. Leftover balances were never published.
- Evidence: of 96 cases, 34 were read at source (GitHub only), 46 are known only from search summaries, and 16 are mixed.

## Cases

**Limit of this evidence.** The research session could open only GitHub pages; every other page (news sites, company blogs, forums, Nostr gateways) was not opened and is known only from search-result summaries, so each row says "read at source" or "search summary, page not opened", and nothing has been upgraded.

Further notes on reading the tables:

- For the first two tables (custodial services, ecash) no page at all was opened, GitHub included.
- Some GitHub pages were read through a fetching tool that summarises pages, so short quotes from them may not be letter-exact.
- Day counts in the "Warning" column are the researchers' own arithmetic from the two dates given.
- Each row is also marked as one of: "documented incident"; "weakness found by review, no known exploitation"; "structural, no incident".
- Every entry in the design-choice column is the researchers' inference, not a finding of any source and not anyone's decision.
- "Not published" means no figure appeared in what was read.
- Twenty sources are given as a plain reference with "link withheld": their addresses contain a personal name or account name, which the hand-back rule keeps out of this repository. The project name and item number are given so the source can still be found.

Words used, in plain terms:

- Custodial: a company holds the money for you. Self-custodial: you hold the keys yourself.
- Lightning address: a payment name that looks like an email address. A service looks it up and says where to pay.
- Zap: a Lightning tip sent through Nostr. Zap receipt: a public Nostr note, written by the receiver's payment service, saying a zap was paid.
- Nostr Wallet Connect (NWC): a secret link that lets an app ask your wallet to pay.
- Ecash mint: a server that holds bitcoin and hands out digital tokens for it. Federation (Fedimint): a mint run jointly by several "guardians".
- Hold invoice: a payment the receiver can freeze and later take or release.
- Keysend: a payment pushed straight to a Lightning node, with no invoice from the receiver.
- Force-close: closing a Lightning channel without the other side's help; it costs on-chain fees and locks funds for days.
- LSP: a company that supplies channels to a self-custodial wallet.
- Macaroon: a password-like file that gives a program control of a Lightning node.

## Rules for a way out

All rules below are the researchers' inferences from the cases above. They are proposals for DubPay, not anyone's decision. Rules that rest only on unverified or no-incident evidence say so.

1. DubPay never holds users' money. It talks to a wallet the user controls, so that DubPay closing, pausing or being blocked leaves every balance where it was.
   Taught by: C10, C11, C12, C7, X1, X3, X11, C17, C18.
   Reason: where a company held the money, a silent or closed operator meant rationing, forfeiture or loss; where users held it, the service vanished and the funds did not. (Most of these rows are search summaries.)

2. The user's payment name must be the user's to re-point. Changing wallet must not mean changing the name people pay.
   Taught by: C1, C3, C5, C6, S3, Z12.
   Reason: addresses survived only where the same provider kept running the lookup; dead addresses in podcast feeds went unnoticed for 22 months.

3. If any service in the path ever holds a balance, its exit rules are stated in advance: stop new money first, give months to withdraw, keep a claim path open afterwards, and never turn a leftover into credits, fees or another currency.
   Taught by: C3, C7, C8, C9, C4, E2, X2, X3.
   Reason: what happens to leftovers at cut-off is a choice, and the bad choices turned a missed notice into a loss.

4. Closing and withdraw-only notices are shown inside the app, to the affected user, until acted on. A blog post is not a notice.
   Taught by: C12, E2, E3, C7, W5.
   Reason: every documented exit depended on the user happening to see an announcement.

5. Any ecash balance is described as "held by the mint", is capped per mint, and sits only at mints the user has approved. DubPay never adds a mint because a token arrived from it.
   Taught by: E8, E15, E2, E9.
   Reason: the projects themselves say an operator can run off with the balance. This rule rests on weaknesses and structural facts only; no mint theft was found, and nothing here was read at source.

6. An ecash tip that has not been redeemed is shown as "not yet money". DubPay pays ecash only to a mint the receiver has listed.
   Taught by: E14, E13.
   Reason: the published rules warn the money can be burned, and the sender cannot take it back. Structural only; no incident found.

7. DubPay watches the health of any mint or federation it uses (reachable, version, closing flag) and tells the user to leave when it worsens.
   Taught by: E5, E6, E7, E1.
   Reason: a mint that is down strands balances even when nothing is stolen. Rests on search summaries; no loss recorded.

8. A zap receipt from someone else's service is shown as "service X says this was paid". DubPay says "paid" on its own authority only when the user's own wallet confirms that exact payment.
   Taught by: Z9, Z2, Z8, Z6, S4.
   Reason: the rules call the receipt "not a proof of payment", and an honest but careless service signed false ones. No exploitation is documented; these are review findings.

9. Every receipt is checked against the key the receiver's own payment service publishes, and against the note and receiver it claims. When the check cannot be made, the receipt is shown as unverified, never as real.
   Taught by: Z1, Z3, Z5, Z7.
   Reason: each documented bug was a skipped check or a check that gave up silently. Review findings; no known exploitation.

10. Nothing of value (access, goods, rank, refunds) is released on a public receipt alone.
    Taught by: Z8, Z2, Z10.
    Reason: a paywall accepted a forged receipt twice in a row; self-payment makes valid receipts cheap. Review findings and structure; no known exploitation.

11. Before paying an invoice fetched from a payment name, DubPay reads it and refuses if the amount is not what the user agreed or it has expired. It never pays an unchecked invoice automatically.
    Taught by: Z4, Z5.
    Reason: this is the one zap-side path where the payer loses money directly. Found by review; no loss reported.

12. DubPay remembers the payment address last used for a contact and warns when it has changed.
    Taught by: Z12, W4.
    Reason: whoever controls the name later receives the money, with a valid-looking receipt. Structural, plus one unverified report of a payout address changed without consent.

13. For a split, DubPay works out every share before sending and shows the payer which receivers cannot be paid and why. The payer confirms with that in view.
    Taught by: S1, S2, S3, S12.
    Reason: shares too small, dead destinations and unsupported payment types were all knowable before sending.

14. Each share has its own result. "Paid" is written only from proof that this share settled for more than zero. An unpaid share stays on a visible list with a retry, and one failure never stops the others.
    Taught by: S4, S5, S6, S9, S14.
    Reason: Lightning cannot make a split all-or-nothing, and apps have recorded unpaid receivers as paid.

15. DubPay states one rule for an undeliverable share (payer keeps it, or it stays owed) and never quietly gives it to the other receivers.
    Taught by: S14, S10, S13.
    Reason: the standards choose no rule, so today the same split pays out differently in each app. Structural; no incident.

16. A payment with no clear answer is shown as "undecided, may still go through". DubPay does not retry or refund until the first attempt has clearly failed.
    Taught by: S11, W9, X14.
    Reason: a blind retry can pay twice, and treating "slow" as "failed" was rated a total-loss flaw. Review findings; no loss confirmed.

17. A receiver can find out that a share was due to them and not paid.
    Taught by: S3, S5, S13, S14.
    Reason: no app found tells the unpaid receiver anything, which is why breakage is found by outsiders or not at all.

18. If DubPay ever offers held payments or buyer protection, the one who decides a dispute is not the one who holds the money. If that cannot be done, the app says so in plain words before the trade.
    Taught by: X15, X7, X11.
    Reason: in every hold-invoice system read, judge and vault are one party, restrained only by reputation. No theft by an arbiter is documented; X7 is an unresolved complaint.

19. Dispute rules are published before any trade: what evidence counts, the deadlines, who reviews a decision. Evidence travels in the same signed channel as the trade.
    Taught by: X7, X8.
    Reason: one dispute demanded identity-revealing video and had no appeal; another stalled because the evidence channel was a separate chat.

20. A held payment is shown as its own state with the latest time it must end. What happens at that time is decided and told to both sides beforehand.
    Taught by: X14, X16, X5.
    Reason: holds last days and disputes can last weeks; at expiry the money returns to one side whether or not that is fair.

21. DubPay never contacts a user first about money, and gives users a way to check that a message is really from it.
    Taught by: X13, X10.
    Reason: an impersonator took a seller's funds, and a genuine emergency email was taken for phishing. Both rows are search summaries.

22. DubPay asks a wallet for the narrowest link that does the job: a spending limit, an end date, only the actions needed. It shows the user what was granted and warns on a spend-everything link.
    Taught by: W6, W7, W2, W16.
    Reason: every total drain went through a credential with no limit and no end. No case of a limit capping a theft is documented; this is inference from the opposite cases.

23. DubPay stores a wallet link where nothing else can read it, never logs it, deletes it on logout, and shows the user where in their wallet to cancel it.
    Taught by: W6, W14, W15, W16.
    Reason: only the wallet can cancel a link, so the app's duties are to keep its copy safe and to point the user to the off switch.

24. DubPay expects a wallet link to be cancelled or replaced without notice, and guides the user through reconnecting.
    Taught by: W4, C5, W1.
    Reason: migrations and incident responses both broke every connected app at once.

25. Nothing that changes where money goes (payout address, wallet link) can be changed with the Nostr key alone.
    Taught by: W4, Z12.
    Reason: a Nostr key cannot be cancelled once stolen. Rests on an unverified report and a structural fact; no confirmed diversion.

26. DubPay tells the user of every payment made through their link and how much of the limit is used.
    Taught by: W6, C3, W4.
    Reason: in the documented losses nobody was told; they found an empty balance.

27. DubPay advises a small, separate wallet for everyday use and says why.
    Taught by: W1, W2, W3, W5, C14.
    Reason: where damage was limited, it was because little money sat behind the stolen credential.

28. DubPay has a written incident plan: a way to reach users at once, "update" and "replace every credential" as separate steps, and a statement made in advance of whether anyone covers losses caused by software.
    Taught by: W1, W2, W3, X9, E10.
    Reason: stolen credentials kept working after the fix, operators could not be reached, and repayment depended on goodwill.

29. DubPay does not depend on one app store, one relay or one server to work.
    Taught by: C2, C16, C19, X19, W13, X4, E5.
    Reason: store removals gave zero to fourteen days' notice; a single relay or coordinator going down locked users out mid-payment.

30. DubPay states the costs of holding your own money: the app may need to be open to receive, and small balances can cost more to move than they are worth.
    Taught by: C5, C16, C20, W8, C17.
    Reason: every move from custody to self-custody added a duty or a fee that users met only at the moment of exit.

31. A user's payment history and any reputation are theirs to export, signed, and to show selectively.
    Taught by: X18, C8.
    Reason: no closed platform was found to have exported reputation. Structural; inferred from absence.

32. DubPay says plainly that it cannot protect against fraud on the ordinary-money side of a trade.
    Taught by: X12, X13.
    Reason: a bank reversal arrives after the bitcoin has been released, and no bitcoin-side hold can undo it.
