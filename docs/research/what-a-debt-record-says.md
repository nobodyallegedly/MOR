## How to read the marks

- **counted**: computed by a script from the table in Part 1 of this file.
- **traced**: shown by the sequence of moments in a named use of the map, or by a named rule of the direct-debit rulebook or of a draft.
- **sourced**: taken from a source I opened; cited.
- **from the addendum**: taken from the digest of the research window's addendum of 19 November given in this session's prompt. I could not read the addendum itself (see "The sources"), so these are not sources I read.
- **from general knowledge**: what I know, checked against nothing.
- **inferred**: my reading, with what it rests on and a confidence range out of 100.

Four things are kept apart throughout. **A debt**: who owes whom, how much, in what. **A statement about a debt**: signed by someone. **A movement on a rail**: something leaves one holder and reaches another, which the record may never hear of. **A rule about debts**: a schedule, an interest rate, a deadline, which says what should happen and is not itself a happening. Debts are written "Ana→Ben 100": Ana owes Ben 100.

## Plain words first

**The smallest set, in ten lines.** (The set is inferred from the table; the numbers are counted.)

1. Three statements track a debt through everything the map found. Each is signed, and each can point at an earlier statement.
2. **Owed.** "This one owes that one so much, of this." It opens a debt. It names the one who owes, the one who is owed, the amount, the unit, and what the debt rests on.
3. **Ended.** "This debt is over, by so much." It closes a debt, whole or in part. It names the debt and how much of it is over, counted in the debt's own unit. It may name what ended it: something handed over, another debt, or nothing.
4. **Passed.** "This debt is now owed to someone else." It names the debt, the creditor who leaves and the creditor who arrives. Nothing is closed.
5. His two sentences are the first two. "Pay this pls" is Owed said by the one who is owed. "This has been paid" is Ended.
6. Who signs decides what a statement is worth. Each counts by itself when the party who loses by it has signed: Owed by the one who owes, Ended by the one who is owed, Passed by the creditor who leaves. Signed by anyone else it is a claim (inferred, 60 to 78).
7. Of the map's 197 moments, 146 are carried by one or more of the three (counted).
8. The other 51 need no fourth statement (counted; that none does is inferred, 55 to 72). In 15 an agreement or a law speaks (a permit, a fence, a request where nothing is owed). In 10 only a rail acts. In 9 a debt already open is said again. In 9 time runs out and nobody says anything. In 6 something is paid where nothing was owed. In 2 the point is that nothing is said.
9. Debts mostly close without ever having been stated: of 95 moments that close a debt, 41 close one that only a rule had opened (a wage, a rent, a share, a dividend), 22 one that a deed had opened, and 32 one that somebody had said (counted).
10. The next smallest set has four: it splits Ended into "paid" and "over without being paid". It buys his second sentence kept literally true, and a reader who can add up what was really paid.

**Whether the frame held.** For most of the map, yes; under the test the assignment set, it breaks in one place.

- Two statements with a pointer to an earlier statement carry every moment except one kind (inferred from the table, 70 to 85).
- The one kind is a debt handed on to a new creditor: a voucher paid away, a keeper's balance moved to another name, a guarantor stepping into the lender's place. It is in 9 moments in 7 uses (counted). It closes nothing and opens nothing, so by the test "what does the statement do to a debt" it is a third statement (inferred, 60 to 78).
- It can be written with the two, as an ending that names a new debt and an opening that names the old one. The cost is that a debt nobody paid reads as ended, and the new debt is opened by someone who does not owe it. One party's own books do exactly this (from general knowledge); a record that both sides share cannot do it without saying something untrue of the debt (inferred, 55 to 75).
- Both of his sentences have to be read wider than their words to carry the map. "Pay this pls" must also cover "I owe you", said by the debtor with no request, and a debt announced with no request to act. "This has been paid" must also cover a debt forgiven, set against another, or lapsed, where nothing was paid. Whether that is still his frame is his to say.
- Three places strain the three statements without breaking them, in my reading: a payment where nothing was owed (a gift, a tip, a donation; and so, possibly, the single click when it is a reward and not a price); money fenced where it lies; and time running out, which in a record of sequence nobody says.

**What Money draft 14 has beyond the smallest set, and what it lacks.** Written in the second commit, after Part 5; nothing above it was changed then.

- **What it has of the set.** Two of the three statements, by other names (traced). Owed is its obligation, signed by the debtor. Ended is three acts: the receipt (the one who received says so), the payment claim (the one who paid says so) and the creditor's release (over without being paid). Of the 76 acts, fields and rules I listed, 31 map to these two (counted).
- **What it has beyond the set** (counted from the same list; the columns are inferred). 19 lines about the movement itself: where a payment goes and how it is proven on a rail. 10 that are managing by Part 4's test, most of them about a stolen key and who bears the loss. 7 that the draft itself hands to Agreements. 5 for the price tag, an offer to anyone. 3 that are another layer's, and 1 that says what Money does not see.
- **What it lacks** (traced to the draft's text; I did not read Agreements). No act hands a debt to a new creditor, and a debt cannot be owed to "whoever holds this". The one who is owed has no act to say "this is owed". A creditor's word that a debt was paid off any rail is the same act as letting it go. Nothing names two debts set against each other.
- **One thing it does that Parts 1 to 4 did not see coming** (traced, rules 8 to 11): a payment counts on neither party's word. It counts on a proof from the rail that anyone can check, and the two signed records are evidence beside it.

## Part 1. The eleven moments, as things that happen to a debt

### How the moments were read

The map told each use as moments that touch money. Here each moment is read again as something that happens to a debt. The readings are mine (inferred from the map's stories; that another hand would tag nine tenths of the rows the same way, 55 to 75). Where a story does not say whether a debt stood behind a move, I took the ordinary case.

**Effect** is one or two of:

- **open**: a debt comes to be.
- **close**: a debt ends, whole or in part, because something was handed over or done.
- **pass**: a debt gets a new creditor and is otherwise the same.
- **set-off**: two debts end against each other, and nothing is handed over.
- **end-unpaid**: a debt ends and nothing was handed over or set against it.
- **fence**: what someone holds or is owed can no longer be used, and no debt changes hands.
- **rule**: nobody acts; a rule changes a debt, or makes a conditional debt certain.
- **none**: no debt is touched.

**Who says or does it** is that party's place in the debt the row is about: the debtor, the creditor, a holder (a third party who holds or carries: a keeper, a bank, a carrier, a treasurer, an agent), a power (a court, an officer, a law, a network's ruling), one side (one party's own book), both, nobody, or "no debt" where there is none. In rows with two effects the label is the place of whoever acts in the first-named debt, so counts of signers are rough.

**The record** says what, if anything, a record of debts would have to hold for this moment:

- **Owed, Ended, Passed**: the three statements of Part 2.
- **agreement**: something an agreement between the parties says (a condition, a permit, an offer, a rate), which a statement about a debt can point at.
- **law**: the same, from outside the deal.
- **rail**: only a keeper's or carrier's dealings change; the debt between payer and payee is not touched by this moment.
- **restates**: a debt already open is said again (a demand, a reminder, a holder showing a ticket).
- **nobody says**: time runs out.
- **free payment**: something is paid where nothing was owed.
- **kept out**: the point of the use is that no record exists.

**Closed debt was opened by**, for rows that end a debt: a **word** (some moment in the story said the debt), a **deed** (a handing-over or work told in the story), or a **rule** (an agreement, a vote, a law, a calendar; no moment in the story opened it).

### Which moments are only a rail's business

**Ten moments in five uses** (counted: 4.9, 6.1, 6.5, 7.2, 7.8): four orders to one's own keeper, one order from carrier to carrier, the card presentment, the netting and the settling between carriers (three moments), and a bank's notice to its own customer that it did not pay.

**And the movement inside every move** (inferred, 75 to 90). All 110 moves are deeds on a rail. A record of debts does not hold the deed. It holds what someone says the deed did to a debt. Under "We don't move money, we track debt" the map's Move and Say it moved change places: the saying is the record's, and the move is the rail's.

"A rail's business" does not mean "no debt" (inferred from the addendum's account of Mehrling and of the BIS definition of a payment, and from the map's surprise 10; 60 to 78). A keeper's balance is the keeper's debt to the one who left money with her, and a payment through her is that debt handed on. The rail is debts one level up. I tagged the street's keeper in 1.3 as a party, with her debts in the table, and the banks in 7.8 as a rail. That line is mine, and it moves with whether the keeper writes in the same record.

What the record loses by not hearing the rail (each inferred, from the uses or rules named):

1. **Proof.** The debtor's "this is closed" cannot be checked against anything. In 2.4 the map says that without Ana's note Ben has money and cannot tell what it settles; the mirror case is a note with no money behind it.
2. **In what it was paid.** The record says the debt is over. It does not say what the creditor now holds in its place: coin, or a bank's promise, or a carrier's (from the addendum: each level's money is a promise of the level above).
3. **Whether it can still be undone.** The rulebook lets a payer's bank send a collection back for five business days, and the payer have it back for eight weeks, or thirteen months if it was never authorised (from the addendum). A record that heard only "closed" learns of an undoing only if somebody says the debt is owed again.
4. **Late against failed.** The bank's "it did not go" (6.5) reaches the payer and not the record. The debt simply stays open. Nothing false is recorded; a reader cannot tell a late payer from a failed payment.
5. **The fence.** That money is reserved for a payee (7.8) is known to the keeper alone, unless the keeper says so.

What it does not lose: who owed whom, how much, in what, and whether the one who is owed says it is over.

## Part 2. The smallest set

### The candidate sets

- **A. Two, plain.** "This is owed" and "this is over". Each describes its debt by the parties, the amount and the unit. Neither can point at another statement.
- **B. Two, with a pointer.** The same two, and each must name the earlier statement it concerns: an ending names the debt it ends; an opening may name what it rests on or what it answers.
- **C. Three.** B, and "this debt is now owed to someone else".
- **D. Four.** C, with the ending split in two: "paid", and "over without being paid".
- **E. More.** One statement for each kind of moment (sixteen), or for each named word of a scheme (the direct-debit rulebook has seven for "back" alone: from the addendum).

A note on counting. If one statement may carry any number of fields, one statement is enough for everything, and the count means nothing. The assignment's rule is the one I used: two statements are the same kind if they do the same thing to a debt, whoever signs and whatever else they name; they are different kinds if one of them leaves the debt in a state the other cannot.

| Revocation, request for cancellation | The collector or its bank, before settlement; words only, outside the scheme | The creditor takes back an Owed: Ended, said by the creditor, naming nothing paid. |

So the seven come to two things in a record of debts: nothing (the reject and the refusal, the debt staying open), and an earlier statement answered by a later one that names it (the other five). That is the book-keeper's rule that an entry is never erased and is answered by a reversing entry (from general knowledge).

### The smallest set

Three statements (inferred; that three is the smallest under the assignment's test, 60 to 78; that no moment in the map needs a fourth, 55 to 72).

| Statement | In one plain line | What it must name |
|---|---|---|
| **Owed** | "This one owes that one so much, of this." | The one who owes; the one who is owed (a named party, or "whoever holds this"); the amount; the unit. What it rests on, where there is something: an agreement and which turn of it, an earlier statement, a permit. |
| **Ended** | "This debt is over, by so much." | The debt: the Owed that opened it, or, where no Owed did, the agreement and turn that did. How much of it is over, in the debt's own unit. It may name what ended it: something handed over, another debt, nothing. |
| **Passed** | "This debt is now owed to someone else." | The debt; the creditor who leaves; the creditor who arrives; how much, if not all. |

Everything else in the map is one of these said by a different signer or naming something different, or is an agreement's or a law's, or is a rail's, or is nobody's.

### The next smallest, and the one below

**Below: two with a pointer (set B).** It carries everything but passing on, and passing on as a tied pair. What the third statement buys (inferred, 60 to 78):

- a debt that nobody paid is never recorded as over;
- no debt is opened by someone who does not owe it;
- a debt keeps one name from its opening to its end, through any number of creditors, so a debtor's part payments and a condition written at the start stay attached to it;
- a reader who adds up what a debtor has had ended adds up only what really ended.

What it costs: a third kind of statement, and a rule for whose signature makes it count. One party's own books do without it. In double entry an account has two motions, a debit and a credit; a receivable sold is a credit to one account and, in the buyer's books, a debit to another, and the debtor's own books change nothing but a name (from general knowledge). So the third statement belongs to a record that the debtor and both creditors read together, and not to any one book.

**Above: four.** The nearest fourth, in my reading, splits Ended:

- **Paid**: "this debt is over because it was paid" (by something handed over or by another debt).
- **Over, unpaid**: "this debt is over and nothing was paid" (forgiven, withdrawn, lapsed, wiped out by a court).

What the difference buys (inferred, 55 to 75): his second sentence, "this has been paid", stays literally true wherever it is said; a reader can add up what a person was actually paid, which is the question behind the single click; and a third party whose own debt depends on the difference can read it (a guarantor is free if the lender forgives, and steps into the lender's place if she herself pays: 1.11). In the set of three the same difference rides in what Ended "may name", and a reader has it only when the signer chooses to say.

Other fourths I tried, and what each would buy (all inferred):

| A fourth statement | What it would buy | Why I rank it below the split of Ended |
|---|---|---|
| **"Not so"**: a statement that contests an earlier one | Tells silence from denial. Carries the rulebook's refusal, and a dispute, without a rule about what silence means. | The opposite statement, naming the contested one, already says it: an Ended by the debtor against an Owed she denies, an Owed by the creditor against an Ended she denies. 55 to 75. |

**Above that: one statement per moment or per word.** Sixteen for the map's kinds, or a scheme's own list. The rulebook's seven words for "back" come to two shapes in a record of debts (above); the 315 status-reason codes and 104 return-reason codes counted in the ISO 20022 code sets (from the addendum) are reasons riding on those shapes. What the long lists buy is a fixed meaning for each case inside one scheme. What they cost is that each scheme's list differs (the map found "reversal" and "refund" meaning different things in cards and in direct debit).

### The single click, in these terms

A reading of the map's own section, not a proposal (traced from the map's uses 2.7, 2.8, 2.9, 3.1, 3.3; inferred that these are the right ones, 65 to 80).

- If the click pays a **price**, there is a debt: an Owed for the price, its Ended, and then one Owed and one Ended for each share the first receiver owes onward. Those onward debts are opened by a rule (the split), so their endings name the agreement and the sale, not an Owed (candidate 14).
- If the click is a **reward** where nothing was owed, and it is fanned out at the source, it is several payments where nothing was owed, and a record of debts has nothing to say about any of them (candidate 13). If it goes to one receiver who shares it out, the receiver's debts to the others open by rule when it arrives.
- Whether "all the people" were rewarded is then read from endings signed by each of them, or from one person's say-so, or from a rail. Which of those is enough is Part 3's question.

## Part 3. Who must say it for it to count

### Statement by statement

All inferred; each line names the use or rule that shows it.

**Owed.** The signature that opens a debt by itself is the debtor's: the baker's voucher (1.9), the keeper's "it is with me" (1.4), the pledge (3.8), the bank's "approved" (7.8). The creditor's signature alone makes a claim. The claim becomes a debt when the debtor had signed beforehand (the permit in 2.3; in the rulebook the mandate is signed by the payer, and the collector keeps it and sends its details with every collection: from the addendum), or signs or acts afterwards (pays the 3 at the till, 2.1), or when a power stands behind the creditor (the ticket, 4.2; the judgment, 4.8). Both signatures make an agreed debt (the elders in 5.7). In the map 22 Asks open a debt, 19 said by the creditor and 3 by a holder for the creditor (counted): the map mostly tells debts as opened by the one who is owed, and leaves the debtor's assent to the next move.

**Ended.** The signature that closes a debt by itself is the creditor's: the receipt (2.1, 4.4). The debtor's alone is a claim of payment. A holder's proves that something moved, and closes the debt only where the creditor had accepted that holder as the place to be paid (from the addendum: the BIS glossary's payment is a transfer of a claim on a party the payee accepts). A power closes by force (the takings in 4.2, 4.8, 7.9). In the map the creditor's own word is a moment in 3 of 95 closings and the debtor's deed in 56 (counted): the stories close debts by paying, and take the creditor's silence as assent.

**Passed.** The signature that hands a debt on is the creditor's who leaves: Ana ordering her keeper (1.3), Ana paying Carla with the voucher (1.9). The debtor need not sign and in those uses is not asked. The debtor does need to be able to learn of it, or will pay the old creditor (from general knowledge: a debtor who pays the old creditor before hearing of an assignment is free). The arriving creditor's word alone is a claim; in the map she proves it by holding something (the lottery ticket, 5.5; the word given at the counter, 7.2). A power can hand a debt on without the creditor (the executor, 5.3; the liquidator, 7.12; the guarantor stepping in by rule, 1.11).

**A debt taken over** (not a statement of the set; the tied pair of 1b). It needs the one who takes it over and the creditor. The old debtor's word is not needed: a third person can pay or promise for another (the employer and the tax, 2.6). The old debtor's word alone does nothing.

**One line under all of it** (inferred, 60 to 78): a statement counts by itself when it is signed by the party who loses by it. The debtor loses by Owed, the creditor by Ended, the creditor who leaves by Passed. From the party who gains, the same words are a claim, waiting for the other signature, an earlier permission, a holder both accepted, or a power.

## Part 4. Tracking or managing

**This coding is mine and is inferred.** The test is his, in the MOR lead's wording of it: managing settles how a deal treats people, and is built on top. I applied it with two questions. Could two groups of honest people reasonably want opposite answers for their own deals? If yes, managing. Must two honest readers of the same statements give the same answer, whatever the parties wanted? If yes, tracking. The entry it comes from ("Built on top, as a test") was about Agreements; carrying it to Money is the assignment's step, and mine.

- **Owed** is the obligation (type 1), "signed by the debtor". The draft says in so many words that a creditor's statement of what it is owed "is a claim, never an obligation". That is Part 3's line for Owed.
- **Ended** is three acts. The receipt (type 2) is the one who received saying so. The payment claim (type 3) is the one who paid saying so. The creditor's release (type 4) is the creditor letting a debt go "without full payment". So draft 14 already splits the ending the way Part 2's "next smallest" set does: paid, and over without being paid.
- **What makes a payment count** in draft 14 is not either party's signature. It is the rail's proof, which the receipt and the claim both carry and which anyone can check (rules 8 and 10); the two signed records are "evidence on equal footing" (rule 11). Part 3 has no such column.
- The receipt with a **forward** (field 7) is one signed act that both ends a hop and states the holder's debt to the next payee. Part 1's rows marked "Ended + Owed" are that act.

### What the smallest set needs that draft 14 does not have

One line each (traced to the draft's text; "does not have" means I found no act, field or rule for it in Money draft 14, and I did not read Agreements).

1. **Passed.** No act hands an obligation to a new creditor. An obligation names its creditor once, as "an identity". One of an obligation's seven states is "redirected", which the draft says depends on Agreements; what it does I did not read.
2. **A debt owed to "whoever holds this".** The creditor field is one identity. The voucher of 1.9 has no form in Money draft 14.
3. **Owed, said by the one who is owed.** There is no act for it. The draft's requests are the price tag, which is an offer to anyone, and the rail's own request for payment, which is outside the MIR. An obligation signed by its creditor is listed among the freeze scenarios as invalid in every client.
4. **Ended by the creditor's word for something handed over off any rail** (coin, a loaf, work). Discharge is by valid routes on a rail. The only ending on the creditor's word alone is the release, whose own definition is "without full payment". So "paid, off the rails" and "let go" are one act.
5. **Ended in part with nothing paid.** A release "ends the whole obligation"; the draft's way to forgive part is a new obligation for the rest and a release of the old one, which is a tied pair of the kind Part 2 describes.
6. **Ended against another debt.** Nothing names set-off. Two releases that each list the other obligation in field 1 would say it, but that field is "a record only, never checked", and each release ends its obligation whole.
7. **A debt said to be owed again** after its ending was answered. Only the debtor signs an obligation, so a creditor cannot say it. In draft 14 a debt's state is worked out from the proofs by any reader, not said by the creditor.
8. **Which turn of an agreement an ending closes** (March's rent). Field 5 names one act. Whether an agreement's turns can be told apart there I cannot tell without Agreements.

## What reading Money changed

I did not edit Parts 1 to 4. These are the changes I would make to them, each with its reason.

1. **Part 3, the table: a fifth column, "nobody's word: a proof anyone can check".** I put a rail's proof under "a third party who holds or carries". Draft 14 keeps two things apart that I merged: a proof any reader can run (rule 8), and a named trusted party's signed confirmation (rule 9). Under the first, a payment counts on no one's say-so.
2. **Part 3, the line "a statement counts by itself when signed by the party who loses by it": I would narrow it.** It matches draft 14 for Owed (only the debtor signs an obligation) and for the release (only the creditor).
