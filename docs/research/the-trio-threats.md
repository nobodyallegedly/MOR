**What the trio is** (the MOR lead's wording, not his): three signed statements about a debt, each counting by itself when the party who loses by it signs. Owed, signed by the debtor. Ended, signed by the creditor. Passed, signed by the creditor who leaves. A debt states its owner through its own chain of passes, as an envelope does. MOR shows forks and keeps the trail; settlement and its safety are outside, in the rail, the cMIP and the module.

**The frame under test** is his: "a person trying to redeem a debt twice gets stopped by the bank, not by MOR" and "We can see forks inside MOR, but the true settlement safety is brought by the rail + cMIP + module combo". The assignment turned it into a claim to try to break: every threat the trio adds is one of three kinds. One, a lie the record makes provable, which the settler then refuses. Two, a risk the world already had with circulating debt. Three, something a cMIP, a module, a place, a rail or a price can stop, chosen by those who deal. That wording of the claim is the assignment's, not his.

## Plain words first

**The top threats, in ten lines.** The order is by my scores (see "The ranking"); the scores are my reading.

1. **The trail itself.** To check who owns a passed debt, a holder needs its chain of passes. So every later holder, and the issuer at the end, learns every earlier holder. It grows with each pass and cannot be un-shown (5.1; inferred, 60 to 78).
2. **A debtor's debts reach people the debtor never chose.** Passed needs no signature from the debtor. Whoever is offered the debt reads it in order to price it (5.2).
3. **One innocent person must lose, and the record cannot say which.** A debtor forced or tricked into signing, whose debt is passed to an honest holder (2.6). An owner whose key was stolen, and the honest person who later holds the stolen debt (4.3). The world before MOR had both, and settled each by a rule about who loses.
4. **Passes that look like value.** Debts signed between friends, or between names one person holds, passed round or cleared against each other (3.2, 3.5). Every statement in them is true. Unlike a payment in Money draft 14, none of them needs a rail's proof, so they cost nothing to make. They can feed any price read from signed trades.
5. **Dominoes** (3.1): holders who counted an unsettled debt as theirs fail when its issuer does.
6. **One debt passed twice** (1.1). The two passes are a provable lie, but only to someone who holds both. Each branch sees a clean chain. In the worked case, of ten people only the liar holds both passes until the second branch reaches the issuer (counted by hand).
7. **The backlog reopens** (4.2). In draft 14 a thief with the signing key cannot collect debts owed to the owner. With Passed, the thief can pass them away (traced).
8. **A pass disowned by a change of key** (4.4). A holder passes a debt, then changes keys and disowns the pass. The record then shows a dispute, not a lie (traced to Identity rule 34).
9. **The double-passer keeps the say** (1.5). Under both fork rules I read, the one who forked is favoured: by Money draft 14's pattern the debt stays with her; by draft 25's she alone can sign which branch goes on (inferred, 50 to 70; neither rule was written for debts).
10. **Whatever stops the forks becomes a watcher and a gate** (5.3, 6.1 to 6.3): a place that orders every pass, a clearing service that sees every ring, a settler everyone must use.

**What is new.** Of 31 threats (counted):

- **2 are new against both baselines**: the full trail travelling with small everyday claims (5.1), and the fork rules favouring the forker (1.5). Both are inferred, and the second rests on rules not written for debts.
- **26 are new against Money draft 14 and old in the world.** All 26 follow from one thing: debts that pass. Of these, the record shows more than the world's paper did in 12 (a provable lie or a visible pattern), and shows nothing new, or is itself the harm, in 14.
- **3 are old in both.**

**What only the core could stop.** By my scores, nothing: no threat scored "nothing outside the core stops it" (counted: 0 of 31). How sure: 55 to 75. Under that answer sit four facts about what the core's own readings fix, which nothing built on top can change (see "What only the core could stop"). The plainest: the thing that most often stops a threat before settlement is a place (5 threats, counted), and no lower text yet defines one (traced to draft 25, section 8.1).

**Whether the frame held.** Its conclusion, that nothing the trio adds needs a guarantee on every act, held in my reading. Its account of where safety comes from held only at the counter. It bends in four places.

- **Debts never settled have no settler.** His words allow a debt that is never redeemed. Then no bank stops anything, and a double pass can circulate for good (1.1).
- **The settler is itself fooled** when it has not seen the latest pass (1.3).
- **Harm that is not about being paid twice** is done before settlement and no settler undoes it: what the trail shows (5.1, 5.2), a debt bought for the hold it gives (6.5), prices fed with passes that cost nothing (3.5).
- **"A provable lie" is weaker than it sounds.** It is provable only to a holder of both branches. An honest slip looks the same (draft 25's own stated cost). And a change of key turns it into a dispute (4.4).

The record shows nothing at all in 12 of 31 threats (counted). For each of those something outside the core could warn or price it, except that nothing anywhere detects a signature given under pressure (2.6).

**On his doubt**, "Maybe the hill is better left alone…": six findings bear on it, some each way. They are listed at the end and not weighed.

## The seven families

Each threat: a short story, then lines a to f. In line d, "a cMIP could carry it" or "a module could carry it" says only that such a thing could, and what it would have to know.

### Family 1. Double use inside MOR, before settlement

#### 1.1 One debt passed twice

Dan owes Ana a voucher worth 100. Ana passes it to Ben for 100 of flour. The same day she passes it to Carla for 100 of eggs. Ben and Carla each hold a clean chain and each pass it on. The full walk is under "The worked fork".

- **a.** Ana does it and gains a second price. The last holder of whichever branch reaches Dan second loses. That holder chose to hold a circulating debt and could not see the other branch.
- **b.** Before, for as long as nobody settles. Found at settlement. Never found if never settled.
- **c.** A provable lie: Ana's two Passed, each naming the same Owed as the act it follows. By Envelopes rule 15: "Two valid acts naming the same predecessor in the same chain are a fork, provable from their signatures and insides." It is provable only by someone who holds both. Acts of this kind are "private by default, visible to their parties" in Money draft 14 (Part three), so each branch holds one (traced). It is a fork in the debt's chain, not necessarily in Ana's own line: "Parallel devices keep separate sequences" (Envelopes, section 4), and two passes signed one after the other in one sequence are no fork of that sequence at all (traced).
- **d.** A place, where the debt names one: an act counts "only once the place shows it, and at the position the place gives it" (draft 25, G7.2). Draft 25 states the cost of having none: "Without a place, no scarcity and no freshness. The same thing can be given twice in two branches". The issuer at the counter stops the second redemption and nothing earlier. The market's price can discount debts that name no place.
- **e.** New against A. Old in B for debts on a book; in B a paper note could not be handed to two people.
- **f.** The same receivable assigned twice. One long-standing rule gave priority to whoever first told the debtor, not whoever was assigned first; a judge of its highest court said it had done at least as much injustice as it prevented, and another country's highest court refused it (sourced: [Dearle]). The same cargo financed with several banks: one trader's failure in 2020 left letters of credit of about 1.5 billion dollars on cargo that did not exist or was pledged more than once; it ended in insolvency and court, and banks looked for a shared record of financed documents (sourced: [HinLeong]).

#### 1.2 One debt pledged twice

Ana holds Dan→Ana 100. She borrows 80 from Ben and gives the debt as security. She borrows 80 from Carla on the same security. A pledge passes nothing; it is a promise in an agreement.

- **a.** Ana gains a second loan. Whichever lender comes second when she fails loses. Each chose to lend on security and could not see the other pledge.
- **b.** Before; found when Ana fails or a lender calls the security.
- **c.** Nothing in the debt's own chain: no Passed was signed. Two agreements each name the debt. Draft 25's stated cost: "The same thing sold in two separate agreements is seen by neither agreement's rules". A visible pattern only to someone who holds both agreements.
- **d.** A place that both lenders read. A cMIP for pledges could carry it; it would have to know every pledge of that debt.
- **e.** New against A (in A a debt is not a thing its creditor can hand over). Old in B.
- **f.** A metals trader at one port pledged the same stock three times over, by duplicated warehouse papers, for loans of more than 2.7 billion yuan; it ended in a criminal probe and a prison term, and lenders moved metal to warehouses they trusted more (sourced: [Qingdao]). The salad oil receipts of 1963: receipts for oil that was not in the tanks; it ended at a collapse (from the earlier file [Liar], its [SALAD]).

#### 1.3 Passed and redeemed

Two orders. **First:** Ana passes the voucher to Ben, then walks into Dan's bakery. Dan has not seen the pass. He hands her the bread and she signs Ended. **Second:** Ana takes the bread, signs nothing, and then passes the voucher to Ben.

- **a.** Ana gains bread and a price. In the first order Dan loses if he must honour Ben too; Dan is a bystander to the pass, which did not need his signature. In the second, Ben and Dan each hold half an accusation.
- **b.** At settlement.
- **c.** First order: a provable lie once both are held. Ana's Passed and Ana's Ended follow the same act in the debt's chain. Second order: nothing at all. The record shows an open debt with a clean chain, because Ended is the creditor's to sign and she did not sign it.
- **d.** The issuer at the counter: Dan can ask for the Ended before the bread, and can look for a later pass, if he can see one. A place gives him "nothing later". In draft 14 this cannot arise on a rail: a debt is discharged "when valid routes ending at the creditor's payee pointer, each naming the obligation, sum to the owed amount" (rule 15), on a proof anyone can check and on nobody's word.
- **e.** New against A. Old in B, where the law met it with a rule: a debtor who pays the old creditor before hearing of the assignment is free (from general knowledge, as "What a debt record says" has it). A paper voucher is handed in when redeemed; a signed record is not.
- **f.** The notice rule above ([Dearle] is its other face). Paper surrendered at the counter (from general knowledge).

#### 1.4 Counted in two clearings

Ana holds Dan→Ana 100 and owes 100 to Ben and 100 to Carla. Two clearing circles each look for debts to set against each other. Ana puts the same claim on Dan into both.

- **a.** Ana gains two of her debts cleared with one claim. The members of the second circle lose; they chose clearing and could not see the other circle.
- **b.** Before, or never: clearing is how a circle avoids settling.
- **c.** A provable lie for whoever holds both clearings: two Ended by Ana on one debt (or two Passed). Each circle alone sees one.
- **d.** A place, or one clearing service that sees both circles (which is threat 6.2). A cMIP for clearing could carry it; it would have to know of every other clearing that debt may enter.
- **e.** New against A. Old in B as one invoice sold to two factors (from general knowledge).
- **f.** I found no named case of one claim entered in two clearing circles. The nearest are the double financings of 1.1 and 1.2. Absence in a search of this length is not proof.

#### 1.5 The double-passer keeps the say

After 1.1, Ben's branch and Carla's branch both stand. What happens next depends on a fork rule, and no text has one for a debt's chain. Envelopes rule 15 says: "Whether a fork is normal or a conflict is defined by the MIR that defines the object." I read the two fork rules that exist.

- By Money draft 14's pattern for its own chains: "Two pointers naming the same predecessor are contested: clients use the last pointer before the fork" (rule 1). Applied to a debt's chain, the last act before the fork is the Owed, and its creditor is Ana.
- By draft 25's: "While a fork stands, neither tip counts as the next act of its line, and nothing built on either tip counts" (G12.2), and the fork is resolved by an update "signed as an update of the shared act must be: for one signer's line, by that signer" (G12.3). The signer of both passes is Ana.

Either way Ana, who lied, is the one the rule leaves holding the debt or the choice between Ben and Carla.

- **a.** Ana gains a hold over both branches. Every holder on both loses the use of the debt while the fork stands. Dan may gain: he owes nobody he can name.
- **b.** Before and at.
- **c.** A provable lie, the same fork.
- **d.** A cMIP could carry it; it would have to know who may say which branch goes on, in advance. Draft 25 has such a thing for agreements: "Terms MAY give, in advance, a power (G5) to sign the resolving update of a fork" (G12.4). A place's order could also answer it.
- **e.** New against both (inferred, 50 to 70, from the two rules quoted; neither was written for a debt, and a rule for debts may read differently). In B, between two buyers of one debt, a rule of law picks the winner, never the seller.
- **f.** None found with this shape. Nearest: the complaint against the notice rule in [Dearle].

### Family 2. Issuers

#### 2.1 Over-issuing

Dan's bakery can bake 200 loaves a week. He signs vouchers for 2,000 and spends them. They pass from hand to hand at face value until the queue at his door is longer than his oven.

- **a.** Dan gains goods now. The holders at the end lose. They chose an issuer's promise; they could not see how many he made.
- **b.** Before and never; found at settlement.
- **c.** A visible pattern, to whoever can add up Dan's Owed. In draft 14's format they are private to their parties, so by default nobody but Dan can.
- **d.** The market's price, where trades are seen: "the eggs from coop and the eggs from storby could end up being priced differently by the market…". A cMIP could carry a cap; it would have to know the issuer's total, so every Owed of his.
- **e.** New against A as circulating debt. Old in B.
- **f.** American banknotes before 1863: printed discounts mostly tracked a bank's soundness, and failures still came (from the earlier file [Issuer], case 1). Argentina's barter clubs: their paper credits were over-issued and forged, and the networks collapsed in 2002 and 2003 (sourced: [RGT]). Each level can make more of its own promises and never of the level above (from the addendum: Mehrling).

#### 2.2 Many cheap identities

Dan issues as "Storby Bakery". When that name's vouchers trade at half, he issues as "Mill Lane Bread", a new identity with no history.

- **a.** Dan gains a clean name each time. Holders of the old name's vouchers lose; so do honest new issuers, who are now distrusted.
- **b.** Before and never.
- **c.** Nothing. Two identities with nothing to tie them.
- **d.** The market's price on newcomers, which also falls on honest ones. A cMIP or a module could carry a tie between a name and something costly; it would have to know something outside the record.
- **e.** New against A as circulating debt. Old in B, and cheaper here: a new name costs one act.
- **f.** "A cheap new name undid reputation everywhere it was available", and the answers on record were all outside the record: closed membership, a licence, an entry fee, distrust of every newcomer (from the earlier file [Liar], item 9). New banks' notes were discounted until they had a record of paying (from [Issuer], case 1).

#### 2.3 An issuer that vanishes

Dan closes the bakery, or dies, or loses his keys. His vouchers are in forty hands.

- **a.** Nobody need gain. The holders lose. They chose his promise.
- **b.** Never settled; found at the first attempt to settle.
- **c.** Nothing. A valid Owed and silence. The record cannot tell an issuer who left from one who lost a key. "Proving something did not happen matters only when there are accusations."
- **d.** The market's price, before the fact. A cMIP could carry a guarantor or a stake; it would have to know who stands behind the issuer. After the fact, nothing.
- **e.** New against A as circulating debt. Old in B.
- **f.** Members of local exchange circles who left owing: no circle found a sure way to stop it, and members in credit grew tired and left too (sourced: [LETS]). Failed banks' notes (from [Issuer]).

#### 2.4 A promise nobody can redeem

Dan's voucher says "one loaf". It does not say which loaf, where, by when, or what happens if the price of flour triples. Or it says "one hour of my work" and Dan's diary is full for three years.

- **a.** Dan gains value for a promise he can always answer "not today". Holders lose. They chose it, and the words were in front of them.
- **b.** At settlement, or never.
- **c.** Nothing: a valid Owed in a unit. The unit is "a small specification of its own" (draft 14, words); what a loaf is, and when the debt falls due, are not the record's.
- **d.** The market's price. A cMIP could carry the terms of redemption; it would have to know what the unit is and when the debt is due.
- **e.** New against A as circulating debt. Old in B.
- **f.** Company scrip: spendable only at the company's shop at the company's prices, worth less everywhere else; ended by statutes against paying wages that way (sourced: [Truck]). An instrument is a credit because it can be handed back to its issuer (from the addendum: Innes); a voucher the issuer will not take back is not one.

#### 2.5 A debt written against the wrong debtor

Ana signs "Ben owes Ana 100". Ben never signed anything.

- **a.** Ana would gain a debt to sell. Ben would lose, as a pure bystander.
- **b.** Before.
- **c.** A statement that is invalid on its face: it names Ben as debtor and Ben did not sign it.
- **d.** The core. Money draft 14: "An obligation is signed by the debtor. A creditor's statement of what it is owed is a claim, never an obligation." Its freeze scenarios list "an obligation signed by its creditor" as "invalid in every client".
- **e.** Old in both, and closed in A already.
- **f.** Forged notes of hand (from general knowledge).

**Does "Owed counts when the debtor signs" close it?** Yes, for a debt forged over someone's name: plainly closed (traced, above). Three things remain, and none is a forgery:

- a debtor **tricked or forced** into signing (2.6);
- a **key that is not the person's**: a stolen key signs a true-looking Owed in the owner's name (4.2, 4.3);
- a **debtor that is an agreement**, signed for by someone without the power (2.7).

#### 2.6 A debtor tricked or forced, the debt passed on

Ana sells Ben a roof repair she never does, and has him sign Ben→Ana 500 on the day. Or she stands over him while he signs. Within the hour she passes the debt to Carla, who pays 450 for it and knows nothing.

- **a.** Ana gains 450. Then either Ben pays for nothing, or Carla holds nothing. One of two people loses, and neither chose it: Ben is a victim, Carla an honest buyer.
- **b.** Before; fought out at settlement.
- **c.** Nothing at all. Ben's signature is valid. The record does not hold the roof, or the hand on his shoulder.
- **d.** Nothing detects it. A cMIP could carry which of the two bears the loss; it would have to know what a debtor may say against a later holder. "What a debt record says" lists exactly this as managing: "whether what the debtor could say against the old creditor can be said against the new".
- **e.** New against A: in A the creditor is fixed, so Ben faces Ana, who knows what she did. Old in B.
- **f.** The honest later holder of a bill takes it free of earlier parties' defects of title and of defences they had between themselves (sourced: [BEA], sections 29 and 38). That rule made bills pass like money. It also let sellers have buyers sign notes and sell them on at once, and it ended, for consumers, with laws that took the protection away in consumer credit (sourced for the exclusion: [BEA], the annotation to section 29; the history from general knowledge).

#### 2.7 A debtor that is an agreement, signed for without the power

The bakery is a collective of five. Dan, one member, signs "the bakery owes Ana 1,000" and Ana passes it on. The collective's rule needed three signatures.

- **a.** Dan and Ana gain. The other members lose, or the last holder does. The members are bystanders to Dan's act.
- **b.** Before; found at settlement.
- **c.** A visible pattern, to a reader who holds the collective's rules. Draft 14, rule 7: a collective's Money act is "signed by a member or a grantee for it… and judged by Agreements", and "A Money-only client shows the standing of every such act unknown". Each later holder must make that judgment or take it on trust.
- **d.** A cMIP, or Agreements' own reading; it would have to know the agreement's rules at the act the Owed cites.
- **e.** New against A as a debt that passes; the rule that judges it is in A.
- **f.** A firm's agent signing beyond his authority; the law often held the firm to it where the outsider could not have known (from general knowledge).

### Family 3. Chains of unsettled debt

#### 3.1 Dominoes

Ben holds 1,000 of Dan's vouchers and counts them as his own means. On their strength he signs 800 of his own to Carla, who does the same to Ana. Dan's oven burns down.

- **a.** Nobody gains. Every holder down the line loses, and so do people who dealt with Ben and Carla and never held a voucher.
- **b.** Never settled, until one link is asked to settle.
- **c.** A visible pattern, to someone who holds the whole chain. No one holder does.
- **d.** The market's price on each link. A cMIP could carry limits; it would have to know what each holder owes and is owed.
- **e.** New against A in length: A has chains (a refund "owed to the last hop's payer, who owes it on to the hop before", rule 28) but each debt has a fixed creditor. Old in B.
- **f.** In calm times the levels of money flatten; under strain they pull apart, and whoever defends a fixed price between two levels is the one exposed (from the addendum: Mehrling). The 1772 crisis around one large Scottish bank (sourced: [Smith]). Against the threat: between firms that deal for years, orders were cancelled freely and disputes settled without the contract (from the addendum: Macaulay), so a chain among people who know each other may bend where a chain among strangers breaks.

#### 3.2 Kiting

Ana and Ben sign debts to each other for 1,000, with nothing behind them. Each passes the other's debt to a third person as payment. When one falls due they sign two more.

- **a.** Ana and Ben gain goods now. The third people lose; they chose to take a debt and saw two real signatures.
- **b.** Before and never.
- **c.** A visible pattern (a pair, or a ring, renewing), to someone who sees enough of both lines. Each Owed is true: the debtor signed it. A long trail of passes between names one person holds also makes a debt look well accepted.
- **d.** The market's price. A module could carry the search for the pattern; it would have to know many people's debts.
- **e.** New against A. Old in B, and cheaper here: in B each name on a bill was a person who could be pursued.
- **f.** Bills drawn and redrawn on each other with no goods behind them, which Adam Smith described and tied to that bank's ruin (sourced: [Smith]). A brokerage that covered overdrafts with fresh cheques between its accounts, a day or two apart: a small bank noticed, and it ended in a guilty plea to 2,000 counts (sourced: [Hutton]).

#### 3.3 Bundling and selling on

Carla collects 500 small debts from Ben's customers and signs one new debt of her own against the bundle: "Carla owes the holder 40,000". That note passes on. Its holders see Carla's promise and not the 500 debts.

- **a.** Carla gains a price that the 500 debts, seen one by one, might not fetch. Later holders lose if the bundle is worse than said. They chose it.
- **b.** Before and never.
- **c.** A visible pattern in layers: each layer is true and signed. The link from the note to the bundle is only as good as what Carla says of it. A later holder can read through to the 500 only along a path their debtors signed for: "Data needs to move along paths that people agree, with signature." (draft 25, section 8.1). So the rule that keeps a debtor's affairs private also keeps a bundle's buyer from looking inside (inferred from G11.6(a), 55 to 72).
- **d.** A cMIP could carry it; it would have to know the debts in the bundle, or name someone who does.
- **e.** New against A. Old in B.
- **f.** One agency gave its top grade to nearly 45,000 mortgage securities in 2000 to 2007 and most of the 2006 crop was later marked down (from [Issuer], item 5). Afterwards, in many foreclosures, who held the debt could not be shown from the papers, and documents were signed in bulk by people who had checked nothing (sourced: [MERS]).

#### 3.4 Pledged onward by each holder in turn

Ana pledges Dan's debt to Ben for a loan. Ben pledges his claim on Ana, with its security, to Carla. Carla does the same. One debt of 100 now stands behind 240 of loans.

- **a.** Each borrower gains a loan. If one link fails, those further along lose a security they thought was theirs.
- **b.** Before and never.
- **c.** Nothing in the debt's chain: no Passed. A pattern only to someone who holds every pledge.
- **d.** A place that every lender reads. A cMIP could carry a limit; it would have to know every pledge down the chain.
- **e.** New against A. Old in B.
- **f.** When a large broker failed in 2008, about 22 billion dollars of its clients' assets, of 40 billion frozen, had been re-used; clients waited years, and re-use fell sharply afterwards because clients feared it (sourced: [LBIE], [Singh-Aitken]).

#### 3.5 A ring that clears debts that were never real

Ana runs three identities. Each signs a debt to the next, in a ring. A clearing ends all three against each other. Nothing was ever owed. The record now holds three debts opened and three ended in good order.

- **a.** Ana gains a history of debts honoured, and trades that any price read "from signed trades" will count. Everyone who reads that history or that price loses: bystanders.
- **b.** Before and never. Nothing is ever settled, by design.
- **c.** A visible pattern at most. Every statement is true: each Owed is signed by its debtor, each Ended by its creditor.
- **d.** A module could carry the search; it would have to know which identities belong together, which the record does not hold. In draft 14 a payment costs real money on a rail: a receipt or claim counts only when its rail's "verification answer is valid" (rule 10). An Owed and an Ended need no rail.
- **e.** New against A, for that reason. Old in B.
- **f.** Sales for one cent to buy feedback; trades of an exchange with itself, by one estimate most of the volume on unregulated exchanges; what caught them was a second measurement by someone the pair did not choose, or a leaked record naming both sides (from [Liar], section 5). On a direct-debit reversal the payer's bank pays back with no duty to check that the first debit happened (from the addendum): a statement can point at a thing nobody checks.

### Family 4. Keys

#### 4.1 A bearer's key stolen

Dan's voucher is owed "to whoever holds this", and holding means holding one key, with no identity behind it. Ben keeps the key on his phone. The phone is stolen.

- **a.** The thief gains the voucher. Ben loses it. He chose to hold a bearer's key.
- **b.** Before; complete once the thief passes or redeems.
- **c.** Nothing. The thief's signature is the holder's signature.
- **d.** A module that keeps keys for people could carry it, and would then be a keeper (6.1). The issuer at the counter cannot tell.
- **e.** New against A: there, in the words of "What a debt record says" (Part 5), "The creditor field is one identity". Old in B: cash.
- **f.** A stolen banknote paid to an innkeeper: the honest innkeeper kept it and the bank had to pay him; the man it was stolen from lost (sourced: [Miller], 1758). That rule is why notes pass as money.

#### 4.2 A signing key stolen, held debts passed away

Ben holds, under his identity, debts from Dan and nine others. A thief takes his phone, and with it his signing key. Before Ben changes his keys, the thief signs ten Passed to an accomplice.

- **a.** The thief gains every debt Ben held. Ben loses them, or the later holders do (4.3).
- **b.** Before.
- **c.** Nothing until Ben's change of keys. Then Identity rule 34 reads the thief's acts: an act outside the kept ancestry, or disowned, "is void, unless" (among other things) "another identity acknowledged it"; "then it is valid and shown as disputed".
- **d.** A cMIP could carry what a pass needs beyond the signing key; it would have to know the holder's safer key or a second signer. An anchoring, or a place, can show which side of the key change a pass fell on.
- **e.** New against A, and against something A closed on purpose. Draft 14's reasoning names "the backlog, closed after the lock change because the version that counts for a debt is the latest the payee's own acts on a kept line hold, and only the debtor signs a debt", and its freeze scenarios hold "a thief changing the flow pointer cannot collect obligations that name an earlier version, nor re-issue them". With Passed, the creditor's signature moves a debt, and the signing key signs it (traced).
- **f.** A stolen chequebook; stolen bearer bonds (from general knowledge).

**How the existing theft rule reaches a debt that has been passed, in the texts' own words.** It does not reach it by its words (traced).

- Money draft 14, rule 29, protects a payer: "a payer who followed the payee's pointer and vault as uploaded is protected", and "Until the change is anchored, the owner bears the loss." Its closing line: "The loss from a theft window falls on the owner until the lock change is anchored, never on a payer whose payment the payee's receipt or an earlier anchor shows." Every clause is about a payment and the pointer it followed. A person who took a pass is not a payer, and a pass follows no pointer.
- The vault protects what comes in: it is "changed only with the chain key" and "a thief with the signing key cannot open a unit the vault does not cover". It says nothing about what the owner holds going out.
- Identity rule 34 does reach a thief's Passed, as it reaches any act signed with the replaced key: void, or valid and "shown as disputed" if "another identity acknowledged it". Whether a later holder's own pass counts as that acknowledgement, and who bears the loss when the status is "disputed", no text I read says. Rule 28 warns in general: "Anyone who accepts an act under a pending key does so at their own risk".
- Envelopes, section 13, on the same window for deliveries: "The rotation voids the thief's act."
- One sentence I met by a search and not by reading its rule: Identity's rule 29, on a stolen chain key and the escape, states as a cost that "an owner robbed of both keys cannot, by escape, undo a sale or an ending the thief completed with a buyer in good faith: as for a stolen chain key, that is the owner's loss". That is about both keys and about acts a chain signature placed; I did not read what it rests on, and it is not a rule about a pass signed with the signing key alone.

#### 4.3 The honest later holder of a stolen debt

After 4.2 the accomplice passes Dan's debt to Carla for 90. Carla is honest. Ben changes his keys the next morning and disowns the thief's pass.

- **a.** The thief gains 90. Ben or Carla loses 100. Neither chose it: Ben was robbed, Carla bought a clean chain.
- **b.** Before; fought out at settlement or after.
- **c.** Nothing at the time Carla buys. Afterwards, a dispute at Ben's change of keys: the thief's pass is void, or "valid and shown as disputed".
- **d.** A cMIP could carry which of the two bears it; it would have to know the order of Carla's taking and Ben's key change, so an anchoring or a place. The market's price can discount debts with young passes.
- **e.** New against A. Old in B.
- **f.** The world had two opposite rules and used both. For paper passed by hand, the honest holder won ([Miller]). For paper passed by signature, a thief could not give a good title, and the honest holder lost (sourced: an 1831 report quoted beside [Miller]; the statute's rule on forged signatures from general knowledge). A stolen key's signature is genuine in the bytes and not the owner's in fact, so it sits between the two (inferred, 60 to 78).

#### 4.4 A pass disowned by a change of key

Ana passes Dan's debt to Ben for 100 of flour. A week later she changes her keys and lists that pass as disowned: "my phone was taken". She passes the debt again, under her new key, to Carla.

- **a.** Ana gains two prices. Ben loses, or Carla does. Neither chose it.
- **b.** Before.
- **c.** Not a provable lie. A visible pattern: a disowned act that someone relied on. The record cannot tell this from a real theft. Money draft 14 states the same cost for payments: "the owner, by a lock change after receiving payments, can put in doubt every payment it affects whose receipt the rotation did not keep (disowning its own receipt included) and whose claim is not anchored by the point".
- **d.** A place, or an anchoring of Ben's taking before the key change: in draft 14 "a client that anchors promptly defeats it". Identity rule 34 keeps the pass "valid and shown as disputed" if Ben, with his own signing key, acknowledged it.
- **e.** New against A for a pass; A states it for payments. Old in B: "that is not my signature".
- **f.** A payer's word alone, inside eight weeks, undoes a direct debit with no reason given (from the addendum). A stopped cheque (from general knowledge).

#### 4.5 A key lost

Carla holds Dan's debt and loses every key she has. Or she holds it under a bearer's key and loses that.

- **a.** Dan gains if the debt can never be claimed. Carla loses. If Dan wants to pay and be free, he also loses: only the creditor signs Ended.
- **b.** At settlement, or never.
- **c.** Nothing. An open debt and silence.
- **d.** A cMIP could carry an end by time; it would have to know a clock, and "time running out" is the agreement's, not the record's ("What a debt record says", Part 4). Identity's recovery by the chain key covers an identity, not a bare key.
- **e.** New against A in the debtor's half: in A a debt is discharged by a rail's proof and "the claim alone shows the money arrived" (rule 11) with no word from the creditor. Old in B: lost paper.
- **f.** Lost bearer paper; unclaimed balances, which many places hand to the state after a time (from general knowledge).

### Family 5. Privacy

#### 5.1 A trail that shows every holder

Dan's voucher goes Ana, Ben, Eva, Finn, Gil. To check that Finn owns it, Gil's client needs the Owed and all four passes. Gil now knows that Ana paid Ben, Ben paid Eva and Eva paid Finn, and roughly when. Dan learns the same when Gil redeems.

- **a.** Nobody need do anything wrong. Each later holder, and the issuer, learns. Each earlier holder is seen by people they never dealt with.
- **b.** Before, at every pass; again at settlement; and for good after.
- **c.** The record is the harm. What a chain of passes reveals, and to whom:
  - **each later holder**: every earlier holder, the order, and who dealt with whom;
  - **the issuer**, at settlement: the whole path of its own voucher;
  - **anyone offered the debt** and anyone a holder forwards it to: the same;
  - **anyone asked to judge a fork**: both branches;
  - **a relay**: not the content of a private act (5.3);
  - **a stranger**: nothing, unless the acts are public, or one holder publishes them.
  What cannot be un-shown: every signed act already in another person's hands. Nothing in MOR is deleted, and a signature proves the act to whoever is shown it.
- **d.** Can the data socket contain it, by its text? Not by its text. K8 asks one question: "May this reader read this report, or what it states, beyond its own leg?", and "The socket speaks for the people bound by the Terms that name it (G5.1), and for no one else." It governs reports of legs under Terms. A pass is not a report, and the later holder must hold the chain to check the owner at all. G11.6(c) says: "No other layer of MOR is to carry a report further than its leg, or to give such a path". A chain of passes carries each holder's dealing to every later holder; whether that is a path in G11.6's sense is not something the text decides (inferred, 55 to 72). A module that vouches for the chain so that holders need not see it could carry it, and would then see everything itself (6.1, 6.3).
- **e.** New against both (inferred, 55 to 75). Against A: draft 14 keeps these acts "visible to their parties" and discloses a route only "to the next payee and to the final creditor" (rule 26). Against B: coins, notes and paper vouchers carried no list of holders. Old in B only for endorsed bills and cheques, which list every signer on the back.
- **f.** The endorsed bill of exchange: a merchants' instrument, where being seen on the back was part of its strength, since each signer stood behind it (from general knowledge; [BEA]). A shared ledger of credit lines, readable by all, on which outside researchers mapped every wallet's exposure (sourced: [Moreno-Sanchez]).

#### 5.2 A debtor's debts shown to people the debtor never chose

Ben owes Ana 500 for a medical bill. Ana wants cash and offers the debt to Carla, then to three others, each of whom reads it to price it. Ben signed for Ana to know. He did not sign for the other four.

- **a.** Ana, lawfully, to sell. The four learn. Ben loses his privacy; he chose the debt, not its readers.
- **b.** Before.
- **c.** The record is the harm. Who learns another person's debts: every holder, and everyone to whom a holder offers it.
- **d.** A cMIP could carry it; it would have to know whether the debtor agreed that the debt may pass, and to whom. "What a debt record says" lists that as managing: "whether a given debt may be handed on at all; whether the debtor must be told". The assignment's own wording of Passed asks nothing of the debtor.
- **e.** New against A, where the creditor is fixed. Old in B.
- **f.** Debts sold to collectors, and the rules many places then made about who may be told of a person's debt (from general knowledge).

#### 5.3 Those who carry and order the acts

Every pass travels through relays to reach the new holder. If the debt names a place, every pass is shown to the place.

- **a.** A relay, a home or a place, by doing its job. It learns who sends to whom, how often, and for a place the order of every pass. The holders are seen.
- **b.** Before, always.
- **c.** The record is the harm, at one remove. A relay "cannot, and need not, read a private act" (Envelopes rule 27); that it sees who delivers to whom is my inference from its job (70 to 85). A place gives "one line of the acts the place was shown, in the order it was shown them" (draft 25, section 8.1), so it is shown them.
- **d.** Nothing stops a carrier from seeing what it carries. The market can offer several.
- **e.** Old in both: relays carry private acts in A, and banks see payments in B. Larger here, because a debt that passes makes more acts between more people.
- **f.** Clearing banks that learned every issuer's business and kept it in their own books (from [Issuer], item 3).

### Family 6. Power

#### 6.1 A settler as the gate

The whole voucher circle of the town redeems through one module that checks chains and one bank that pays. The bank decides whose vouchers it will take.

- **a.** The settler gains a fee, a view, and a veto. An issuer or holder it refuses loses, whatever their record shows.
- **b.** At settlement.
- **c.** Nothing: a refusal leaves no act.
- **d.** The market: another settler, where one exists. A named party is at least named: in draft 14 "The verification answer MUST name that party" (rule 9).
- **e.** Old in both. A already rests fiat rails on "a named trusted party". B has banks.
- **f.** One Boston bank made every New England bank keep a deposit with it and sent home the notes of any that would not (from [Issuer], case 2). In 1907 one member bank said it would no longer clear for a large trust company, and the run began the next day (sourced: [Knick]).

#### 6.2 A clearing service that sees every ring

To find rings of debts that cancel, a clearing service must be shown everyone's debts. The more it sees, the more it clears.

- **a.** The service gains the map of who owes whom. Members are seen; they chose to join.
- **b.** Before and never.
- **c.** The record is the harm: what members hand over to be cleared.
- **d.** A module could carry clearing with less shown; it would have to know enough debts to find a ring, which is the same thing.
- **e.** New against A, which names no set-off ("What a debt record says", Part 5, item 6). Old in B.
- **f.** Mutual-credit circles run by one operator that holds every balance; one such operator banned trade in its credits when a market price for them appeared (from [Issuer], item 4). His own words mark the difference he sees: "It's also sardex in a way… without central ledger".

#### 6.3 A place that decides every fork

Dan's vouchers name a place. When Ana passes one twice, the place's order says which pass came first. The place's operator is Ana's cousin.

- **a.** A place gains the power to order, delay or leave out. Whoever it places second loses.
- **b.** Before and at.
- **c.** A provable lie only if the place signs two answers that contradict: then "it has no answer from that place: **unchecked**" (G7.2). A pass that the place simply never shows leaves nothing; "nobody can sign "nothing later" as a fact" (section 8.1).
- **d.** A cMIP could carry a choice among places, if a debt may name more than one; draft 25 leaves that open ("P4. One place or several"). The market: another place.
- **e.** New against A, where no place exists. Old in B: registers.
- **f.** A private register of mortgage transfers that most lenders came to use: when the papers were tested in court, in many cases the chain behind the register could not be shown (sourced: [MERS]).

#### 6.4 An issuer whose refusal is final

Gil brings Dan a voucher with a clean chain. Dan says "that one was already redeemed", or "there is a fork, I pay neither", or nothing.

- **a.** Dan keeps the bread. Gil loses. He chose Dan's promise.
- **b.** At settlement.
- **c.** Nothing. An open debt. A refusal is not an act, and Gil cannot prove that something did not happen. Where there is a fork, Dan's refusal is even backed by the record (1.5).
- **d.** The market's price on Dan afterwards. A cMIP could carry a judge or a stake; it would have to know who decides and what Dan put up.
- **e.** New against A where the refusal rests on a fork or an unseen pass. A debtor who simply does not pay is old in both.
- **f.** Banks that made their notes hard to present (from [Issuer], case 1, disputed there). A reversal paid with no check of the first debit (from the addendum): the mirror case, where the one who refuses to check is the one who pays.

#### 6.5 A debt bought for the hold it gives

Ben owes Ana 2,000 and they are on good terms. Carla, who wants Ben's shop, buys the debt from Ana for 2,100. Ben now owes Carla.

- **a.** Carla gains a creditor's hold over Ben. Ben loses the creditor he chose.
- **b.** Before.
- **c.** A visible pattern, to Ben if he can read the pass. Passed "holds" without his word.
- **d.** A cMIP could carry it; it would have to know whether the debtor agreed that the debt may pass (as 5.2).
- **e.** New against A. Old in B.
- **f.** Debts bought by collectors and by rivals (from general knowledge). Workers held through scrip and the company shop (sourced: [Truck]).

### Family 7. Outside the record

**Rules differ by place. This is not a legal reading, and it is no advice.** It lists kinds of rule that exist, for no named country (from general knowledge throughout, with three points sourced). The documents cited as sources each come from some place; they are cited as examples of a kind, not as the law anywhere.

**Kinds of rule.**

- **Who may issue.** Many places reserve to licensed bodies the taking of money from the public against a promise to repay, and the issuing of stored value that is redeemable for money. Some forbid private notes payable to the bearer on demand.
- **What must back it.** Where issuing is allowed, there are often rules that the money received be kept apart from the issuer's own, or matched by safe assets, and that the issuer hold capital (sourced: [EMD]).
- **Who may hold customers' value.** Keeping other people's money or claims for them is often a regulated activity of its own, with rules on keeping it separate.
- **What a prospectus is for.** Offering transferable debt to the public as something to invest in often requires a disclosure document, so that buyers are told what stands behind the promise. A transferable debt of a business may count as a security.
- **What a licence is for.** Operating a place where such claims are exchanged, cleared or transmitted is often licensed separately from issuing them. Duties to identify customers and report suspicious dealings often attach to those who issue, exchange or transfer.
- **How gift cards and vouchers are often treated differently from money.** Commonly, value that can be spent only with its issuer, or within a limited network, or for a limited range of goods, is carved out of the money rules. The carve-out is not automatic, has no single test, and tends to narrow as a voucher is accepted by more parties or can be turned into cash (sourced: [EMD]). Vouchers often have their own rules on expiry and unclaimed balances. When an issuer fails, voucher holders usually rank as ordinary creditors.
- **Wages.** Many places forbid paying wages in an employer's own tokens (sourced: [Truck]).
- **Consumer debt.** Many places limit what a buyer of a consumer's debt may do, keep the consumer's defences alive against the buyer, and regulate collection.
- **Personal data.** A debt is often personal data about the debtor, with rights to have data corrected or erased.
- **Tax on barter.** Commonly, goods exchanged for goods are taxed as if each had been sold at its market value, and organised exchanges may have to report their members' trades (sourced, weak: [Barter-tax]).
- **Tax on debt forgiven.** A debt let go is often treated as income to the debtor or as a gift from the creditor. Sales taxes often treat a voucher for one named good differently from a voucher for many.

#### 7.1 A regulated thing done without knowing

Dan sells vouchers for cash to people who mean to spend them at other shops. Carla runs the place where the town's vouchers are swapped. Ben keeps his neighbours' vouchers on his phone for them. Ana forgives Ben's debt with an Ended that names nothing paid.

- **a.** Each does an ordinary thing. Each may be doing one of the regulated things above. Dan, Carla and Ben carry the risk themselves; holders carry it if an authority stops Dan.
- **b.** Before, at and after.
- **c.** The record is the evidence. Every issue, pass and ending is signed, named and kept. In his words: "Lucky my name stays out of it".
- **d.** Nothing in the record. Knowledge of the rules where one lives is outside it.
- **e.** New against A as issuing and passing. Old in B as an activity; the durable signed trail of it is the part B's local circles did not leave.
- **f.** One operator of a mutual-credit system banned the trade of its credits for money (from [Issuer], item 4). Scrip ended by statute ([Truck]).

**What a person issuing or holding vouchers might not know they are doing**, as kinds, with no claim about any place (inferred from the list above):

- selling a voucher for money may be taking money against a promise;
- a voucher accepted by many shops may no longer be a "limited" voucher;
- passing vouchers for others, or keeping them for others, may be a service of its own kind;
- running the place or the clearing may be operating a system;
- a swap of goods may be two sales;
- an Ended with nothing paid may be a gift or an income;
- the trail is a durable, signed record of all of it.

## The worked fork

**Set-up.** Dan signs an Owed, O: Dan owes Ana a voucher of 100. Ana passes it to Ben (P1) and to Carla (P1'), each pass naming O as the act it follows. Each then passes it on three more times. Ben's branch: Ben to Eva (P2), Eva to Finn (P3), Finn to Gil (P4). Carla's branch: Carla to Hana (P2'), Hana to Ivo (P3'), Ivo to Jo (P4'). Then Gil reaches Dan. No place is named, and the acts are private to their parties, as draft 14's obligations are. Each person who takes the debt gives 100 of goods for it.

| Step | What is signed | What each can see afterwards |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Dan signs O | Dan and Ana hold O. |
| 1 | Ana signs P1, to Ben | Ben holds O and P1: a debt of Dan's, passed once by its first owner. Dan holds only O and does not know of the pass. |
| 2 | Ana signs P1', to Carla | Carla holds O and P1': the same picture as Ben's. The fork now exists in the bytes. **Only Ana holds both passes.** |
| 3 to 5 | P2, P3, P4 | Eva, then Finn, then Gil, each receive O and the passes so far on Ben's branch. Each sees a chain in which every pass is signed by the holder of the moment. None holds anything of Carla's branch. |
| 6 to 8 | P2', P3', P4' | Hana, Ivo and Jo see the same on Carla's branch, and nothing of Ben's. |

**Counted by hand from these steps.**

- Statements: 1 Owed, 8 Passed, 1 Ended.
- People: 10. Eight gave value for the debt.
- People able to see the fork before step 10: 1 of 10, Ana. After it: 2, and whoever they show.
- The fork was three passes deep on each branch before anyone but the liar held both. Nothing in the steps limits that depth: with n more passes per branch it is n deep. The number of people who can see it does not grow with n.
- Ana received 200 for one debt of 100. The loss is 100 and sits with Jo.
- Whether Jo can turn to Ivo, and Ivo to Hana, and so back to Ana, depends on whether a pass carries any promise by the one who passes. The trio's Passed, as worded, names the debt and the two creditors and nothing else. On an endorsed bill each signer stood behind it (from general knowledge). If such a promise exists, the loss walks back through four people, each needing a settlement of their own, and stops at the first who cannot pay.

**What the steps do not settle** (inferred, 55 to 75; see 1.5).

- **Whose branch was rightful.** Dan paid the branch that arrived first, not the pass that was signed first. Nothing in the record orders P1 and P1': "Between two acts of which neither so names the other, no order is shown from the acts alone" (draft 25, G13.3).
- **Whether Dan's payment at step 9 ended the debt.** If a debt's fork were read as draft 25 reads forks, "nothing built on either tip counts" (G12.2), and E was signed by a holder whose title rests on a tip. Dan would then stand under "What others relied on stands", shown as "disputed" (G12.5). If it were read as draft 14 reads a forked pointer, the debt was still Ana's and Dan paid a stranger. No text says which reading applies to a debt.
- **Whether Ana lied.** "A signer who loses track of their last act (a lost device, a restored backup) and signs again produces a fork that looks exactly like a lie" (draft 25, stated costs).

**Three variations.**

- **Dan sees both branches before anyone settles** (Carla happens to show him P1'). He can refuse both. Then eight people hold a debt that nobody will honour, and the loss is no longer one person's.
- **A place is named.** Whoever is shown second learns it at once, if the place answers truly. The fork is one step deep, and the place has seen every pass (5.3, 6.3).
- **Nobody ever settles.** His words allow it: "hold and transfer debt without settling it". Steps 9 and 10 never happen. Two claims of 100 circulate on one promise of 100, each with a clean chain, and no one but Ana can know. Each person who takes one and passes it on is whole. The harm is spread thin over everyone who takes Dan's vouchers at face value, and shows only when both branches meet in one reader's hands, by settlement or by chance.

## What only the core could stop

**Nothing, by my scores: 0 of 31 threats** scored "nothing outside the core stops it" (counted). I looked for a threat that only a guarantee carried by every act could stop, and did not find one. How sure: 55 to 75. The doubt is not in the count. It is in whether "a place" belongs outside the core, as the assignment's list has it, and in rows where "something outside stops it" means only "where that thing is used".

Under his sentence "ID and text are universal, development is definition, the rest is to build on top of", four facts stand under that "nothing". Each is a statement about what the texts I read fix, not a proposal.

1. **What makes two passes a provable lie is already the core's** (Envelopes rule 15), and it reaches only a reader who holds both. No rule read makes any reader hold both.
2. **The status of a pass signed with a stolen or replaced key is fixed for every reader by Identity** (rule 34: void, or "valid and shown as disputed"). Anything built on top can attach a consequence to that status; it cannot give a different status.
3. **How much of the trail a holder must hold to check the owner follows from how a chain is read**, which is the layer's own. Nothing built on top can let a holder check the owner with less than the chain asks for, except by trusting someone who holds it.
4. **The place is the main thing that stops a threat before settlement in 5 threats** (counted), and draft 25 says of it: "no current lower text gives one", and "Until this exists, every "per place" in this text has nothing under it that can be built" (section 8.1).

One more, of the same kind: **what a fork in a debt's chain does to the debt is said by no text** (1.5). Envelopes leaves it to "the MIR that defines the object".

## Whether the frame held

Kind by kind, then the four ways the assignment said it would break.

**Kind one: a lie the record makes provable, which the settler then refuses.** Held at the counter; bent before it.

- Held: in 3 threats (1.1, 1.4, 1.5) and in one form of a fourth (1.3) the record holds two statements by one signer that cannot both stand (counted), and a settler who holds both can refuse.
- Bent: the lie is provable only to a holder of both branches, and in the worked fork that is nobody but the liar until the second branch arrives (counted by hand). The settler "refuses" only the second claimant, by order of arrival.
- Bent: "A signer who loses track of their last act… produces a fork that looks exactly like a lie" (draft 25). The proof is of a fork, not of a lie.
- Bent: a change of key turns a double pass into a dispute (4.4, traced).
- Bent: the fork may reward the one who made it (1.5, inferred).

**Kind two: a risk the world already had, which MOR only makes visible.** Held for the count; bent on "visible".

- Held: 26 of 31 are old in the world (counted).
- Bent: of those 26 the record shows more in 12 and no more in 14 (counted). And "visible" is always "to whoever holds the acts", which by default is the parties.

**Kind three: something a cMIP, a module, a place, a rail or a price can stop, chosen by those who deal.** Held as "can"; bent on "chosen by those who deal" and on the cost.

- Held: every threat but two has something outside the core as its main stop (counted: 28 of 31 outside the core, 1 the core, 2 nothing; the 2 are 5.3 and 7.1, which the core cannot stop either).
- Bent: the loser is often not among "those who deal". In 8 threats the loser did not choose the risk (counted).
- Bent: the outside things that stop threats are the same things that make Family 5 and Family 6. A place stops a double pass by seeing every pass.

**The four breaks, looked for on purpose.**

- **Harm done before any settlement that no settler can undo.** Found: 5.1, 5.2, 5.3 (what is shown), 6.5 (a hold over a debtor), 3.5 (a history and prices fed with statements that cost nothing), and 1.1 in the never-settled case. None is about being paid twice, except the last. The frame's two sentences are about redeeming twice; these are outside what they say.
- **The record shows nothing at all.** Found in 12 threats (counted), and in one form of 2 more. For 11 of the 12 something outside could have warned or priced it. The twelfth is partly an exception: for 2.6, nothing anywhere detects a signature given under pressure; a rule about who loses is all the world ever had.
- **An honest holder loses and nothing outside the core could have warned them.** Not found cleanly. The nearest are Carla in 4.3 and Ben in 4.4: each held a clean chain. For each, an anchoring or a place could have shown the order, and a price could have warned in general. A general warning ("this debt names no place") is not a warning about this debt.
- **The only thing that could stop it is a guarantee every act would have to carry.** Not found (0 of 31, counted; 55 to 75 sure).

**In one line:** the frame's conclusion held and its picture of safety did not stretch. "Stopped by the bank" covers the moment a bank is asked. It does not cover debts that are never brought to a bank, a bank that has not seen the last pass, or harms that are not a second payment.

## What his doubt bears on

His words: "Problem… double spend becomes a much bigger problem to solve. Maybe the hill is better left alone…". The findings that bear on it, each way. This section does not answer it.

1. **The problem grows with the very thing the trio is for.** The more debts are held and passed without settling, the fewer meet a settler, and the settler is where his own sentences put the safety (1.1, never-settled case; traced in the worked fork).
2. **"It express itself in various ways and each have different solutions"** (as relayed in the assignment) matches the table: five forms in Family 1, with three different main stops among them (a place in three, the issuer at the counter in one, a cMIP in one), and for 1.5 a fork rule that no text has written (counted from line d).
3. **The stops have a price that is not money.** What stops forks before settlement is a place, and a place sees and orders every pass (5.3, 6.3). The two top-ranked threats are privacy threats (counted).
4. **Most of the hill was already there.** 26 of 31 threats are the world's own, wherever debt has circulated (counted). The world lived with them through rules about who loses, not through records.
5. **Leaving it alone has its own cost, already written down.** "What a debt record says" found that without Passed a voucher handed on is written as an ending and a new debt, so "a debt nobody paid reads as ended". That is a record saying something untrue, which is a different threat from any here, and I did not rank it.
6. **Three things draft 14 had closed or priced reopen** (4.2, 3.5, and the debtor's chosen creditor). They are costs of the hill that fall on people who never use it to issue anything.

Also on the record, his sentence in "MOR can issue money": "There are pillars to sustain. There is what the bytes can do. There is a desire to define language over solutions. That is what decides…". Family 7 bears on "Lucky my name stays out of it": the names that stay in are the issuers'.

## What this cannot tell

- **Whether any of this happens among people.** These are stories I wrote. They show where the trio could break, not how often, and never that it holds.
- **Anything about texts that do not exist.** There is no text for Passed, for a debt's chain, for a debt owed to "whoever holds this", for a fork in a debt's chain, or for a place. Where I applied draft 14's or draft 25's rules to a debt, I applied rules written for something else, and said so.
- **Agreements.** I read only the named parts of draft 25. What the rest of it does for a voucher, which is an agreement in his words, I did not read. Some threats here may already be met there.
- **Full sources.** No outside page opened in this session. Every sourced point rests on a search excerpt or an abstract, and several on excerpts of secondary pages. The figures most in need of a check: the 1.5 billion dollars and the 2.7 billion yuan; the 22 of 40 billion; the 2,000 counts.

- **Completeness.** I looked for threats in the seven families and in what the sources showed (the notice rule, the honest later holder, the forker's say). A search of this length can miss one.
