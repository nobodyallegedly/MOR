**The question (the MOR lead's wording).** MOR proves who said what, in what sequence. It never proves that what was said is true. An issuer signs "six eggs delivered"; no eggs arrived. The record is perfect, and the lie is in the world, not in the bytes. What has made a lie costly where it could not be proven? And what did those arrangements need from the record they ran on?

**The frame (the MOR lead's wording).** Wherever a lie that could not be proven was made costly, three things were present. One, a name that lasts, so that the liar is the same party tomorrow. Two, something that party stood to lose that was worth more than the lie: future dealings, a posted stake, or the price of its paper. Three, someone other than the two parties who could come to believe the accusation, without proof, and act on it. The frame breaks if an arrangement made such lies costly without one of the three, or had all three and lying still paid. A second thing to test: "the witness is the hard residue".

**Three cases, kept apart throughout.**

- **P**: a lie that could be proven later, at a cost. Someone can go and count the eggs.
- **T**: a lie that only the two parties can ever know. Delivered or not, with no one else there.
- **C**: a claim nobody can check even after the fact. Was the repair needed?

## Plain words first

1. **Three different things have made an unprovable lie costly, and the frame describes only the first.** One: other people stop dealing with the liar (reputation). Two: the liar has already handed over something that the other side simply keeps (a stake). Three: a rule that makes the lie pointless or binds the speaker to his own words, so that nobody ever has to decide whether he lied (liability; a carrier held to its bill of lading; pay that follows a second measurement).
2. **Inside a pair, no outsider was needed.** The cheated party knows, needs no proof, and stops dealing. That is measured: Kenyan rose exporters and their buyers; 1,471 firms in eastern Europe, of whom 61 in 100 settle disputes with no third party (sourced: [MM], [JMW]). An outsider was needed to start with a stranger and to grow.
3. **Where outsiders did act, they mostly did not believe an accusation. They watched something they could see.** Diamond and cotton tribunals kept rulings secret and published only that a named member had defied a ruling (sourced, excerpts: [BER92], [BER01]). The judge in the best-known model keeps one list: judgments not paid (sourced, excerpt: [MNW]). Lenders watched whether an instalment was paid in front of the group (sourced: [FFP]). "Did he deliver?" was replaced by "did he obey?", which a third person can witness.
4. **Where outsiders acted on bare accusations, the accusations were the weak joint.** On eBay a buyer who left a neutral or negative mark got a negative back about 1 time in 5 (against about 1 in 100 for buyers overall), so buyers stayed silent: 99 in 100 public ratings were positive while 64 in 100 sales ended with any positive at all (sourced: [RZ], [NT]). In the Geniza letters the accused answered with counter-accusations and were often believed (sourced: [E&O-M]).
5. **A witness was not always needed, but every way around one had a price.** Pairs stayed small. A stake held by the other side moved the lie to the holder. A stake that burns when the two disagree punishes the honest one equally and invites extortion (sourced: [GOH]). Liability works only where a bad result shows.
6. **A witness was needed when someone absent had to rely: a lender, a later buyer, a regulator. And then the lie moved to the witness.** What predicted a witness's lie was who chose it and who paid it. In Gujarat the same audit firms reported falsely for plants that hired them and truthfully for plants they were assigned to, in the same months (sourced: [DGPR]). That fact, who chose and who paid, was usually not on any record.
7. **Two who lie together passed every check that asks only "did these two deal?".** One-cent sales to buy feedback, refunded "verified" purchases, wash trades (sourced: [BM], [HHP], [CLTY]). What caught them: a second measurement by someone the pair did not choose; one of many insiders holding proof; a pattern across many records; a collapse at the last deal. Rotation alone has no good evidence behind it.
8. **For claims nobody can check (case C), nothing that relied on belief worked.** In a laboratory market with 936 people, reputation raised sellers' trade and did not reduce cheating; a liability rule did (sourced: [DKS09]). Customers returned 30 in 100 more often to the inspector who passed their car (sourced, abstract: [HUB02]). No study found measures a premium earned by an honest expert.
9. **A cheap new name undid reputation everywhere it was available**, and a name can change hands without anyone seeing. The answers on record were all outside the record: closed membership, a licence, a purchased office, an entry fee, or distrust of every newcomer (sourced: [FR], [JT], [G04]; [TAD] abstract).
10. **The frame held in neither direction as worded.** Lies were made costly without the third thing (pairs, stakes, liability) and, more weakly, without the first (belief, conscience, a posted stake). And all three were present where lying still paid: Arthur Andersen, the rating agencies, diamond dealers after 2000, eBay's ratings, Madoff. What the frame leaves out is below under "Whether the frame held".

Two limits on all of this. Five of the eight central sources were not read. And both scholarly disputes the assignment calls central (the Maghribi coalition; the law merchant) stay open; I give both sides and settle neither.

## 1. Witnesses and attestation

**a. What could not be proven, and by whom.**

- A notary attests that the parties appeared, were who they said, and agreed to these words on this day: a T fact made into a three-party fact (inferred from the notary's function, 80 to 90 in 100; sources (B): [HPR], [ARR]).
- A carrier's bill of lading attests quantity and apparent condition at loading. The buyer or bank far away cannot see it. Quantity is P: the consignee counts at arrival. Condition at loading becomes T after the voyage, since damage could have happened at sea (inferred, 75 to 85 in 100; sourced for the rule: [HV], excerpt).
- An auditor's opinion is partly P (does the stock exist) and largely C (are the estimates reasonable) (inferred, 70 to 85 in 100).
- An inspector's reading (pollution, grain grade, emissions, road materials) is P in principle. A reading on one day decays: pollution varies, so a re-measurement ten days later can show a pattern over many readings and cannot prove one reading false (sourced: [DGPR] †, sec. I and III; the reading is the sub-session's, 85 in 100).
- Nearly all the measured successes of witnesses are in case P, where a second measurement is possible. The large failures (audits of estimates, ratings of complex securities) are in case C (inferred from the cases below, 70 to 80 in 100).

**Why a witness's word was worth more than a party's.** Four reasons recur (inferred from the sources below, 70 to 80 in 100): no share in this deal; many deals, so any one is small; a name that is hard to replace; and law that tells a court to believe the witness or holds the witness to its statement.

- Many clients: an auditor with many clients loses more, if caught, than any one client can pay it to lie (sourced, excerpt: [DEA]). **Disputed**: Watts and Zimmerman put the advantage of large audit firms on better internal checking of each auditor ((B): [WZ]).
- A name hard to replace: a notary's purchased office in a closed number ((B): [HPR]).
- Law that binds the witness: under the 1968 rules a bill of lading, once passed to a third party in good faith, cannot be contradicted by the carrier that signed it (sourced, excerpt: [HV]). The carrier's lie is costly because the statement is enforced as if true against its signer. Nobody has to show it was a lie.

**b. What the liar stood to lose, and who decided.**

- **Other clients, each for themselves, with no court.** After a German audit scandal, clients of the audit firm lost about 3 in 100 of their value and more of them changed auditor; in Japan, after a regulator suspended a firm for two months in 2006, about a quarter of its clients left, in a country where lawsuits against auditors play almost no part (sourced, abstracts: [WWZ], [SS]). **Disputed** for Arthur Andersen: one study finds its other clients' shares fell after it admitted shredding papers; another puts the fall on oil prices and Andersen's many energy clients (sourced, abstract and excerpt: [CP], [NPR]).
- **The regulator, on paper.** In Gujarat an auditor found inaccurate could lose its certificate and have all its reports voided. It was not applied (sourced: [DGPR] †, sec. II.B).
- **A village meeting and an election.** In Indonesian road projects the auditors read their findings aloud to the village; audits bit harder where the village head faced a close election (sourced: [OLK] †).
- **A court, holding the witness to its statement** (bills of lading, above).

**c. What had to be on record.**

- *Who said it:* the witness's lasting name on each statement. *About what, and when:* the exact reading or count, the place, the date. *To whom:* the relying party; in Gujarat one regulator, for notaries the parties and the courts, for a bill of lading whoever holds it. *In what order:* for a warehouse receipt, whether an earlier receipt for the same goods already exists; for a ship changing its classification society, the demands the previous society left open (sourced, excerpt: [CLASS]; the reading inferred, 80 in 100).
- *Seen by whom:* none of these needed a public record. Notarial acts sat in the notary's own register, open to the parties and courts. Gujarat's readings went to one body.
- *What did not need to be on record:* the truth itself; any finding that someone lied.
- *What mattered most and was usually not on record:* who chose the witness and who paid it (inferred from [DGPR], [BPST], [HUB02], [EH], 75 to 85 in 100).

**The Gujarat experiment, because it isolates the point** (sourced throughout: [DGPR] †, pp. 2 to 31).

- Before: plants hired and paid their own auditor. Rotation already existed (no more than three years at one plant), and so did decertification. Of control plants, 93 in 100 were reported compliant on one pollutant; independent re-measurement put 59 in 100 above the limit. Across pollutants, 29 in 100 of readings were falsely reported as compliant. The going price of an audit (about 24,000 rupees) was below the cost of taking the samples (about 40,000): often the witness did not look.
- The change, at 473 plants, half treated: the auditor was assigned at random; paid a flat fee from a central pool; a random fifth of readings were re-measured by an independent body; in the second year pay followed accuracy against those re-measurements.
- After: false reports of compliance fell by about 80 in 100. They did not end.
- The same named audit firms worked both sides in the same season and reported differently. Conduct followed who chose and paid, not the name.
- No accusation was made and no single lie was established. Pay followed a ranking.
- The authors could not separate the four changes, and themselves name the next collusion: re-measurers with auditors.

**d. Where it failed, and why.**

- **The witnessed party chose and paid the witness, and could go elsewhere.** Where motorists pick their emissions tester, a station with more nearby rivals passes more cars, and stations lose the customers they fail (sourced, abstract: [BPST]; 28 million tests). More witnesses to choose from made each one more lenient.
- **The stake belonged to the firm; the choice belonged to a person paid by one client.** Arthur Andersen had a century-old name and thousands of clients and still signed Enron's accounts; the ship *Erika* had moved between classification societies before it sank in 1999; rating agencies gave better grades to issuers who brought more business, most on complex deals (sourced, excerpts and abstract: [COF], [CLASS], [EH]; the common cause is inferred, 75 to 85 in 100).
- **The witness requirement moved the lie.** A shipper who wants a clean bill for damaged goods gives the carrier a private promise to cover any claim; the English court in 1957 refused to enforce such a promise, which left the lying carrier with the whole loss and the shipper free (sourced, excerpt: [BJ]). In Indonesia the suppliers' countersigned receipts were where the inflation sat (sourced: [OLK] †).
- **The witness opted out.** For a sealed container a carrier writes "said to contain". Then no witness exists and trade runs on the shipper's name, the bank's documents and insurance (sourced, excerpt: [HV]; the reading inferred, 70 to 80 in 100).
- **No place to see a duplicate.** In 1963 a field-warehousing firm issued receipts for salad oil that was mostly water; in 2014 at Qingdao the same metal was pledged to several lenders on duplicate receipts. Each receipt looked true alone. The first ended at insolvency, the second through an unrelated inquiry (sourced, excerpts from press and popular accounts only: [SALAD], [QING]). An insider's telephone tip in 1960 did not stop the first.
- **Size and the last deal.** Punishing a witness too hard removes it: after Andersen, four large audit firms remained (inferred, 80 in 100). Once its name was gone, nothing was left to lose.
- **A cheap new name** is not open to an audit firm (entry is hard). The witness-side equivalent is the client hopping to a new witness.
- **Watching was left to people with no private reason.** Inviting hundreds of villagers to the accounts meetings changed missing materials little; it cut missing wages, where the people owed the wages were in the room (sourced: [OLK] †).

## 2. Repeated dealing and reputation

### 2.1 Inside a pair

**a.** Stated in so many words as case T: when Kenyan roses arrive, the buyer can refuse to pay and say they were poor; the seller can always say otherwise (sourced: [MM] †, pp. 2912, 2915).

**b.** The liar loses the future of this one relationship. The other party alone decides. The value was measured from 189 relationships: at least 384 in 100 of an average week's revenue (sourced: [MM] †). Among 1,471 small manufacturers in five post-communist countries, 61.1 in 100 always or almost always settle disputes with no third party, and belief in courts adds nothing measurable once a relationship is over a year old (sourced: [JMW] †).

**c.** Nothing needs to be on any shared record. Each party has its own dealings in memory. No accusation is made to anyone.

**d.** The pair cannot begin with a stranger: belief in courts, and news through business networks, raise credit to new partners, not old ones (sourced: [JMW] †). Trade is capped by what the relationship is worth. A known last deal unravels it ((B): [TEL]). In Ghana, by one account, news of bad payers mostly does not travel, and firms stay small (sourced, excerpt: [FAF]).

### 2.2 The Maghribi traders (disputed; see "Where scholars disagree")

**a.** A merchant in Fustat sent goods to an associate abroad, who could misreport the price received and keep the difference (sourced: [E&O-M], p. 1, summarising Greif). Prices are P (others were in the same market). Costs, losses and effort abroad are nearer T.

**b.** On Greif's account: all future agency work with every member, decided by each member for himself on the strength of letters. He defines the coalition as an implicit agreement to hire only members and never one who cheated a member (sourced: Greif 1989 as quoted in [E&O-M], p. 2). On the critics' account: this partner's business, some local gossip, and the courts.

**c.** Letters, held by the parties; read to friends (one accused man complains that his correspondent had shown his letters around). No register, no judge of the accusation. Acts were also commonly done before witnesses, and deeds drawn in Jewish and Muslim courts (sourced: [E&O-M], sec. 4, searched).

**d.** Edwards and Ogilvie's reading of the five cases Greif offers, which I read in full (sourced: [E&O-M], pp. 6 to 19):

- In the 1055 case the accused wrote that everyone repeated the charge, each naming a different sum; he denied it, cursed the unnamed accuser and reproached his correspondent for spreading slander "never proven". One merchant is seen to stop writing to him. The authors find no compensation and no sign the news reached Sicily.
- In the second, the principal withheld money (a two-party sanction) and creditors' letters shamed the agent in his own town.
- The fifth rests, by their account, on two documents run together; the hostility followed the arrival of a legal power of attorney and stopped when a judge took it up.
- They add: accusations were disputed and often disbelieved (one merchant wrote that had he listened to talk he would never have entered the partnership); letters took months; and, unlike the diamond and cotton trades, nothing neutral existed to weigh a claim before others were asked to act.

Greif's answer, which I could not read: ostracism is rare in the documents because the threat worked; about three quarters of agency relations had no legal contract; counting court cases is the wrong measure (sourced, abstract and excerpts: [G12]).

*Limits on Greif's account:* the group could not grow past its letter network or hire outsiders; old age was covered by sons inheriting the father's standing (sourced, as summarised in [E&O-M]; (B) for detail).

### 2.3 The law merchant and the fairs (disputed)

- **The model** (sourced only through secondary summaries: [MNW], excerpt and (B)). Traders meet a new partner each time. A judge with no power to seize keeps one list: who has a judgment against him that he has not paid. Before dealing, a trader pays to ask the judge about his partner. If cheated he pays to appeal; the judge rules; the loser pays or goes on the list; anyone on the list is cheated by all. Asking is itself noticed: a trader who did not ask cannot appeal.
- The model assumes the judge can find the truth once asked. It covers case P and is silent on T and C (inferred from the model's structure, 75 to 85 in 100).
- The judge is a witness who can lie: take a bribe to report a debtor clean, or threaten to list a clean trader. The authors' answer, as summarised: his fee income keeps him honest ((B) for the conditions).
- **The critics**: at the Champagne fairs there were no private judges; public officials of the counts ran the courts and bans were backed by force (sourced, abstract and excerpts: [E&O-C]). The fullest English fair-court rolls show a lord's court using seizure, with customs differing from fair to fair and no sign of ostracism (sourced, abstract: [SACHS]). A uniform merchant-made law is a later idea (sourced, excerpts: [K12], [K15]).
- **Community responsibility** (sourced, abstract: [G04]; full text for [BR08] †). If a merchant of one town defaulted abroad, the creditor's court could seize goods of any merchant of that town; they then recovered from the defaulter at home. The lender needed to know only which town a man belonged to. The lasting name was the town's and the stake was his fellow townsmen's goods. It declined as towns grew and affiliation became easy to fake; England ended it for debts in 1275.

### 2.4 Modern trades with their own tribunals

**a.** Diamonds: deals closed by word, stones handed over on credit; much of it T ((B) and excerpts: [BER92]). Cotton: quality is P (samples are graded); what was said on the telephone is T (sourced, abstract and excerpts: [BER01]).

**b.** The club's own arbitrators decide. The loss is the right to trade there, and standing in a small community.

**c.** Rulings stayed secret if paid promptly. Only an unpaid award was posted in the club room with the member's picture and sent to other bourses (sourced, excerpt: [BER92]; (B) for the posting detail, 85 in 100). So what third parties saw was that a named member defied a ruling, which the club itself witnessed. The deal and the reasoning were not published.

**d.** The diamond system eroded after about 2000: thinner margins, new entrants from outside the community, dealers able to move themselves and their stock abroad; a group of twelve dealers ran a years-long fraud on fellow dealers (sourced, abstract and excerpts: [RIC17]; causes (B), 65 in 100). Names, future dealings, tribunal and wall were all still there.

**Macaulay's manufacturers** (sourced, excerpts: [MAC63]; otherwise (B), 80 in 100). From interviews at 43 companies and six law firms: businesses seldom enforced contracts. The sanctions were this buyer's future orders and talk among purchasing agents and salesmen. Nobody judged. Courts were used when the relationship was ending anyway. His disputes were mostly P (late or defective goods). I found nothing in the opened material on how listeners chose between the complainer and the accused.

**Known cheats who kept trading** (sourced, excerpts: [K19], [K15i]). Among London merchants of the 1500s and 1600s, reputation was built from rumour that few verified, and fraudsters manufactured its signs; creditors feared secrecy and flight more than late payment.

### 2.5 Online reputation

**a.** Not sent, not arrived, not as described: T, sliding to C for vague quality words. Among buyers' negatives in 1999, the largest group was "paid, nothing received" (sourced: [RZ] †, Table 2).

**b.** Future sales under the same pseudonym, decided by each later buyer. An established identity earned 8.1 in 100 more than new identities run by the same dealer; one or two negatives on a new identity cost nothing measurable (sourced: [RZSL] †). After a first negative, a seller's weekly sales growth turned from about plus 7 to about minus 7 in 100 (sourced: [CH] †).

**c.** On the platform's record: rater, rated, a mark of plus, zero or minus, a line of text, the date; from 2000, feedback only where the two had a recorded sale (sourced: [RZ] †, sec. 2). Not shown: the real person, and the seller's total number of sales.

*What the record measured* (1999; 36,233 sales): buyers left feedback on about 52 in 100 of sales; 99.1 in 100 of it positive; 0.3 in 100 of all sales drew a negative. It did predict: a newcomer had a 1.91 in 100 chance of a reported problem, a seller with 100 clean positives 0.18 (sourced: [RZ] †).

**d.** Where it failed.

- **The accuser paid.** When both sides rated, the seller was positive 99.8 in 100 of the time after a positive and 39.3 after a neutral or negative; sellers gave a negative after 19.4 in 100 of buyers' neutrals and negatives, against 1.2 overall (sourced: [RZ] †, Tables 7a, 7b). The authors note this cannot separate retaliation from a deal both sides found bad. In 2011, 0.39 in 100 of sales drew a negative while over 1 in 100 had a dispute opened (sourced: [NT] †).
- **The truth was in the silence, and only the platform could read it.** Positives divided by all sales averaged 64 in 100, against 99.3 for positives divided by ratings left. Buyers were not shown how many sales a seller had made. When the platform quietly ranked search results by the first figure, buyers came back more often (sourced: [NT] †). Nobody accused anyone.
- **Removing retaliation helped, partly.** From 2008 sellers could not leave negatives; conduct improved (sourced, abstract: [KLS]). Hiding reviews until both had written cut retaliation on Airbnb (sourced, abstract: [FGH]). On a labour platform ratings still inflated: of employers who privately said they would not rehire, 28.4 in 100 publicly gave four stars or more (sourced: [FHG] †).
- **The last deal.** Sellers who left had 4.38 in 100 negatives in their last 25 comments, against 1.61 over their whole record (sourced: [CH] †).
- **A cheap new name.** With free new names, the best any arrangement achieves in theory is that newcomers are treated badly for a while; a paid name, or one name per person issued by a trusted intermediary, restores cooperation (sourced: [FR] †).
- **Unverifiable claims paid.** For ungraded baseball cards, sellers claiming top grades got higher prices, delivered no better and defrauded more; not so where a third-party grader had certified the card (sourced, abstract: [JK]).
- **Who decided case T.** For "item not received" the platform believes whoever holds the carrier's tracking record; without one, it believes the buyer (sourced, excerpt of current help pages: [EBAY]). On darknet markets, escrow paid the seller if the buyer stayed silent for some weeks (sourced: [JT] †).

**Markets with no courts and throwaway names** (sourced: [BLM] †, [JT] †). Over 1.5 million darknet drug sales, 2013 to 2016: a seller with a tenth of a month's sales rated bad sold about a fifth less the next month; badly rated sellers left; complaint rates were below police-measured rip-off rates on the street. A vendor account cost about 500 dollars on one market. The stake that was stolen was the escrow, by the market's own operators: one market vanished in March 2015 with a reported 12 million dollars. Escrow needing two of three signatures would have removed that power and "appears to have been hardly used".

## 3. Something to lose, posted in advance

Neither central source (Williamson; Ghatak and Guinnane) could be opened. What is said of them is (B) unless marked.

**a. What could not be proven.** Whether the giver of the stake really broke the deal: mostly T. In lending: whether the borrower's project really failed, which is P for a neighbour and near T for a bank.

**b. What the liar stood to lose, and who decided.**

- **A stake held by the other party: the holder decides, alone.** Nobody else has to believe anything. Williamson's hostage binds a buyer who might cancel an order after the seller has invested ((B): [WIL]; one sentence sourced through [EDL] †). The device protects only against the giver's lie. Against the holder's lie ("you breached") it does nothing, and it creates that lie's reward (inferred from the structure, 80 to 90 in 100).
- The remedies in that literature, as I know them ((B): [WIL], [KRON], 70 to 80 in 100): choose a hostage the giver values and the holder does not, so the holder gains nothing by keeping it and is also compensated for nothing; make exposure mutual; or rely on the holder's reputation with others, which brings the third party back. Kronman's summary, as I recall it: each device moves or shrinks the risk and none ends it.
- **Evidence that holders lie** is thin. An English advice-bureau survey of 1998 found 48 in 100 of its clients said a landlord had wrongly kept deposit money. **Disputed** as a sample of people already seeking advice. The law from 2007 moved deposits to a third-party scheme with an adjudicator who decides on inventories made at the start and the end (sourced, excerpts from trade and legal press only: [DEP]). Large bonds posted by employees are rare, which one line of work reads as the same problem (sourced, abstract: [DKLS]; (B) for the reading).
- **A stake that burns when the two disagree: nobody decides.** Both deposit; each reports whether the goods arrived; if the reports differ, everything is destroyed. No believer is needed. The party who reports last can extort the other, and for rational parties no such contract avoids both a third party and extortion (sourced: [GOH] †). The honest party loses the same as the liar.
- **Spending sunk in a name** (advertising, premises): nobody holds it, so nobody can seize it by lying; each buyer decides by not returning ((B): [KL]). The model assumes news of cheating reaches all buyers at no cost.
- **Joint liability.** The lender decides by rule on non-payment; no finding of a lie is made. In theory the neighbour who must pay for a false claim of failure will contest it, so a cheap local witness is recruited by making him pay if he stays silent ((B): [GG]; abstract confirms the four headings).
- **A guild's embargo** was what a ruler stood to lose for abusing foreign merchants; the guild declared it and held its own members to it (sourced, abstract: [GMW]). **Disputed** by Ogilvie, who reads guilds as cartels sharing rents with rulers, with embargoes rare (sourced, abstract: [OG14]; (B) for detail).

**c. What had to be on record.**

- For a bare hostage: nothing beyond possession.
- For deposits after 2007: an inventory at the start and one at the end, which turns T into P; and a rule for when there is none (the money is the tenant's unless the landlord shows otherwise) (sourced, excerpt: [DEP]).
- For group lending: who owes what, and each instalment paid or not in front of the group (sourced: [FFP] †). Why anyone failed was not recorded; it stayed in members' memory and talk.
- A stake is control and not record. The stranger window's sentence says the record "moves nothing"; in every case here something or someone else held the value.

**d. Where it failed.**

- **The holder was the liar, or the thief.** Landlords (above); market operators leaving with the escrow (family 2).
- **The witness was inside the stake.** Group members who must pay for each other can also default together. In Kolar in 2009 a local committee told one community to stop repaying microloans; across 54,435 loans, borrowers in mixed groups who could pay stopped once co-members did (sourced: [GKP] †, a preliminary draft). Guild inspectors were members inspecting colleagues ((B) and excerpt: Ogilvie).
- **The formal stake could be removed without loss, where repayment stayed public.** Two randomised trials in the Philippines converted groups to individual liability and kept the weekly meetings: default did not rise (sourced, abstract: [GK]). In Mongolia group and individual loans were repaid alike (sourced, abstract: [ATT]). **Disputed**: one Indian lender's move to joint liability improved repayment ((B): [CCSZ]). Weekly instead of monthly meetings made first-time clients four times less likely to default on their next loan (sourced: [FFP] †).
- **The last deal:** the holder keeps the stake when nothing follows. **A cheap new name** does not help the giver, whose stake is already posted; this is the device's strength. It helps a holder who absconds.
- I found no measure of what a group member who reported a cheat risked.

**Oaths, oath-helpers and ordeals** (sourced, abstract and excerpts: [LEE]; the readings are inferred, 55 to 70 in 100).

- Ordeals were used where no ordinary evidence existed. On Leeson's account, belief that God would reveal guilt meant only the innocent were willing; the priest saw the willingness and arranged the result. The cost of lying came from the liar's own belief: no posted stake, no lasting name. **Disputed** by historians; the counts from the main register differ between sources.
- Oath-helpers swore to their belief in the party's oath, not to the facts. They were neighbours staking their own standing. The practice decayed when helpers could be hired.
- Roman law made a knowing false accuser bear the penalty he had sought. That falls on an honest accuser who cannot prove, as much as on a liar (sourced, excerpt, encyclopedia level).

**Conscience as a cost, with none of the three** (sourced: [ANR] †, pp. 1 to 6). Across 72 studies and 32,503 people reporting a private die roll or coin flip, where no single lie can be detected, people gave up about three quarters of what lying could have gained. Raising the stakes 500-fold changed almost nothing. Part of the cost was wanting to be seen as honest. It is a discount on lying, not a bar.

## 4. Price as the verdict

Note discounts and ratings are in the issuer-pricing file. Added here: only what bears on lying.

**a. What could not be proven.** In credence goods the expert knows what is needed and the buyer does not, even afterwards. The lies are three: more treatment than needed, less than needed, or a bill for more than was done (sourced: [DKS09] †, restating [DK06]). Only the first is cleanly case C. Too little shows later as a failed result; overbilling can be checked against a tariff.

**b. What the liar stood to lose, and who decided.**

- **In the theory, nothing: the lie is made pointless.** Two conditions do the work. Liability: the seller must solve the problem, which removes too-little. Verifiability: the buyer can see what was done, which removes overbilling and lets prices adjust so that the expert earns the same on every treatment (sourced: [DKS09] †, pp. 1, 6, 7, 32). No decider, no accusation. Darby and Karni's limits on fraud, as I know them: repeat custom, brand names, warranties ((B): [DK73]).
- **In the laboratory** (936 people; sourced: [DKS09] †, pp. 20 to 32). Liability worked: trade rose from about half to above 80 in 100. Verifiability did almost nothing, against the theory. Reputation (each buyer seeing only his own past with each seller) raised sellers' trade and did not reduce too-little treatment; the authors' words are that it "benefits the sellers only". Competition lowered prices and not cheating. With no arrangement at all, 13 of 48 sellers never cheated.
- **In the street** (sourced: [BBKS] †). In 174 one-off taxi rides in Athens, overbilling against the tariff happened in 11 in 100; detours of at least a twentieth of the route in 45. No driver sought repeat custom. The authors put the restraint on the risk to a lifelong licence and on guilt. The lie stayed cheap where it was deniable and became costly where a complaint could be proven.
- **When the price is the verdict on a discovered lie**, the deciders are customers, suppliers and lenders, each revising their own terms. For 585 firms caught misreporting, the loss in value beyond legal penalties was over 7.5 times those penalties (sourced, abstract: [KLM]). The verdict followed an official finding. And it appears only where those lied to are those who trade with the firm: for environmental violations the market loss about equals the fine (sourced, abstract: [KLW]).
- **When no one can tell**, the price falls for everyone, liar or not ((B): [AKE]).

**c. What had to be on record.** Under verifiability: what was done, in a form the buyer can check (a meter, an itemised bill). Under liability: the outcome, and the rule that the seller pays for failure. Neither needs a record of what was needed. For the price verdict: a public, named finding.

**d. Where it failed.**

- **The payer was absent.** A taxi passenger who said an employer would reimburse was overbilled in 35.2 in 100 of rides against 22.7 (sourced: [BKS] †). A computer repair cost over 80 in 100 more when the customer mentioned insurance (sourced, abstract: [KNS]).
- **Custom rewarded the lie.** Motorists returned 30 in 100 more often to the station that passed their car (sourced, abstract: [HUB02]). In Indian private clinics price rose with effort and also with unnecessary treatment (sourced, abstract: [DHMM]). Here customer and expert lie together against someone absent.
- **Repeat custom did little.** Posing as a likely repeat customer did not significantly improve car repairs (sourced, excerpt: [SCH]). **Disputed**: a German study finds weak reputational concern goes with more overcharging (sourced, abstract: [RW]).
- **What did reduce over-treatment:** an informed buyer (doctors' own families had less surgery; a patient who showed knowledge got fewer antibiotics); removing the gain (a patient who said he would buy the drug elsewhere: antibiotics prescribed fell from 63.3 to 11.7 in 100); a full appointment book (sourced, abstracts and excerpts: [DOM], [CLM], [GMW20]).
- No study I found measures a price premium earned by an honest expert in the pure C case (a gap in what was read, not proof).
- **A cheap new name:** the VAT "missing trader" collects the tax and vanishes; the paper trail is complete and the name is disposable (sourced, excerpt: [MTIC]).

## 5. Two who lie together

**a. What could not be proven.** That the witness, the rater or the counterparty was independent of the party it spoke about. To a reader of one statement this is C. In aggregate, to whoever holds many records, it is P.

**The forms found.**

| Pair | The lie | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Plant and its hired auditor | "compliant" | [DGPR] † |
| Shipper and carrier | clean bill against a private indemnity | [BJ] excerpt |
| Depositor and warehouse | receipts for goods not there | [SALAD], [QING] excerpts |
| Motorist and tester | "passed" | [HUB02], [BPST] abstracts |
| Issuer and rater | grade follows business brought | [EH] abstract; issuer-pricing file |
| Village team and supplier | inflated receipts for a kickback | [OLK] † |
| Seller and friendly buyer | one-cent sales for feedback; refunded "verified" reviews | [BM] excerpt, [HHP] abstract |
| An exchange and itself | wash trades: 77.5 in 100 of volume on unregulated exchanges, by estimate | [CLTY] † |
| Employer and employee | under-reported pay | [KKS] † |
| Shop and customer | no receipt | [POM] †, [NAR] † |
| Borrowers in a group | default together | [GKP] † |

**b. What they stood to lose, and who decided.** Usually one body acting on a pattern: a platform removing reviews, a regulator, a tax office. For wash trades, nothing while unregulated.

**c. What caught it, and what had to be on record.**

- **A second measurement by someone the pair did not choose.** Gujarat's re-measurements; Olken's engineers digging core samples; the consignee's count on arrival. Needs: both readings on record, matched by place and date, and the assignment held by neither of the pair.
- **Opposed interests, each keeping its own record.** In a VAT chain the buyer wants a high recorded price (to deduct) and the seller a low one, so their books check each other. The chain stops at the final customer, who gains nothing from a receipt (sourced: [POM] †). São Paulo paid customers for receipts and showed each customer what the shop had reported about them: reported retail sales rose 21 in 100 over four years. A first complaint needed no evidence (sourced: [NAR] †).
- **Too many insiders holding the true record.** A large firm must keep true books to run itself; any one employee can hand them over. With third-party reporting, evasion in Denmark was 0.3 in 100 of income; where only the taxpayer reports, 37 (sourced, abstract: [KKKPS]; the mechanism sourced: [KKS] †). The whistleblower here brings proof and need not be believed without it.
- **Whistleblowers and bounties.** Of 230 alleged frauds at large American firms, employees revealed 19 in 100 of those revealed from outside, more than auditors or the securities regulator. Where a reward of 15 to 30 in 100 of the recovery existed, employees revealed 46.7 in 100 of cases against 16.3 elsewhere. Of named employee whistleblowers, 82 in 100 were fired, quit under pressure or were demoted (sourced: [DMZ] †). About a third of frauds are detected in normal times (sourced, abstract: [DMZ23]).
- **Cross-checks against an independent record, and patterns.** Wash trades were proven only by leaked internal records naming both sides, or on a public ledger showing both addresses; statistics supported suspicion (sourced: [CLTY] †; abstracts: [AL], [VW]). Staged-accident rings were caught by the web of repeated links among people, cars and officers (sourced: [SFB] †).
- **A cost per statement.** A hotel site that takes reviews only from people who booked a night had fewer fakes and far fewer reviews (sourced: [MDC] †).
- **Rotation.** Italy since 1975, Korea, and American partner rotation show no gain or harm (sourced, excerpts: [ROT]). Gujarat already had rotation. It does not change who chooses the next witness (inferred, 75 to 85 in 100).
- **A second witness** helped only where chosen by someone else ((B) for the notarial example).
- **Collapse at the last deal**, or an unrelated inquiry (salad oil; Qingdao).

**d. Where it failed.**

- **The lie moved to the unchecked line.** Told of a mismatch with third-party data, firms in Ecuador raised reported revenue to match and raised reported costs by 96 cents per dollar (sourced, abstract: [CPS]). In the Philippines tighter inspection of one import channel moved evasion to another (sourced, abstract: [YANG]).
- **The check "did these two deal?" was met and the statement was still bought.** Every one-cent feedback sale was a real recorded sale.
- **Free new addresses** defeat the public-ledger check in part (sourced: [VW]).
- **The checker is the next to be bought** (the Gujarat authors' own warning).

## What sits outside any record and no arrangement solved

- **The event itself in case T.** Every arrangement replaced the question. "Did he deliver?" became "who holds a carrier's record?", "did he obey the ruling?", "whom does the default rule favour?". None answered it.
- **Case C.** Nothing relying on belief worked. What worked removed the gain or shifted the loss, and only where a bad outcome eventually shows.
- **Whether silence means content.** Only a keeper who counted every deal could read it, and reading it openly would have changed it (sourced: [NT], the authors' own caution).
- **A payment made off the record** between two who lie together.
- **How much the liar values tomorrow.** The last deal was not solved by anyone: exiting sellers, operators leaving with escrow, Andersen after its name was gone. The partial answers moved the name to something longer-lived: a family, a town, a firm.
- **Willingness to accuse.** Those who know often have no reason to speak; those with a reason often lack the knowledge (sourced: [DMZ], the authors' "paradox").
- **Whether the party behind a name is the same as yesterday** (row 5).
- **Conscience.** It set a floor in every experiment and no arrangement produced it.

Short sellers moved prices only when they had a record of earlier claims confirmed and brought new facts (sourced: [LQ] †). Accusing and being believed were separate hurdles.

**Where the arrangement worked with no accusation at all.**

- The cheated party in a pair simply stops dealing ([MM] †, [JMW] †).
- Liability and equal mark-ups ([DKS09] †).
- A carrier held to its bill: a claim for the shortfall, no charge of lying ([HV]).
- Pay ranked against re-measurement ([DGPR] †).
- A platform ranking sellers on silence, and filtering reviews on patterns ([NT] †, [LZ] abstract).
- Two tax records matched by machine ([KKKPS]).
- Non-payment at a public meeting ([FFP] †).
- A stake kept by its holder: the keeping is the accusation and costs the holder nothing unless others watch (inferred, 75 to 85 in 100).

**Where proving that something did not happen came up.** In these sources it came up in four places.

- *After a claim of non-delivery* ("item not received"). The platforms did not prove the absence; they assigned the burden by a rule fixed in advance.
- *As the one fact a register published*: a judgment not paid; an award defied. Here an absence was the whole of what third parties were shown, and it was shown before anyone new had accused the debtor of anything. It was readable because one keeper held both the ruling and the payment.
- *As silence read by a keeper*: sales with no feedback. No accusation was involved; the reader was the only party who knew how many sales there had been.
- *As a duplicate that nobody could see*: the second receipt for the same goods. No one accused until the collapse.

The first matches the sentence as worded. The second and third are cases where an absence carried weight without a fresh accusation, each time through a single keeper. Whether that bears on what he meant is his to say.

## Where scholars disagree

I give both sides and do not settle them.

**1. Did a Maghribi coalition exist?**

- *Greif:* yes. Members hired only members and all shunned one who cheated any. The evidence is letters showing accusations spreading and merchants cutting ties, and the pattern of agency (mostly without legal contract, inside the group). Shunning is rarely seen because the threat worked.
- *Edwards and Ogilvie:* no. Exactly five cases are offered; none shows collective punishment; several show courts. Accusations were contested and often disbelieved; letters took months; nothing neutral weighed a claim; merchants dealt with outsiders and used Jewish and Muslim courts.
- *Goldberg* (not read; excerpts and (B)): reputation did carry most of the daily weight, in degrees and not as exile, inside a frame of witnessed acts, accounts and courts; the group was not closed.
- *Greif's reply* (abstract and excerpts): the critics misstate him; at most four of their five legal cases are agency disputes; counting cases is the wrong test.
- *What each side means for the frame.* If Greif is right, this is the frame's third thing in its purest form: peers acting on an unproven accusation, each for himself, with no witness and no judge. The price was closure: no outsiders, no growth past the letter network. If the critics are right, the three things were still present, so the frame is not broken; but the case stops being evidence that belief without proof was enough. Lies were made costly by turning T into P in advance (witnesses, deeds, accounts) with courts behind, and reputation worked by degrees on top. That reading supports "the witness is the hard residue": the merchants made witnesses.
- *Open:* whether "rare because deterred" can be told apart from "absent"; the dispute is over five documents.

**2. Was there a private law merchant at the fairs?**

- *Milgrom, North and Weingast:* a judge with no force and one list is enough to make honest trade among strangers hold.
- *Edwards and Ogilvie; Sachs; Kadens:* at Champagne and St Ives the courts were public or a lord's, with force; no merchant-made code appears; custom varied by place.
- *Benson's answer to Sachs* (excerpt): court rolls cannot record a boycott.
- *What each side means for the frame.* The model, taken as a parable, says how little record the frame needs: a lasting identity, one list of unpaid judgments, and a step that marks who looked. It also assumes a judge who can find the truth, so it speaks to P only. If the critics are right about the history, the fairs show provable defaults enforced by force, and say nothing either way about unprovable lies.

**3. Was town liability private reputation or public procedure?** Greif: towns guarding a collective name. Edwards and Ogilvie: a regulated remedy of public courts, from which merchants sought exemption. Boerner and Ritschl: a liability pool that lasted where jurisdiction was fragmented. All agree courts carried it out.

**4. Merchant and craft guilds.** Greif, Milgrom and Weingast: a way for rulers to commit. Ogilvie: cartels; quality inspection by members of members did not work. Epstein and others defend guild quality marks (not opened).

**5. Does reputation discipline at all?** Bernstein's trades and darknet ratings say yes. Kadens's London cheats, Richman's diamond breakdown, the credence laboratory and Schneider's garages say little or no. The difference tracks whether cheating is ever detected by the one cheated (the laboratory authors' own explanation).

**6. Why are large auditors better; did Andersen's clients pay?** Many clients as a bond (DeAngelo) against internal monitoring (Watts and Zimmerman). Chaney and Philipich against Nelson, Price and Rountree.

**7. Does a joint stake raise repayment?** No difference (Giné and Karlan; Attanasio and others). Yes (Carpena and others, (B)). It can cause default (Giné, Krishnaswamy and Ponce).

**8. Verifiability.** As good as liability in theory; nearly useless in the laboratory.

**9. Is the price fall after fraud "reputation"?** The Karpoff studies say yes. A re-run of the recall study with a cleaner sample found no effect (sourced, excerpt: [HPR88]).

**10. Short sellers:** informed accusers whose facts mostly hold, or manipulators.

**11. Simultaneous reviews:** helped in the laboratory (Bolton and others, abstract); in the field cut retaliation and did not change who entered (Fradkin and others, abstract).

**12. Bernstein's cotton and grain evidence:** the assignment asks about later doubts. I found a comment questioning what courts should draw from it ([JOH], title and excerpt) and could open no critique. Unverified either way.

## Whether the frame held

**First direction: lies made costly without one of the three.**

- *Without the third (an outsider who believes).* Pairs: the victim knows and leaves (sourced: [MM], [JMW]). A stake held by the other party ((B): [WIL], [KRON]). A stake that burns (sourced: [GOH]). Liability and removal of the gain (sourced: [DKS09], [CLM]). In each the cost was real and measured or modelled. In each there was a price: small scale; the lie moved to the holder; extortion; or reach limited to outcomes that show.
- *Without an accusation being believed, though an outsider acts.* A carrier held to its statement; pay ranked on re-measurement; a tribunal publishing defiance; a platform ranking on silence; tax records matched. The outsider acts on something observable or on a rule fixed in advance.
- *Without the first (a lasting name).* A posted stake needs none for the giver. Ordeals, on Leeson's account, and conscience in every experiment, made lying costly to strangers. In theory, punishment can spread without names if everyone once cheated cheats everyone (sourced, abstracts: [KAN], [ELL]); I found no field case.
- *Without the second (something to lose).* Only conscience.

**Second direction: all three present, and lying still paid.**

- Arthur Andersen; the *Erika*'s classifier; rating agencies on complex deals; licensed grain inspectors in the 1970s (family 1).
- Gujarat before the experiment: lasting names, decertification, rotation, and 29 in 100 false readings.
- Emissions testers and Indian clinics: the name and repeat custom rewarded the lie.
- eBay: 99 in 100 positive on the page, 64 in 100 in fact; top-grade claims on ungraded cards.
- Diamonds after about 2000; Kadens's London merchants.
- Madoff; and two thirds of large-firm frauds undetected in normal times.
- Darknet operators leaving with the escrow; Kolar's borrowers.
- The credence laboratory: fixed identities and repeat buyers, and no less cheating.

**What separated the cases where the three worked from those where they did not** (inferred from the above, 60 to 75 in 100).

1. **Whose stake, and when.** The comparison that mattered was made by the person deciding to lie, at that moment. A firm's name did not bind a partner paid by one client. A stake worth more than the lie on paper was worth nothing if never collected (Gujarat's decertification). A name's future shrank near exit.
2. **The outsider needed knowledge and a private reason.** Villagers invited to watch did not. Creditors who knew a debtor was shaky kept quiet. Customers who wanted the false pass rewarded it. Accusing and believing were two separate conditions, and the first failed more often.
3. **What the outsider acted on.** The durable arrangements gave outsiders something they could see for themselves: defiance, non-payment, a second reading, a count. The ones that asked outsiders to believe an accusation produced contested gossip (the Geniza letters), retaliation (eBay), or bought statements (reviews).
4. **Which case.** In P a second measurement existed and most successes sit there. In T every working arrangement substituted an observable or a default rule. In C nothing that relied on belief worked.
5. **Who chose and paid the witness**, where there was one.
6. **Whether a new name was cheap.**

**"The witness is the hard residue."** A witness was not always needed: not inside a pair, not where a stake was posted with the other side, not under liability, not where both parties signed a receipt or split a tally. It was needed whenever someone absent had to rely, which is every case of growth beyond the pair. Where it was needed, it was the part that lied, was bought, or was chosen by the party it watched. And for case C no witness existed at all. So the sentence held as a description of what stayed hard, with one addition from the sources: the hard part was less the witness's existence than who chose it, who paid it, and whether a second one could be sent that the pair did not pick (inferred, 65 to 80 in 100).

## What these sources cannot tell

- **Five of eight central sources were not read.** The statements about Greif, Milgrom-North-Weingast, Macaulay, Williamson and Darby-Karni rest on excerpts and memory. The most exposed: the conditions of the law-merchant theorems; Williamson's hostage wording; Macaulay's sample details; Bernstein's posting rule; Goldberg's corpus.
- **How listeners chose between an accuser and the accused.** No opened source measures it, or the rate of false accusation, anywhere.
- **Which part of the Gujarat package did the work.** Not separated.
- **Anything about goods as small as eggs.** The cases are audits, loans, ships, diamonds, online sales and tax. None concerns a neighbour's delivery.
- **Whether an honest expert earns more** in case C. Not measured in what was read.
- **Field evidence for a second witness, random assignment or a bounty on its own.**
