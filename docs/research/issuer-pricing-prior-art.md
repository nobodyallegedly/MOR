**The question (the MOR lead's wording).** Vouchers for one good from different issuers may trade at different prices. The difference rates the issuer, set by a market and not by an agency. How has that been done or tried, and what did it need?

**The frame (the MOR lead's wording).** A market price on an issuer's paper carried real information only where three things held together: the paper could be redeemed, and tested by redeeming; someone gathered and published the prices; and enough trades happened to make a price.

## Plain words first

1. **It has been done, once, at scale.** In the United States before 1863, about 1,500 banks printed their own notes. Brokers in each city bought distant banks' notes below face value, and newspapers called banknote reporters printed the going discount for each bank, weekly or monthly. Shopkeepers read the list at the counter and took a note for that much less (sourced: [Gorton-Zhang], [Dillistin], [Mihm]).
2. **Did the discount tell the truth about the bank? Mostly yes, by three studies; loosely, by a fourth; and a fifth doubts the trades behind the far-away quotes.** New banks' notes were discounted more until they had a record of paying; discounts rose up to two years before a failure; and they moved with the things that sink a bank (sourced: [Gorton 1996], [Bodenhorn 1998], [Jaremski]). But the tie to the cost of carrying a note home was loose, and most notes never travelled 50 miles (sourced: [Weber], [Bodenhorn 2026]). Disputed; see case 1.
3. **The two systems people at the time thought worked best removed the price.** In New England (the Suffolk system) and in Scotland, every bank's notes passed at face value. One bank, or the banks among themselves, collected each other's notes and sent them home for payment, constantly. What the collector learned about each issuer stayed in its own books. The public saw only a yes or a no: taken, or not taken (sourced: [RSW 1998], [Calomiris-Kahn], [White]).
4. **Mutual credit has no price on a member at all.** In LETS, WIR and Sardex, limits, collateral, brokers and open balances stood in its place. The one price that did appear, francs paid for WIR credits, rated the whole system, and the operator banned the trade in 1973 (sourced: [Schraven 2000], [Studer], [Defila]).
5. **Rating agencies rank ordinary companies well and failed on the paper nobody traded.** One agency rated nearly 45,000 mortgage securities at its top grade in 2000 to 2007; most of the 2006 crop was later marked down. Agencies switched from being paid by readers to being paid by issuers around 1970 to 1974, and one agency's ratings rose after it switched (sourced: [FCIC], [Jiang]).
6. **Market prices move before ratings do, but not long before trouble.** Prices of insurance against a company's default lead rating changes. Yet in early 2007 those same prices treated banks as safer than other firms, and Greek state debt paid barely more than German (sourced: [Hull], [Norden-Weber], [Raunig-Scheicher], [Gibson]).
7. **The frame did not hold as worded.** It held where it was first drawn, American banknotes. It bent in the first direction: prices carried information where redeeming was suspended (wartime paper money, notes of banks that had stopped paying), and where trades were few and no claim on the issuer existed (default insurance). In each, what the price followed was a payment expected later (sourced: [Willard], [Calomiris 1988], [Weber], [Flannery 2010]; the reading is inferred, 70 to 85 in 100).
8. **It bent in the second direction too.** Paper that was redeemable, published and heavily traded gave little warning where a rescuer was expected, and none about single issuers where a clearing system had made all notes equal (sourced: [Acharya], [Gibson], [Calomiris-Kahn]; inferred as a break of the frame, 65 to 80 in 100).
9. **Three things the frame does not name recurred.** Who pays the one who publishes. Whether someone stands behind the issuers, which flattens every price. And whether the paper is meant to pass as money: one line of scholarship holds that paper used as money works best when nobody needs to ask about the issuer, so a visible discount is a cost and not a service (sourced: [Mihm], [Acharya], [Gorton 2014], [Holmström]; disputed by [Calomiris-Kahn 1991], [Flannery 1998]).
10. **What I did not find:** a test of whether a published discount beat a private monitor at warning the public; any mutual-credit system where members' credits traded at member-by-member prices; and a clean case of an American bank quoted near face value that then failed without warning. Absence in a search of this length is not proof.

## 1. Banknote reporters and note discounts, United States, 1837 to 1863

**a. How the discount was formed.**

- Note brokers in each city bought the notes of distant banks below face value and sent them home for payment. Reporters printed the brokers' rates (sourced: [Gorton-Zhang], [Koning] (weak)). Publishers were often brokers themselves; Thompson, of the best-known reporter, was a Wall Street broker and dealer in such notes before he began publishing in 1842 (sourced: [Mihm], [Dillistin]).
- Each quote is a price in one city. The surviving series are for New York, Philadelphia, Cincinnati and Cleveland (sourced: [Weber]). The Philadelphia reporter was monthly from 1839 to 1858 and covered 3,089 banks in all, about 1,500 at a time; some banks had no quote (sourced: [Gorton-Zhang]). Thompson's came weekly or twice weekly at 3 or 5 dollars a year (sourced: [Mihm] (weak, through an encyclopedia)).
- Subscribers paid. Banks also paid, for advertisements (sourced: [Mihm]).
- Quotes were stated against the city's own notes, not coin, and many were one figure for a whole state (source B: [Gorton 1999], from what I know of it).

**b. Who read it, and what they did.**

- Merchants, shopkeepers, bankers and brokers. A shopkeeper looked up a customer's note, checked it against the list of known forgeries, and allowed it at the printed discount, then sold it to a broker (sourced: [Mihm], [Koning] (weak)).
- A bank's notes passed at face value near home (sourced: [Ales]).
- Banks could act together: in April 1861 the Wisconsin bankers' association jointly declared the notes of 37 banks not acceptable (sourced: [Clio] (weak)).

**c. Whether it predicted failures.**

- [Gorton 1996]: notes of new banks, 1838 to 1860, were discounted more than those of established banks, and the gap closed as the new bank built a record of redeeming. He concludes over-issue was disciplined (sourced, abstract; sample size not found).
- [Gorton 1999]: after allowing for travel time and cost, note prices reflected the risk of the issuer, and banks under free-banking laws were not riskier (sourced, abstract).
- [Bodenhorn 1998]: discounts rose up to two years before a bank failed, 1838 to 1843 (sourced through later papers that cite it; not seen directly).
- [Jaremski]: in New York and Philadelphia quotes, discounts answered almost only to what drives default (suspensions, falling prices of the bonds behind the notes, narrow portfolios). He also reports discounts that rose for banks that survived: false alarms (sourced, abstract and thesis excerpt).
- [Weber]: mixed. Prices were lower when the issuer had suspended, and followed the cost of redeeming only loosely; some distant notes stood at face value (sourced, abstract).
- How often banks failed: of 709 free banks in four states, about half closed and about 15 in 100 did not pay their notes in full; most failures came when the bonds backing the notes fell in price (sourced: [Rolnick-Weber 1983], [Rolnick-Weber 1984], through secondary excerpts).
- **Disputed: how bad the losses were.** Rolnick and Weber find them modest and wildcat banks rare. Economopoulos puts Illinois losses at 33 cents on the dollar and says they understate. Dwyer finds Michigan in 1837 to 1838 a real collapse with heavy losses (sourced: [Economopoulos], [Dwyer]).
- **Disputed: distance or risk.** Gorton and Jaremski say risk, once the cost of redeeming is allowed for. Weber finds only a loose tie either way. Bodenhorn's newer work finds most notes stayed within about 50 miles, few reached 200, and says the pricing studies assume travel that did not happen (sourced: [Bodenhorn 2026]).

**d. What bent or broke it.**

- **Distance.** A Tennessee note could stand at 20 in 100 off in Philadelphia (sourced: [Gorton-Zhang]). Railroads and the telegraph narrowed discounts (sourced: [Gorton 1996]).
- **Forgery.** One reporter listed 1,930 kinds of forged note in 1840 (sourced, weak: a newspaper report cited in a later study). Reporters were sold as counterfeit detectors as much as price lists (sourced: [Dillistin]).
- **Who paid.** Publishers were accused of taking money to rate weak banks well, and of asking banks for payment under threat of a bad listing. The dated evidence I found is a newspaper report of 1884, after the era, which the reporter's staff denied (sourced: [Mihm], weak, through an encyclopedia). I found no dated case from before 1863.
- **Thin trading.** Unquoted banks and state-wide quotes (above).
- **Suspension.** When banks stopped paying coin (1837 to 1842 in places, 1857), quotes continued, larger, and still told suspended banks from paying ones (sourced: [Weber], [Ales]).
- **The end.** A federal tax of 10 in 100 on state bank notes from 1866 took them from 143 million dollars to 4 million in two years; uniform national notes replaced them (sourced: [Cleveland Fed]).

## 2. The Suffolk system, New England, about 1824 to 1858

**a. How the price was formed.**

- Before it, country banks' notes passed in Boston at a discount put by secondary sources at half of one to 5 in 100, and brokers carried them home (sourced, weak: [Holdsworth], [Minneapolis Fed 2010]).
- The Suffolk Bank, with other Boston banks, took over the brokers' job. A country bank kept a permanent deposit with it, 2,000 dollars for each 100,000 of capital, earning nothing, plus a working balance. In return the Suffolk took that bank's notes at face value and set them off daily against the bank's account. A bank that would not join had its notes gathered and presented at its own counter for coin (sourced: [RSW 1998], [Bodenhorn EH], [Richmond Fed]).
- By 1838 over 300 banks, nearly all of New England, cleared through it. It cleared about 109 million dollars in 1841 and over 400 million in 1858, against under 40 million of notes in circulation: each note came home roughly ten times a year (sourced: [Minneapolis Fed 2010], [Trivoli]).
- So the discount went to zero for every member. Outside New England, reporters quoted the region's notes at uniform low discounts (sourced: [Calomiris-Kahn]). I could not check quotes bank by bank.

**b. Who read it, and what they did.**

- Boston merchants and banks took any member's note at face value. The public signal was a yes or a no: on the Suffolk's list, or struck from it (sourced: [Trivoli]).
- What the Suffolk knew about each bank (its daily balance, how fast its notes came back, how far it was overdrawn) sat in the Suffolk's own books. It acted on that by granting or refusing overdrafts and by threatening to present notes for coin (sourced: [RSW 2000], [Trivoli]). The reading that this was a private monitor standing in for a price is Calomiris and Kahn's (sourced, abstract).

**c. Whether it predicted failures.**

- Failures were fewer: 24 of 354 banks (6.8 in 100) in four New England states, against 47 of 325 (14.5 in 100) in four mid-Atlantic states (sourced: [Minneapolis Fed 2010]).
- In the panic of 1837 the Suffolk kept clearing and lent to its members; what other banks owed it reached 1.17 million dollars that September (sourced: [RSW 2000]).
- Whether striking a bank from the list warned the public ahead of a failure: I found no named case and no study.
- **Disputed: what the Suffolk was.** Calomiris and Kahn: efficient self-policing, with no unusual profits among Boston banks. Rolnick, Smith and Weber: a monopoly; the Suffolk itself paid 10 in 100 dividends in 1850 to 1855 and had the dearest bank stock in Boston. Bodenhorn: the Boston banks made small country banks carry the cost, and a rival that priced fairly drove the Suffolk out (sourced: [Calomiris-Kahn], [RSW 1998], [Bodenhorn 2002]).

**d. What bent or broke it.**

- Resentment of forced membership and of its profits. Country banks chartered their own clearing bank, which opened in 1858 and paid interest on balances; the Suffolk withdrew that November (sourced: [Trivoli], [Bodenhorn 2002], [House Divided]).
- Par for everyone removes the price from public view. Banks at the edge of the region issued heavily on little coin under it (sourced: [Calomiris-Kahn]; that the one explains the other is inferred, 50 to 70 in 100).

## 3. Scottish free banking, 1716 to 1845

**a. How the discount or rating was formed.**

- I found no price list on Scottish banks and no description of brokers quoting them. White's account is that notes of most banks passed at face value across the country. Sechrest says there is evidence they did not always (sourced: [White], [Sechrest 1988]; Sechrest's evidence not seen).
- The one discount on record is on a whole class. From 1730 to 1765 notes could carry a clause letting the bank put off payment six months, with interest. Adam Smith reports the exchange on London running up to 4 in 100 against Dumfries while it stood level for Carlisle nearby, and puts the difference on such notes (sourced: [Smith]).
- What did the pricing was returning notes. From 1771 the Edinburgh banks exchanged each other's notes, twice a week in Edinburgh, and required the country banks to join. A bank that issued too much saw its notes come straight back for payment (sourced: [White], [Gazetteer]).
- Behind every bank but three stood its partners, liable without limit. The three chartered banks had limited liability (sourced: [Carr-Glied-Mathewson]).

**b. Who read it, and what they did.**

- The other banks. They watched how many of a rival's notes reached them, and returned them fast or refused them. I found nothing showing the public pricing notes bank by bank.

**c. Whether it predicted failures.**

- White's figures: bank failures ran at about 4 per thousand a year in Scotland against 18 in England, 1809 to 1830, and all losses to the Scottish public to 1841 came to about 32,000 pounds (sourced: [White], through excerpts and [Sechrest 1993]). **Disputed:** Sechrest finds no significant difference over 1772 to 1830; Dowd answers that Sechrest's own figures show one; Rothbard says few failures is no proof of soundness (sourced: [Sechrest 1991], [Dowd], [Rothbard]).
- The test case is the Ayr Bank. It opened in 1769, issued a large part of all Scottish notes within three years (one weak source says near two thirds), and stopped in June 1772. The other banks, suspicious, had been returning its notes quickly and were not caught holding them. The two senior banks refused its notes only after it had stopped. I found no signal the public could have read beforehand. Its partners, more than 200, paid all creditors in full, at a cost of about 663,000 pounds and many of their estates (sourced: [White], [Goodspeed]; figures weak, from an encyclopedia).

**d. What bent or broke it.** Each point is one side of a dispute I do not settle.

- **Was redemption real?** Rothbard and Sechrest: no. People asking for coin were leaned on, the banks stopped paying coin from 1797 to 1821 along with England, and they settled in drafts on London. Selgin answers on the suspension. (sourced: [Rothbard], [Sechrest 1988], [Selgin])
- **Was there a redeemer behind it?** Cowen and Kroszner, and Goodhart: the Bank of England stood behind the system. Dowd and White deny that it mattered (sourced: [Cowen-Kroszner], [Dowd]).
- **Was entry free?** Carr and Mathewson: unlimited liability for all but the three chartered banks was a barrier that protected those three. White: it was not significant (sourced: [Carr-Mathewson]).
- **Few issuers.** Dow and Smithin: competition ended in a few large banks acting as central banks for the rest (sourced: [Dow-Smithin]).
- The clause deferring payment and notes under a pound were banned in 1765; new banks of issue were barred in 1845 (sourced: [Act 1845], [Goodspeed]).

## 4. Issuer risk in mutual credit: LETS, WIR, Sardex

In mutual credit every member who spends below zero has issued. The unit is the same for all, so there is no place for a member's price to show.

**a. What stood in place of a discount or rating.**

- **LETS.** Open books. Any member may see another's balance and turnover, and administrators publish them. Many British schemes added limits on how far a member could go either way (sourced: [Linton], [Schraven 2000], [Aldridge-Patterson]).
- **WIR.** The operator decides who gets credit and carries the loss. At the end of 1997, 792 of 885 million francs of its lending was secured on property (sourced: [Studer]; the share, about 89 in 100, is my arithmetic).
- **Sardex.** The company sets each firm's limit by its size. A firm below zero must sell its way back within twelve months or pay in euros, and can be sued. Credits may not be bought or sold for euros (sourced: [Sartori-Dini], [Monneta], [Motta]).
- **A price did appear once, on the system.** Until 1973 WIR credits were openly offered for francs below face value. The size of the discount then is not in what I read. A Swiss television report of 2017 is cited for a dealer paying 65 francs per 100 (sourced: [Defila], [Veblen], [New Evidence] (weak)). It was a price on WIR as one issuer; no source reports a price on any member (inferred, 80 to 90 in 100).

**b. Who read it, and what they did.**

- In LETS the seller is meant to look up the buyer. I found no evidence that sellers refused members deep below zero. The measured trouble ran the other way: in one London scheme about half the members never traded in 30 months, and members were unwilling to go below zero (sourced: [Aldridge-Patterson]).
- WIR's managers read the discount as a threat and in 1973 barred the trade; it went on out of sight (sourced: [Defila], [New Evidence] (weak)).
- In Sardex the operator and its brokers read the balances.

**c. Whether it predicted failures.**

- Nothing was tested. I found no measured rate of LETS members leaving in debt, no WIR loan-loss series, and no audited Sardex figure. A Sardex founder said in 2019 that defaults were perhaps 5 to 10 in 100 (sourced: [Littera interview] (weak)).
- Argentina's barter clubs, which ran on paper credits from the network operators, show a spend-side test: prices in credits rose before trust went, and in October 2002 one network's leaders put 90 in 100 of circulating coupons as forged (sourced: [Gómez]; the forgery figure is from an excerpt I could not place exactly).
- Commercial barter exchanges: the trade body describes operators spending credits they had not earned until members stopped accepting them (sourced: [IRTA]).

**d. What bent or broke it.**

- Credits can only be spent, not cashed, so nothing is tested by redeeming (sourced: [Monneta]).
- The price was banned by the issuer (WIR) or by contract (Sardex).
- One issuer, so nothing to compare.
- Forgery of paper (Argentina); operators over-issuing (trade exchanges).
- **Newer systems.** In Kenya's Sarafu network since 2022, each voucher names its issuer and is worth a stated amount of that issuer's goods; vouchers swap in pools at values and limits set by the pool's stewards, not by trading. In Circles, personal tokens swap one for one along lines of trust. On the XRP ledger, issuers' IOUs can trade at different prices. I found no study of issuer-by-issuer prices in any of them (sourced: [Sarafu vouchers], [Grassroots docs], [Circles]).

## 5. Rating agencies, who pays them, and market signals

**a. How the rating, and the market signal, is formed.**

- **Rating.** An agency's committee sets it from public figures and what the issuer tells it in private, and changes it seldom, on purpose: a rating moves only when the gap to what a model would say passes a threshold, and then part of the way (sourced: [Altman-Rijken]).
- **Who pays.** Readers paid until about 1970. Moody's then began charging issuers, and S&P followed in 1974. In 1975 the American regulator named a small class of recognised agencies (sourced: [Jiang]; the 1975 point is B, [White 2010]). Three agencies held over 95 in 100 of outstanding ratings in 2018 (sourced, through secondary reports of the regulator's figures: [SEC]).
- **Bond prices.** Dealers trade them; well over half of American company bonds trade less than once a day (sourced, weak: [Bond liquidity]).
- **Default insurance (credit default swaps).** A contract that pays if a named borrower defaults. In three months of 2010, most named borrowers traded less than once a day, about 3 in 100 traded ten times a day or more, and 63 in 100 of trades were among 14 dealers (sourced: [Chen]). Published prices are largely dealers' quotes (B).

**b. Who reads it, and what they do.**

- Regulators wrote ratings into the rules on what banks, insurers and pension funds may hold, which made buying rated paper compulsory and the agencies' errors everyone's (sourced: [White 2010]). A 2010 law ordered those references removed (sourced: [939A]).
- Contracts carry rating triggers: a downgrade can demand collateral at once. Enron's contracts depended on its rating (sourced: [Senate Enron]).

**c. Whether it predicted failures.**

- **Ordinary companies: yes, as a ranking.** One agency's own measure of how well its ratings sorted American companies by default within a year was 84.7 in 100 in 2022 (sourced: [S&P], the agency's own study).
- **But late at the edge.** Enron kept an investment grade until four days before it filed in 2001. Lehman was rated A or better until the day it filed in 2008 (sourced: [Senate Enron], [Citizendium] (weak)).
- **Paper built to a rating: no.** Moody's rated nearly 45,000 mortgage securities triple-A in 2000 to 2007, and 83 in 100 of those rated triple-A in 2006 were later downgraded (sourced: [FCIC], through a transcript and press reports; another secondary source gives a different share). A Senate inquiry found 91 in 100 of triple-A subprime mortgage securities cut to junk (sourced: [Senate PSI], through a secondary copy). Before April 2007, 91.2 in 100 of triple-A tranches of one kind met only the agency's own double-A standard (sourced: [Griffin-Tang]).
- **Who pays, measured.** S&P's ratings were lower than Moody's while S&P was still paid by readers, and rose to match after 1974, most where the issuer had most to gain (sourced: [Jiang]). A reader-paid firm was quicker and better at ranking than Moody's (sourced: [Cornaggia]). After a reader-paid agency began covering a company, S&P's ratings of it became more informative (sourced: [Xia]). **Disputed: does competition help?** Where a third issuer-paid agency gained share, ratings rose and predicted default worse (sourced: [Becker-Milbourn]). Theory: when issuers can shop among agencies and show only the best rating, ratings inflate even if each agency is honest (sourced: [Skreta-Veldkamp]).
- **Ratings against market prices.** Default-insurance prices anticipate downgrades (sourced: [Hull], [Norden-Weber]). For 15 large financial firms in 2006 to 2009, those prices took in news about as fast as shares and far faster than ratings (sourced: [Flannery 2010]). **Disputed: which is better to steer by.** Löffler finds no clear winner; stable ratings suit some uses (sourced: [Löffler]).
- **Disputed: what a bond's extra yield measures.** One study puts expected default loss at no more than about a quarter of it; another says the larger part is default risk (sourced: [Elton], [Longstaff], through excerpts).

**d. What bent or broke it.**

- Who pays, and shopping for ratings (above).
- Compulsory use by rule, and three firms.
- **No trading at all.** The mortgage securities that failed were seldom traded and were valued by model. Gorton argues that an index on them, launched in 2006 and quoted by dealers, first made the risk common knowledge when it fell in 2007 (sourced: [Gorton 2008]).
- **Thin trading.** In 2018 a fund that had bought insurance on a house-builder's default paid the builder to miss a small payment so the insurance would pay. The market regulator said such acts may be manipulation (sourced: [Hovnanian]).
- **A rescuer.** For the largest American financial firms, 1990 to 2012, bond yields did not respond to the firm's risk, though they did for smaller ones and for other industries (sourced: [Acharya]).
- **Settlements.** S&P paid 1.375 billion dollars in 2015 and Moody's about 864 million in 2017 over pre-crisis ratings, neither admitting fault (sourced: [Settlements]).

## Whether the frame held: both directions

**Prices that carried information without one of the three.**

- *Without redeeming.* American paper dollars could not be turned into gold from 1862 to 1879. Their daily gold price moved with the Union's fortunes in the war (sourced: [Willard]). The Confederacy's notes did likewise; its gold bonds in Amsterdam implied about a 42 in 100 chance of Southern victory before Gettysburg and 15 by the end of 1863 (sourced: [Weidenmier], [Weidenmier-Oosterlinck]). The explanation in the literature is that the price followed expected redemption later (sourced: [Calomiris 1988], [Smith-Smith]). One counter-case: Confederate bonds secured on cotton followed the cotton, not the war (sourced: [Brown-Burdekin]).
- *Without redeeming, among banks.* Notes of banks that had suspended were still quoted, lower, and the 1838 to 1843 study that found discounts leading failures overlaps a suspension (sourced: [Weber], [Bodenhorn 1998]). No one has tested whether discounts during suspension picked the banks that never resumed.
- *Without many trades or any claim on the issuer.* Default insurance: see case 5. A critic notes the best evidence is on 15 heavily traded names (sourced, weak: [Expected Loss]).
- *Without a published price.* The Suffolk's books, the Scottish note exchange and the later American clearing houses each held issuer-by-issuer knowledge that insiders acted on (sourced: [Calomiris-Kahn], [White], [Gorton-Mullineaux]). The public saw none of it. I found no test of it as a warning.
- *With no price at all.* Reader-paid raters (above).

**All three present, and little or no warning.**

- *Banks, 2007.* Until August 2007 default-insurance prices treated banks as safer than other firms (sourced: [Raunig-Scheicher]). Across banks, those prices did still follow balance sheets (sourced: [Chiaramonte-Casu]): the ranking held, the level did not warn.
- *Greek state debt.* It paid 10 to 30 hundredths of a point more than German debt from late 2002, less than the country's figures warranted (sourced: [Gibson]). **Disputed:** mispricing (sourced: [De Grauwe-Ji]) or a correct bet on rescue (B). A state bond is not redeemable on demand, so the first condition held only weakly.
- *Money funds.* A share was redeemable daily at one dollar until, on 16 September 2008, one fund holding 785 million dollars of Lehman paper marked its shares to 97 cents (sourced: [Reserve]). The share's price was fixed by design, so it could say nothing; funds' yields had differed (sourced: [Kacperczyk-Schnabl]).
- *Silicon Valley Bank, 2023.* The share price reflected some of its weaknesses and not others; deposits stood at face value until the run (sourced: [Fed 2025]; the second point B).
- *A stablecoin with nothing behind it.* TerraUSD held near a dollar until the run of May 2022; large holders left first, visible in the public record of transfers, before the price fell (sourced: [Liu]).
- *New England.* Uniform quotes: see case 2.

**A theory that cuts across the frame (disputed).** Gorton, with Dang and Holmström, argues that paper used as money works because no one needs to look into it. On their account the banknote discount did reveal the issuer, and that was the trouble: a note worth a different amount in each town and each month was poor money, and the move from notes to deposits closed that market on purpose. A price that starts revealing the issuer marks a crisis (sourced: [Gorton 2014], [Dang-Gorton-Holmström], [Holmström], abstracts). Against: Calomiris and Kahn hold that the holder's power to demand payment is what keeps the banker honest; Flannery, and Berger, Davies and Flannery, find market prices at least as timely as bank examiners; the yield on banks' junior bonds told risky banks from safe ones when the state was not expected to cover losses (sourced: [Calomiris-Kahn 1991], [Flannery 1998], [Berger], [Flannery-Sorescu]). I do not settle it.

**My reading (inferred, 65 to 80 in 100, from the cases above).** The frame names what made the American banknote discount work, and that case supports it. As a general statement it is too strong on "tested by redeeming" (an expected later payment was enough) and on "enough trades" (few were enough to lead a rating, though few were also enough to rig). It is silent on three things that decided several cases: who paid the publisher, whether a rescuer stood behind the issuers, and whether the users wanted a price at all.

## The smallest set of signals

Findings, not proposals. What each working case had, sorted three ways. "Signed" here means a named party put its name to a statement and could be held to it; none of these sources involves digital signatures.

|---|---|---|---|
| US banknotes | the bank's name and town on the note; the reporter's list of discounts; its list of forgeries; whether a bank had suspended | the note, by the bank's officers (B, general knowledge); the list, by its publisher, whose name was the title | notes for local money at a broker's; notes for coin at the issuer's counter |

Shared by every case in which outsiders could tell one issuer from another (inferred, 70 to 85 in 100, from the table):

1. **Public:** who the issuer is, and the paper's terms (what it is a claim on, where and when it is paid).
2. **Public:** whether the issuer is paying now. In the banknote case a suspension showed in the discount (sourced: [Jaremski], [Weber]).
3. **Signed:** the paper itself, by the issuer, in a form others can tell from a forgery. Where they could not, a forgery list had to be published beside the price list.
4. **Either traded or signed:** a comparison between issuers by a party with something to lose. In the banknote case, a broker's buying price. In the Suffolk and Scottish cases, a clearer who had to take the notes. In ratings, an agency's name. Where that party was paid by the issuer, the signal bent (sourced: [Jiang], [Mihm]).
5. **A way for the signal to reach people who did not make it.** Where it did not (Suffolk, Scotland, Sardex), the knowledge existed and the public had a yes or no.

Redemption appears in the table as a trade: the note for the good, at the issuer's counter. In the Suffolk and Scottish cases it was the only trade, and it was enough for those doing it.

## What fails when issuers are few

- **Nothing to compare.** With one issuer the price is a price on the whole system (WIR). With a handful, quotes collapse into one figure for the group: state-wide quotes in the reporters, uniform New England quotes (sourced: [Calomiris-Kahn]; the WIR point inferred, 80 to 90 in 100).
- **The few clear among themselves.** Scotland and the Suffolk show a small number of large issuers accepting each other's paper at face value and policing each other in private. The price disappears and a club takes its place (sourced: [White], [Dow-Smithin], [RSW 1998]). Whether the club is efficient self-policing or a cartel is the dispute in cases 2 and 3.
- **One issuer can be most of the market before anyone outside knows.** The Ayr Bank in under three years (sourced: [Goodspeed], weak for the share).
- **Each failure is everyone's problem, so a rescuer is expected, so the price goes flat.** Largest banks, 1990 to 2012 (sourced: [Acharya]).
- **The good and the issuer cannot be told apart.** A discount between two issuers of the same good isolates the issuer. With one issuer of a good, its paper's price mixes the worth of the good with the worth of the promise (inferred from the logic of the comparison, 80 to 90 in 100; no source measures it). The cotton-backed Confederate bond is the nearest case: its price followed the cotton (sourced: [Brown-Burdekin]).
- **Few who rate is a separate problem with the same shape.** Three agencies, compulsory use, and issuers choosing among them (sourced: [SEC], [Skreta-Veldkamp], [Becker-Milbourn]).

## What fails when trades are thin

- **The printed price is a dealer's offer, not a trade.** Reporter quotes were brokers' buying rates, published by brokers; most default-insurance prices are dealers' quotes (sourced: [Gorton-Zhang], [Chen]).
- **Some issuers get no price, or share one.** Unquoted banks; one figure per state (sourced: [Gorton-Zhang]; B for the state-wide quotes).
- **The price mixes in other things.** Distance and the cost of carrying a note home; in bonds, the difficulty of selling (sourced: [Weber], [Elton]). Disputed in both literatures how large that part is.
- **Far from home the trades may hardly exist.** Most notes stayed within 50 miles (sourced: [Bodenhorn 2026]).
- **A thin price is cheap to move.** The 2018 arranged default; a 2025 field experiment in prediction markets found rigged prices only partly corrected by later trading, and less so where traders were few (sourced: [Hovnanian], [Manipulation experiment]).
- **The publisher gains power over the issuer.** Where few trades stand behind a quote, the one who prints it can sell a good one or threaten a bad one. Alleged of the reporters (sourced: [Mihm], weak).
- **False alarms.** Discounts rose for banks that survived (sourced: [Jaremski]).
- **Yet thin was not always empty.** Thinly traded default insurance led ratings; new banks' notes were marked down from their first appearance (sourced: [Hull], [Gorton 1996]). **Disputed** whether more trading even helps: one prediction-market study found it did not reduce errors (sourced, weak: [Tetlock]).

## What these sources cannot tell

- **Anything about goods.** Every case prices a claim on money, coin or an index. None prices two issuers' claims on eggs. Spoilage, quality and delivery are outside all of them. The closest, Sarafu vouchers for an issuer's own goods, has administered values and no study.
- **Whether a market discount warns the public better than a private monitor.** The Suffolk had fewer failures with no public price; the banknote market had discounts that led failures. No study I found compares them on the same banks.
- **How much trading is enough.** No source gives a threshold.
- **Whether redemption must be possible now.** The wartime cases say an expected later payment sufficed for a price to carry news. They concern states at war, not shops.
- **Who was right in each dispute.** Left open: wildcat losses; distance or risk; the Suffolk as police or monopoly; whether Scotland was free banking at all; whether competition among raters helps; whether opacity is a virtue of money.
- **Pre-1863 proof that reporters sold good ratings.** The allegation is in the history; the dated evidence found is from 1884.
- **Full texts.** This is the largest limit. Not one paper was opened in this session; every point rests on abstracts and excerpts. The numbers most in need of a check against the originals: Rolnick and Weber's failure rates; the FCIC's 83 in 100; White's 32,000 pounds; the Ayr Bank figures; Gorton's coverage counts; the WIR discount. Hosts refused included the Federal Reserve banks' sites, NBER, RePEc, BIS, SEC, IMF, IEA, the Mises Institute, Cato, the community-currency journal, LSE's archive and Wikipedia. A second pass with those open would firm up every case.
- **My own search for breaks.** I looked for a break in: wartime paper money, suspended banks, stablecoins, default insurance, clearing houses, state debt in the euro area, money funds, two failed firms (2001, 2023) and the mercantile credit agencies of the 1840s onward. I report above what bent the frame. A search of this length can miss a case.
