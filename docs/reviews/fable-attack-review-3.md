## In plain words

The seven decisions shut what the second attack found, as it was found. Nobody can now sign, let an agreement run for a year, and then make it never have existed. A sale no longer sweeps away the deals made before it. Nobody is handed a power over a person who signed nothing.

What replaced those holes has a common shape. **Each hole was an act that undid something. Each is now an act that somebody can decline to make.** A signature taken back late has become a second signature never given. A person kept in an agreement has become two people who need not sign the next one. Not acting leaves no trace in the record, and draft 25 forbids a verifier to say "stuck", "locked" or "ended". So the record is now honest, and silent, and the person who gains is the one who waits.

The six things that matter most:

1. **The last to pin holds the option.** After everyone has signed, whoever has not yet made the second signature can wait as long as they like, then make it or not, having seen how things went. The others, who pinned, cannot take their signatures back. This is the late withdrawal, moved one step earlier and made lawful.
2. **Pinning leans on each person's own line, and breaks where one name has two hands.** A person and her agent, or a group that signs by several people, sign on different lines. Nothing orders the agent's pin against the person's own withdrawal. The race is back there.
3. **Leaving gives every agreement a new name.** The new agreement between those who stay has a new root. Every payer, every link, every other agreement that names the old root keeps pointing at the old one, and each of them must be changed by its own signers. And two "new agreements" that both point back are not seen as a fork by anything.
4. **The dissolution is words.** His correction says the end is an ordinary update whose version states the dissolution. The grammar does not read what a version states. So no verifier can say that the first of the two acts happened, and nothing stops a later update from bringing the dissolved agreement back, with the leaver in it.
5. **A buyer of the baker's rights cannot make himself safe.** Every past holder, for ever, can sign Terms that cite a point before the sale. The anchor the buyer was told he can ask for is not the one draft 25 reads: it reads the place named by the Terms being judged, and the person backdating writes those.
6. **"My signature" has to carry everything, and one click is a signature.** A power over a person can still be reached in four ways with her signature somewhere behind it: through a group she is in, through an agent she once named, through a rule that lets others pick who holds the power, and through an offer she clicked.

Below: the brief's six questions in order, then a ranking, then questions for Nobody, allegedly.

## 1. Does the pinning close the late withdrawal, or move it?

**His words, read.** "Agreed. Let them get stuck and cook it clean". The MOR lead's lean, which that answers: "Terms count as in force only once their order is pinned, by a place, or by each needed signer acting once after completion; until then 'not yet in force'; one more round of signatures for a bare agreement, and no signature taken back after it."

**In plain words.** It moves it, and makes it honest. Before, the record said "in force" and could be made to un-say it. Now the record says "not yet in force" until a second round is done, and never un-says anything. That is a real gain. But the choice the late withdrawal gave (bound or not, decided afterwards) still exists. It belongs to whoever pins last, it costs them nothing, and it leaves no act.

### 1.1 The pin must be read one signer at a time, or the race returns one level up

**Read.** The "Arrived at" line: "each signer whose signature was needed has acted after the completion, citing it ... A withdrawal after the pinning changes nothing."

**Reasoned.** "After the pinning" can be read two ways.

- *As a moment:* the Terms come into force when the last pin is made, and a withdrawal after that moment changes nothing. Then Carla pins, and a year later signs a withdrawal that cites only her own line. Nothing orders it against Ana's pin, which was the last. That is a race between a withdrawal and a coming into force, and "What a race stops" gives it to Carla. The second attack's 1.1, unchanged, with one more act in it.
- *One signer at a time:* once a signer has acted after the completion, citing it, no later withdrawal of hers counts, because her own line orders the two. The Terms are in force when every needed signer is in that state. This reading holds. It is the only one that does, because a person's own line is the only order a bare agreement has.

The line should say which. The rest of this section assumes the second.

### 1.2 Who can refuse to pin, and what they gain

**Reasoned.** Every signer whose signature was needed, except one whose own signature already cites all the others (the one who signs last is pinned by that signature; a sole signer needs no pin, since her own line orders everything).

- **What she gains:** time. She has signed; the others see three signatures and, being people, start work. She watches. If it goes well she pins, and the Terms are in force. If it goes badly she never pins, or takes her signature back, which draft 25 allows "until those Terms are in force" (G4.10, read). No race is needed and nothing is hidden.
- **What the others lose:** those who pinned cannot take their signatures back (1.1). They are not bound, since nothing binds until Terms are in force (G4.3, read), but they stand as an open offer she can accept in ten years. Draft 25's section 7 calls the time between a first and last signature "an open option against themselves" which the first signer "can close". After the pin they cannot close it. The first to pin gives the most.
- **Either way someone holds an option.** If a pinned signer may still withdraw until all have pinned, 1.1's first reading returns and the option is the withdrawer's. If she may not, the option is the last pinner's. Without an anchoring there is no third position that I can find: something would have to make an unfinished round lapse, and lapsing needs a clock.
- **What the record shows:** signatures, some pins, and "not yet in force". Refusing, being offline, being dead and having lost a key all look the same. Read, G4.11(i): "A verifier MUST NOT answer, of itself, that an agreement is locked". "Let them get stuck" is his choice. But the record cannot say "stuck", and this stuck is lopsided: one side can still move.

### 1.3 Can a pin be raced, or made late?

**Reasoned.** Against its own signer's withdrawal, no (1.1). In three other ways, yes.

- **One name, two hands.** Read, G4.10: an assent made for a party by an agent "is cancelled ... by a counter signature in that party's name"; "the party itself MAY make it, in its own name". The agent's acts stand in the agent's line, the party's in hers (G1.2, G6.1, read). So if Ben signs and pins for Dana under a power, and Dana signs a withdrawal in her own name that cites only Ben's first signature, nothing orders her withdrawal against his pin. The same holds for an agreement that signs "by every one of its signers", each on their own line. Pinning closes the race only for a name that signs with one hand. Who pins for a party that signs through others is not said, and if the answer is "the party herself", an agent can never complete anything alone, an executor least of all.
- **A fork of the pin.** Carla signs a pin and shows it. She also signs a second act naming the same previous act, and keeps it. Later she shows it. Her line has forked at the pin (G6.2). "No release by a fork or a key" rests, in the lead's words, on G12.2, read: "Terms that were in force before a fork was made stay in force". Nothing in the bytes says when a fork "was made": the second tip cites nothing. So whether the fork came before or after the Terms came into force is itself an order nobody can tell. Section 5, window 3.
- **A pin kept in a drawer.** A pin is in force for those who hold it. Carla can sign her pin and show it to nobody, or to some. When she shows it, nothing says when it was made. So she chooses, afterwards, whether everything done in the meantime was done under Terms in force.

**What kind of act a pin is, is not said.** Draft 25 has four kinds of Statement and none that only acknowledges. A second assent citing the completion would do it with nothing new; a new kind would go against "when in doubt, leave it out". Also not said: whether an act in another agreement that cites the completion pins. By draft 25 a line is "within an agreement", so such an act is not ordered against her withdrawal, and should not.

### 1.4 What a payer sees between completion and pinning

**Reasoned.** "Not yet in force", as shown. What follows from it:

- By G4.10 (read) "nothing in Terms counts, until those Terms are in force". So a payer who names those Terms is answered "not shown" for who receives. If she pays anyway (the money moves in another layer), the record does not say whom it was owed to.
- When the last pin appears, the same payment names Terms that are in force, and the answer becomes "shown". **The last pinner decides, afterwards, whether money already paid was paid under the Terms or under nothing.** If she is the receiver and never pins, she has received under nothing and owes no one a share.
- Two readers, two answers, as before: whoever holds every pin sees "in force"; whoever lacks one sees "not yet". She chooses who holds hers.
- A payer cannot tell "the second round is under way" from "it will never finish".

### 1.5 Does pinning apply to an update, and can one signer hold every change hostage?

**Read.** His words are about Terms; an update is Terms. "The two gates": "The new version's own `in_force` governs only its own later life, not whether it replaces the old one." G4.5: "Terms stop being in force when Terms that replace them ... are in force."

**Reasoned.**

- **"Replaced, and not in force" was a corner; it is now the normal state.** Every update passes through a time when the condition has answered valid and the pins are not all in. The two sentences read above disagree about which Terms govern in that time. The second attack asked (its 1.3, and rank 9); it has to be answered before pinning can be written.
- **Hostage:** only by someone whose signature the update needed, who could already refuse to sign. What is new is how: she signs, and does not pin. The record shows her signature. It reads as consent and works as a veto, and it replaces the second attack's "signs each time and takes it back each time" with something that leaves no withdrawal to count.
- **Old proposals do not die.** An update everyone signed and one person never pinned stays ready. A year later, when it suits her, she pins it, and it replaces the version in force. The others pinned and cannot withdraw. To get ahead of it they need another update, which under the default needs her. Every unfinished proposal is a card in her hand.
- **Late updates get harder to see.** By G4.7(a) (read) a rival is late by knots where what it cites is "enough for F to be valid and in force". "In force" now includes F's pins. A rival that cites F's signatures and not its pins was late yesterday and is a fork today.
- **Bare offers:** under the default every buyer must sign every update (the second attack, read from G4.8(a)). Now every buyer must also pin it.
- **Under a rule in `in_force`.** Where the rule is "any two of three" and three signed, "each signer whose signature was needed" does not say which two must pin. If all who signed must, a signer nobody needed can stall. And the rule is a module that reads the acts for itself (the second attack's section 5): whether it is handed signatures with the pins already checked is the difference between pinning being a guarantee and being a habit.

## 2. The two acts of leaving

**His words, read.** "What is important is not to have her signature on the agreement they work from from there onwards, so leaving is closer to an end, she leaves, agreement cannot work without her, the other two agree, right after they sign the new in force agreement between the two". "Yes, because her signature was the problem for other updates in the future, no? So, the mechanism of leaving forces two acts to be created instead of one, thus solving her signature problem". "Wait… an agreement can always end, it is not a feature, it is an update to the agreement that states the dissolution of it". And that morning, of a new agreement that follows the old one: "B is impossible. C is not needed".

**In plain words.** The two acts do what he wants for the new agreement: her signature is on nothing in it. The trouble is at the two edges. The old agreement does not stop being a thing that can be updated and paid. And the new one is a stranger to everything that knew the old one.

### 2.1 The dissolution is words, and the grammar does not read words

**Read.** G4.9: "a verifier MUST NOT answer that Terms or an agreement have ended ... shows the agreement as going on in its last version ... whatever its conditions say becomes of the work and its shares; what they say is theirs (G2, task K1)." The corrected "Arrived at" line: "nothing is open in the bytes for it beyond how the new agreement points back."

**Reasoned.** I think something is open. A version that "states the dissolution" states it in its body. The grammar reads the body only through its cMIP. So:

- **No verifier can say the first act happened.** It can say that the new agreement points back, and to what. It cannot say that what it points to is dissolved. A reader without the body's cMIP gets "unchecked"; a reader with it gets that cMIP's reading, not an answer of the grammar.
- **The dissolved agreement can be updated again.** The dissolving version is the version in force. It has an update condition like any other. If that is "two of three", carried over from before, Ana and Ben can pass a next version that brings the agreement back to life, names Carla, and binds her (G5.1(c), read: "the act is a Terms act that replaces Terms by which they are bound, and the update condition of those Terms answers valid for it"). She left, and is back, with no signature. For the dissolution to be an end, the dissolving version has to be one nothing can replace: a lock, which is a choice of cMIP that the verifier may not name.
- **So "out of the parties, still bound" is narrowed, not shut.** It is shut where people take the two-act path and the dissolving version cannot be replaced. It is open where they do not. An update that simply leaves Carla out is still an ordinary update that a condition may answer valid; nothing makes people use two acts. The lead's lean at the start of that exchange, that a version binds only the parties it names, is not in the "Arrived at" line, and his words went another way. The second attack's question 2 is still unasked.
- **What the dissolving version says about her.** Under a majority condition, the two write it. What she still owes is theirs to write.

### 2.2 Between the dissolution and the new agreement

**Reasoned.** One line each for what the brief asks.

- **Money still arriving.** A payer who names an old version has paid as it says (G13, read: "A version that replaces one an act names does not change what these grammar rules read that act as having done"). Every link already out in the world names an old version. So money keeps arriving at the old receiver, divided in the old shares, Carla's included, for good. Nobody is bound to pass it on: the old version is replaced, and by "What the core is for" the receiver's duty after that is a cMIP's. If the old receiver was Carla, the person who left keeps receiving for the work.
- **Money that names no version** is read from the old agreement's Terms in force, which is the dissolving version. If that names no receiver, "no one receives" (G1.4, read).
- **Powers given.** A power needs Terms "which are in force" (G5.1(b), read). Powers of the old versions end unless the dissolving version repeats them. Whether an agent's act came before or after is G5.4's: without a place, "**not shown** where citing gives no order". An agent who signs for the old agreement and cites nothing leaves whoever dealt with them unable to know.
- **Spots filled.** Fillings belong to the old agreement. The new agreement has its own roles and its own rule for who fills them. Those who earned a place are in the new one only if the two who wrote it put them there. They are not asked: they never signed it.
- **A contest under way** stays on the old agreement's record and reaches the signers of the version it was made against, Carla among them. Since old versions keep taking money, new contests against them keep reaching her after she left. The new agreement starts with a clean record. A reader shown only the new one sees none of it, unless a verifier must show the pointer back with every answer, which nothing says.
- **Other agreements that name the old one.** A service's deal, an escrow, a work built on this one: each names the old root, as a party or as receiver. Each must be updated to name the new root, by its own update condition, by its own signers, any of whom may refuse. Until then they pay and serve a dissolved agreement. The entry "The key concept" makes an agreement as a party the centre of the design, and a party is named by its root. A leaving now changes that name for everyone who used it.
- **The baked work.** Terms for an envelope count only with the holder at the act cited among their parties, bound (G14.2, read). If Carla is that holder, the two cannot make a new agreement for the work at all unless she is in it, or first transfers her rights. For a baker, leaving means selling.

### 2.3 Using a leaving to end things for everyone

**Reasoned.** Yes. Nothing makes the second act follow the first. "Right after" is what people intend; the grammar has no "right after".

- Ben wants out and Ana will not let him go. Carla asks to leave. All three sign the dissolution. Ben then does not sign the new agreement. He is out, by a door "No default way out" says does not exist.
- Ana can protect herself only by order: the new agreement signed and pinned first, or its `in_force` rule made to wait on the dissolution. That is a module's work. Bare, whoever signs the new agreement last holds the same option as in section 1.
- With pinning, a bare three-person leaving is about eight signed acts where it was three. Each is a place where one person can stop.

### 2.4 An expulsion in two acts

**Reasoned.** Under the default she cannot be made to sign, and without her signature the dissolution does not pass. Under a majority condition her signature is not needed: the two pass the dissolution and sign the new agreement. That is the constitution she signed, as the lead said of expulsion in "Firing an employee". Two things are new.

- **It sheds more than her.** An expulsion by update kept the agreement, and with it its buyers, its fillings, its deals and its record. A dissolution sheds all of them at once, on the same two signatures.
- **A leaving and an expulsion look alike:** a dissolving version and a new root. What tells them apart is whether her signature is on the dissolving version. That may be the "clear identifiable evidence" he asked for on 17 November; it is his to say.

And the second act needs no first act. Where one of the two holds the baker's rights, they can sign a new agreement for the same work today and leave the old one standing with Carla in it. Draft 25, section 7, read: "The same thing sold in two separate agreements is seen by neither agreement's rules." That was always possible. What is new is that "a new agreement that points back" is now what an honest continuation looks like, and a reader cannot check the first act (2.1).

### 2.5 What the new agreement inherits, and by whose signature

**Reasoned.** Nothing by the grammar. Everything the two choose to write, by their signatures alone. They can take the work, the name and the audience, and leave the debts, the contests and the contributors. The pointer back is theirs to write or leave out.

**And nothing sees two successors.** Inside one agreement, two next versions are a fork, and both stop (G12). Ana with Dana, and Ben with Eve, can each sign a new agreement that points back to the old one. Those are two roots. No fork is seen, both count, and each tells its payers it is the continuation. The decision moves the moment of continuation out of the one place where the grammar catches two histories.

**One thing to put to him plainly.** That morning he said of a new agreement that follows the old one: "B is impossible." That evening the second act is one. Both are his words, and the later refines the earlier. But what made B impossible in the morning is not in the ledger, and it may be what 2.2 and 2.5 describe.

**Where the two acts do not fit at all.** A buyer, or anyone who fills a spot, is not in the list of parties; the role is. A buyer who wants out is not "leaving" in this sense, and no one would dissolve an offer for it. And under the default, dissolving an offer needs every buyer's signature (G4.8(a)), so a bare offer that has been bought cannot be left by its makers either.

## 3. A sale, again

**His word, read.** "Agreed", to the lead's (b): the buyer of the rights carries the risk, "who can ask for a place or an anchor when he buys".

**In plain words.** The second attack's broom is gone: a sale no longer makes honest earlier deals fall. The cost was put on the buyer of the rights on the ground that he can protect himself. From what I read, he cannot.

**Who can backdate against him. Reasoned.**

- The seller, with anyone, a second identity of her own included. After the sale she signs Terms that cite a point of the envelope's chain before it, with herself as the holder there. Read, G14.2: "Where neither the knots nor an anchoring show it, the Terms are not late, and are read by the holder at X, however late they may in fact be."
- Every earlier holder back to the baker, each at their own point, for ever.
- Anyone holding a power to sign for any of them that was never ended. By "A power given binds its giver" that counts as the holder being bound.
- Pinning does not help: those backdating pin their own Terms.

**What they can make.** A separate agreement for the same work that counts: a free licence without end, a receiver that is the seller, an update condition only she can meet. A payer who names it pays her. The buyer's own Terms still count beside it. What he loses is being the one agreement for the work.

**What he must do when buying, and why it does not work. Read, G14.2(b):** Terms are late by anchoring where "the place or the clock the agreement names (G7.4) shows the Terms after Y; or, where it names none, the anchoring the reader accepts". G7.4: "a verifier MUST NOT give an answer of G7.2 for that agreement per another anchoring."

**Reasoned.**

- "The agreement" there is the agreement of the Terms being judged, which the backdater writes. If she names a place she runs, every reader must use it and no other, and it will never show her Terms after the sale. The buyer's anchor is, by the letter, not read.
- If she names no place, the buyer's anchor counts only for readers who choose to accept it, and only if it can show her Terms after the transfer. Her Terms are never brought to it. Whether "not there when the transfer was recorded" means "after" is said for a place an agreement names (G7.2) and not for a reader's own.
- What would make him safe is a closed list for the work: Terms for an envelope count only once shown in a place the envelope names. Draft 25 lists it as not known: "whether that layer gives an anchoring of its own that orders Terms against its transfers" (section 8.1), and of the exchange, "not in the exchange" (section 8.2, item 3).
- He cannot search either. Honest earlier Terms may be private (section 7, "Conceal"). A verifier answers what exists "as shown".
- One thing would raise the cost. If the seller's Terms had to follow her transfer on a line of all her acts, backdating would mean forking that line, and she would lose every agreement she has. That line is the lower layer's ("Two threads, two owners"). In draft 25 a line is "within an agreement", and the first act in a new agreement names no previous. I did not read the lower layer. As draft 25 stands, backdating costs her nothing.

**A stronger move, standing, for Envelopes.** She signs a second transfer from the same point to a second identity. That is a fork in the envelope's chain. Where that layer then gives no holder, the buyer's own Terms are "not shown" (G14.2, G14.3(b)), and whoever resolves it is that layer's to say.

**Is this readable by someone who does not negotiate?** No. He is shown that the envelope names him as holder. He is not shown that agreements he has never seen, made by people he never met, may count beside his. If it stays a cost on him, the plain sentence belongs in what he is shown when he asks whose the work is.

## 4. Nothing about me without my signature

**His words, read.** "Absolutely, no need to even debate this. Nothing about me is accepted without my signature. But, I suspect we opened Pandora's box… how to fire an employee?" The "Arrived at" line: "a power to act for someone counts only where that person's own signature is on the version that gives it."

**Here the line says less than his words.** "Nothing about me" is wider than "a power to act for me". Taken alone it would also stop a majority from rewriting her share, which the same exchange accepts ("a member's expulsion is an update passed by the constitution he signed"; he: "True, it is now a set of agreements"). So he means something between the two. Where the edge is, is his. Cases on it, each done by an update she did not sign: she is made the receiver, and now holds others' money; a data cMIP is added, so her reports are read by people she never agreed to, against "Data needs to move along paths that people agree, with signature."; what she owes is raised (the second attack's buyer under a maker-only condition). The minted power was one of a family, and the line names one.

### 4.1 What it breaks that should work

**Reasoned.** Everything turns on two words: "own", and "the version that gives it".

- **A collective naming an officer.** An officer acts for the agreement. Acting for an agreement binds each of its people (G5.1(b), read: "names an agreement by whose Terms they are bound"). If that counts as acting for each member, every member's own signature is needed on the version that names the officer: no officer is appointed or replaced by a vote, and one absent member blocks it. If it does not count, as the lead's answer on firing assumes ("the power to act for the company, which the company gave"), then a power for the group is given by majority, and section 4.2's first road is open. Both cannot hold. Which one does is not said.
- **A power kept through an update she did not sign.** She gave the power in version 1. Version 2 passes by two of three, without her, and repeats the entry word for word. An act under the power must name Terms in force, which is version 2. Is her signature "on the version that gives it"? If no, every majority update silently ends every personal power in the agreement. If yes, "the version that gives it" means the version where it first appeared, and the rule must say what counts as the same power.
- **An estate, a successor.** Named in advance, she signed: that works. But the dead sign nothing again. If the power must be re-signed at each version, it dies at the first update after she does, which is when it was for. And by section 1.3, if only she can pin, her executor can complete nothing.
- **A guardian.** A power the ward did not give is exactly what is now impossible. Draft 25 already said that someone who cannot sign "is no party" (section 7). What goes is the chance to build guardianship on top. It can exist only as someone holding her key, which is Identity's.
- **A majority can also take a power away.** Dana gave Ben her proxy. An update she did not sign removes it. That is about her, without her signature, and the line (which speaks of giving) does not reach it.

A way round most of this is what "Firing an employee" already points to: a power for a person lives in its own agreement between her and her agent, where under the default her signature is on every version. Then see 4.2, last item.

### 4.2 What still gets through

**Reasoned.** Four roads to a power over her with her signature somewhere behind it.

1. **Through the group.** Two of three add a power for the *agreement*, not for Dana. The agreement then signs Terms on Dana's baked work that name her. Draft 25 counts her as bound by them: bound "by an assent made for it under a power, or as an agreement's signature (G5.1(b), G1.2)" (G14.2, read). The power was minted by a majority; her work is ruled; the line is kept to the letter.
2. **Through an agent she once named.** Ben holds a power to assent for her. He signs, for her, the version that widens his power or drops its stop (the second attack's 1.2(b)). Is that "her own signature on the version"? "A power given binds its giver" says his signing counts as hers. If it does here too, one signature long ago gives any power later. If "own" means her own hand, say so, and the first item of 4.1 has its answer for people. A group has no hand of its own.
3. **Through a rule that picks the holder.** She signs a version whose power says "held by whoever two of three elect". The two elect themselves. Her signature is on the version. The only difference from the minted power is that she could have read it.
4. **Through one click.** An offer's Terms may carry a power for whoever fills the buyer's spot. She clicks; her own signature is on the version. The maker may now sign for her, her own baked works included. The second attack called the click "the heaviest signature in MOR, made by the person least likely to read". This decision rests the whole protection on that signature.

**Can a power still be given that nothing ends?** Yes; the decision does not touch it. With G5.5's default gone, a bare power between two people ends only by an update that the agent must sign. An agent who is gone leaves it standing for good. A stop read by the power's own rule is a race the giver can make (the second attack's 1.2(c), unanswered). And "which change fails in a race", the second attack's question 5, now decides three cases: the giver's stop, the fork against a pin (1.3), and the agent's pin against the party's withdrawal (1.3).

**Firing, briefly. Reasoned.** "A set of agreements" works, and it has the cost of every set: nothing ties two agreements together. ## 6. The set as a whole

**It agrees with itself in what it wants.** An agreement holds; nothing undoes a signature; nothing about a person comes from others; and whatever is uncertain does not work. I found no two of the seven that contradict each other outright. I found four places where two of them need a sentence between them: the pin and the two gates (1.5); "own signature" and "a power given binds its giver" (4.2); a person and an agreement as the one acted for (4.1); the second act and "B is impossible" (2.5).

**Reasoned, five things about the direction.**

1. **Undoing has become withholding.** Every repair turned "an act that takes something back" into "an act somebody must still make". That is the right direction for a record: it no longer says anything false. But the gain went from the one who acts to the one who waits, waiting leaves no act, and the verifier has no word for it. "Let them get stuck" is honest only if a person looking at the record can see who it is stuck on. Today they can see who has not pinned. They cannot see that it has been a year.
2. **Bare, the only order is one person's own line.** Pinning works because it turns a question between two people into a question on one person's line. Each place it fails is a place where that is not so: an agent, a group, a fork, another agreement. Three of the seven still speak of "before" and "after" between different people's acts: the pin read as a moment, Terms "in force before a fork was made", an agent's act before or after dismissal. Each will need the same turn, or a place.
3. **The set multiplies agreements, and between agreements the grammar is blind.** A working relationship is now several agreements; a leaving makes another. Inside one agreement there is a chain, a line and forks. Between two there is none of that: no fork, no order, no "both or neither", and draft 25 says so ("seen by neither agreement's rules"). The more of life is put between agreements, the more of it sits where no lie is exposed. "Agreement as a party" is the centre of the design; decision 6 changes a party's name whenever someone leaves.
4. **Signature is now the whole protection, and signatures are not equal.** "Nothing about me without my signature" is as strong as the moment of signing. The cheapest signatures in MOR are the click and the joining of a group, and both can carry a power or a majority condition. Nothing was added on what a client must show before either. The second attack said so; with decision 4 it matters more.
5. **Bare has become heavier, not safer.** Two rounds to make, two rounds for each update, about eight acts to leave. Each added act is a new last act, and the last act is where the option sits. This pushes everyone to name a place, and the place is still the least written thing: the sentence on a place that contradicts itself is still out of the grammar by "The place is a pointer", and every attack of the first review's section 4 stands.

**On decision 7. Reasoned.** The move is sound, with two notes for the scribe's check. G9.2 is half about Agreements' own things (counter signatures that lean on each other, powers through powers), and G8.4 is about spots and legs. And the answer words are no longer enough for what Agreements must tell a reader apart. Draft 25's "not shown" means "an act not held might" show it. The set now has three states under it: an act I do not hold might exist; the second round is not finished; two acts that can never be ordered. A payer needs to know which. "Not yet in force", from decision 1, is not among draft 25's answers, and where it will live after the move is not said.

## 8. Questions for Nobody, allegedly

In the order I would ask them, one at a time. None is mine to answer.

1. Ana and Ben have signed and pinned. Carla has signed and not pinned, and waits a year. May Ana and Ben take their signatures back while she waits?
2. Two of three dissolve an agreement. A month later the same two pass a new version of the old agreement that names all three again. Is Carla bound by it?
3. The song is dissolved and signed again by two. The film that pays the song still names the old one. Whose job is it that the money finds the new one, and what does the film's payer see until then?
4. Two of three give their group a power, and the group signs Terms on a work the third one baked. Is that "about me without my signature"?
5. An agent I named signs, for me, a version that gives him more power over me. Is that my signature?
6. A buyer clicks an offer whose Terms let the maker sign for whoever buys. Is one click enough to give that?
7. Someone buys the baker's rights. A year later Terms appear, made with the seller, citing a point before the sale, on a relay the seller runs. Who was supposed to be able to stop that?
8. Carried from the second attack, still unasked, and now deciding three cases: in a race between an act that binds and an act that releases, is it always the binding that fails?
