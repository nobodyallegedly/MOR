## In plain words

**The verdict.** The first verdict holds, and it is stronger now than it was. Both conditions are met in the way I asked (the MIR hash is the file's bytes and nothing else; "same payment" is the rail's answer, unknown without it), and the heavy rules that had only reasoning behind them are now built twice and agree on 108 cases. None of the 24 questions finds a text wrong in substance. The residue is of the same kind as before: two sentences the texts leave unsaid, a handful of gaps the C build names itself, and three places where both implementations took the same step away from a text that had said otherwise.

**The questions that matter most, in order.**

1. **Q55, what a step of "belongs to" is.** The text's own sentence says "each `objects` entry or act answered being one step". The C verifier follows every `objects` entry, to any act; the Rust `belongs_into` follows an `objects` entry only when it names terms, and otherwise steps only through Money's purpose and fulfils, an obligation's field 4 and an offer's field 0. A contest or any other Agreements act that names an obligation in `objects` belongs to the deal for C and to nothing for Rust. "Belongs to" decides which keepers can place an act, and keepers decide void against disputed (Identity rule 34) and deadlines; this cannot wait. One sentence settles the reading, and whichever is chosen, one of the two cores changes.
2. **Q53, a sale on a claim nobody verified.** Money line 183: a client MUST NOT treat a receipt or claim as valid unless its verification answer is valid. For disagreements both verifiers hold to it (a record counts only with a valid rail answer). For a vow's sales and a purchase, both fail open: the Rust `vows.rs` reads a `rail_invalid` set, so a claim with no answer counts as a sale, and the C verifier says so in its choice. The vectors do not separate the readings because every sale's claim in them carries a valid answer. A verifier that says "sold" on an unverified claim misleads a seller who ships, and the buyer's contest of F386 now attaches to such a purchase. Before the freeze.
3. **Q62, where a private link must be found.** Identity line 186: at the homes the signer's chain names *at the link act's binding*, a later move of homes not voiding it. Rust reads the homes at the binding (`chain.rs`, `res.states[k].homes`); the C verifier reads the homes at the chain's latest position (`identity.c`, `private_link_unplaced`, `c->pos[c->npos - 1]`). After a move of homes the two give opposite answers. A link decides nothing binding, so this can wait if it must, but the fix is one line and the text is unambiguous.
4. **Q57 and Q58, the collective's records and endings in the C build.** The text is precise and Rust implements it; the C verifier stops short where it says so (a record's signatures not counted against the clone's mark, nor its parent or the constitutional tier checked; a fork or closing judged without the voices departures removed). Both are the way out of a collective, so both need a vector from the chain-signature family before the freeze; neither is a text question.

**The divergences found between the C reading and the Rust core, where no vector decides.** Seven: Q55 (a step through `objects`), Q57 (a record whose signatures fall short of the mark), Q58 (a closing after a departure), Q60 (a settlement that omits a tip; a judge that contradicts itself), Q61 (an inner deal that forked), Q62 (homes at the binding against homes now), Q64 (a contest among finals against cannot tell). Each has its vector below. Q53 is a divergence of both from the text, not of the two from each other.

### Identity

**Q62. A private link "found at a home".** Identity line 186 is explicit: the sealed form is published at the homes the signer's chain names at the link act's binding, and a later move of homes does not void it. Rust `status_cx` (the F152/F159 branch) tests `res.states[k].homes`, the homes at the binding; C `private_link_unplaced` tests `c->pos[c->npos - 1].homes`, the latest. **C wrong.** A second, smaller difference: Rust turns a disputed private link found nowhere into unknown; C leaves it disputed (its test is `inner == MOR_ST_VALID`). Vector: a private link claim bound at position k, its chain naming home H1 there, a rotation at k+1 moving to H2; found at H1, expected valid; found only at H2, expected unknown. *Can wait:* yes, strictly, since a link gives no authority (rule 16); but it is a one-line fix against a sentence nobody disputes, so it should not.

### Agreements

**Q55. "Belongs to" at any depth: what a step is.** Line 98 opens with "names, in `objects`, that agreement or an act of its chain; or through the act it answers", lists Money's purpose and fulfils, an obligation's field 4 and an offer's field 0, and then says "each `objects` entry or act answered being one step". C `belong_steps` takes every `objects` entry (chain and predecessor) and every answered field as a step, stopping at terms and at Identity acts; Rust `belongs_into` takes an `objects` entry only when it names terms directly (`Some(_) => {}` for any other act) and steps only through the Money, obligation and offer fields. The paragraph supports C; the bullets support Rust. **Text unclear**, and whichever reading is chosen, **Rust wrong** or **C wrong** follows. My lean is the C reading, because the text's own sentence says it and because a contest or a settlement request that names an obligation of a deal is an act about that deal, which its keepers should be able to place; the cost is that anyone can make an act "belong" by naming, which gives a keeper work and nothing else, since a record places an act and decides nothing about it. Closing sentence at line 98, replacing "each `objects` entry or act answered being one step": *each `objects` entry, chain and predecessor alike, and each act answered (a Money record's claim and what it fulfils, an obligation's agreement or offer, an offer's agreement) being one step, whatever the act named is, save an identity's genesis, rotations and Identity everyday acts, which are followed no further*. Vector: a party's contest (type 14) naming, as chain and predecessor, an obligation of a deal; a keeper record of the contest; the keeper question, expected placed under the deal's keepers. *Can wait:* no. It decides which records count under Identity rule 34 and Agreements rule 33.

**Q56. The version in force: what "history holds" means.** Line 105: "what its `prev` and `objects` name, followed back". Rust `act_history` follows `prev` and each `objects` entry's predecessor; C the same ("objects' predecessors"). Same verdict. **Housekeeping**: the text says "`objects`" where both implementations read "the predecessors `objects` name"; one word, *predecessors*, at line 105 makes the reading the text's. *Can wait:* yes.

**Q57. The C verifier checks the first and says it does not check the second; it also does not check the parent or the tier. **C wrong**, declared. Vector: a record whose field 2 names valid signatures short of the clone's mark (one of three under a two-of-three mark); expected the clone not in force, the record still a line. A second: a record naming a clone whose parent is not the agreement in force for it; expected not in force. *Can wait:* no. The agreement in force decides every collective act's consent.

**Q58. **Q59. An act after an ending's line.** Line 3936 says what C chose, word for word, and Rust's `history` gives the same line (bound to an earlier link, or bound to the ending's chain act and in the history the tips cite). Same verdict; settled by agreements[21], [22], [25], [26].

**Q60. The chain of judgment for a deal's fork.** Lines 1269 to 1273 and rule 35 as C reads them; Rust the same on the vectors. ### Money

**Q51. What identifies "which payment" in the rail's answer.** Rule 12 (line 204 on): the rail Module's answer, never the proof's bytes; without the answer for both records, unknown, bytes matching or not. The facts map proof bytes to a payment; C adds that records on different rail Modules are never one payment, and Rust `same_on_rail` says the same (`a.0 == b.0`). Rust `record_payment` gives each unstated record a payment of its own, so two byte-identical unstated proofs are a pair, as C reads (money[5]). Same verdict; settled by money[2] to [6]. **Housekeeping**, if anything: the fact could name the rail Module beside the payment, so that the vectors say what both verifiers assume.

**Q52. Which pairs an answer "depends on".** Both implementations define the pairs as those the computation compares: C "each record compared with the first record of each payment so far", Rust `same_payment_as` recording into `read` inside `two_ways`. Counted that way, the number of pairs is the number of payments (stated payments, and each unstated record on its own) that the records read fall into, taken two at a time where at least one is unstated, and it comes out the same in any order, which is why the two agree on money[4], [5], [8], [10] and [12]. But the text defines a pair by a trace ("whose sameness the rules giving that answer ask"), and a third implementation that compared an unstated record with every record of a payment rather than with the payment once would count more pairs and answer unknown where these two stand. **Text unclear**, lightly. Closing clause at line 206, after "Only the pairs an answer depends on count": *a pair is counted between payments, never between records: an unstated record and a payment the answer reads are one pair, however many records that payment has, and two unstated records are one pair*. Same verdict today. *Can wait:* yes, but the sentence is cheap and it is what makes "two verifiers answer alike" true of a third.

**Q53. A vow's sales: which records, which act, which state.** Agreements rule 13 and lines 3578 on, as C reads them, and Rust `vows.rs` the same, save one shared step away from Money: C states that "the rail's verification answer is not required" for a record to count in the vow question, and Rust `sale` (`vows.rs` 342, 406, 466) tests only `rail_invalid`, so a claim with no verification answer counts as a sale. Money line 183 says a client MUST NOT treat a receipt or claim as valid without a valid verification answer, and for disagreements both verifiers follow it (Q28). **Rust wrong, C wrong, and vector wrong** by omission: every sale in the vectors has a claim with a valid answer, so the fail-open path is never exercised. Closing sentence, at Agreements rule 13 or Money rule 8: *A receipt or claim whose verification answer is not valid is no purchase and no sale; where the answer is pending or not held, whether the payment is a purchase or a sale is unknown.* Vector: a vow's sale whose payer's claim carries no verification answer in the facts; expected unknown (or no sale), not a pending sale. *Can wait:* no. "Sold" on an unverified claim is a payment verdict someone ships on.

**Q54. What repays a refund.** Money rule 4 and the refund-repaid question as C reads them; Rust the same on money[11] and [12]. Not built on the C side: the bound on a refund paid to the payer's refund rail, and refunds to a bare key. Declared; the text is clear (rule 19). *Can wait:* yes, with a vector when the family allows.

## Does the first verdict hold

Yes. "Freezable, with two conditions" was the verdict when one implementation existed and a second had read the texts; the conditions are met, and the second implementation now builds the heavy rules and agrees on every case the first produces. The 24 questions contain no text wrong in substance and no divergence the texts cannot close in a sentence. Three things should be done before the rehearsal and none of them is a design: the "belongs to" sentence and the core that loses the reading (Q55); the verification answer read fail-closed for sales and purchases, with a vector (Q53); and the vectors from the chain-signature family for the gaps the C build names (Q57, Q58, Q60), so that the way out of a collective and the settling of a deal are shown twice rather than once.

Taken together, the 24 point at one weakness rather than a gap, and it is the one the first review named from the other side. Then, the heavy rules had no vectors; now they have vectors and the C build still stops short of some of them, honestly, because they are long. A rule that one implementation builds and the other declares out of scope is not shown twice, however many vectors agree, and a reader of "334 of 334" will not see that. The freeze report should carry, beside the count, the list of rules the second implementation does not build, taken from its own "What is not implemented", so that what the two agree on and what only one has built are told apart. That is a paragraph, not a change.

The second thing I would name is the pattern behind Q53, Q55 and the lone seller's purchase: in each, both implementations agree with each other and with the vectors, and the text says something else or something more. Agreement of two builders proves the texts can be read alike; it does not prove they were read. The vectors are the only thing that catches a shared misreading, and they catch it only where someone wrote the case. So the vector families should be grown by reading the texts for sentences no case exercises (Money line 183 was one), not only by reading the code for branches none reaches.
