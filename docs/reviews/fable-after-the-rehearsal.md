## In plain words

Everything the core took on since the rehearsal passes the admission test, and nothing new should move above the core: each addition is either a word every act is now written in (termination, the mark, the successor's clause) or a promise the core already made and had failed to keep (what others relied on stands; no stake moved without its holder; a receipt that counted keeps counting). What the pass did not do is say each thing once: the escape's rule is now in five places, the mark in three, termination in four, and Agreements grew by 3,800 words to 81,500, a fifth of them the same sentences again. That is a pass of words, not of meaning, and it should be done before the second round so that the players read a text that says each new thing where it lives.

On the four hard questions, taking the person who reads literally, does not negotiate and leaves without a confrontation as the one the rules must serve: the escape and the lost chain key are right as they stand, and both are answered by where a key lives, which the client and a Module must offer plainly at genesis rather than the core adding a mechanism. The veto is right to allow and wrong to leave to a cMIP's own summary: the task should produce, beside met or not yet met, a declaration in the core's own words, so that the client can show "you can never end this alone" in three lines no cMIP wrote. And the refund is where the texts still lean on the seller's side structurally: a plan can make silence pay the seller but cannot make silence refund the buyer, because the plan's key 3 has no "back to the payer". One line fixes that and lets a buyer-friendly default exist; the market can then demand it.

**Before the second round:** that line in key 3; the task's declaration and the client line that shows it; the founding non-party holder judged under the founding's clause, not "the clause in force"; and the say-it-once pass on the new sentences. The rest is fine as it stands.

## 2. Four questions

For each: who gains, who pays, whether the one who pays did nothing wrong, what the alternatives would cost, and what the literal reader who does not negotiate and needs to leave without a confrontation gets from the texts as they stand.

### 2.1 The escape: a thief's completed sale stands

**Who gains:** the buyer in good faith, and with it every buyer, since a placed sale is a sale. **Who pays:** the owner robbed of both keys, in the window before it escapes. **Did it do nothing wrong?** Nothing; it was deceived, which is how keys are most often taken. Both sides are innocent, and the core had to pick one; it picked the one who could not have known.

**The alternatives.** (a) The owner wins and the escape unmakes the sale: then every seller can unmake every sale at will, no verifier can tell an honest escape from a seller's regret, and buyers must hold every purchase provisional until the seller's next chain act, which is never scheduled. That moves the loss from one robbed owner to every buyer, and makes the stake market need escrow it has no grammar for. (b) A window: a transfer placed by chain signature completes only when an anchor shows it some blocks old with no escape at that position. The core has no clock, but it has anchors (task 11), so the rule rests on a fact anyone can check; the cost is that every sale waits, every buyer's client learns a second kind of "complete", and the window protects only an owner who notices the theft inside it, which the deceived rarely do. Possible, a mechanism, and I would not add it. (c) What stands: a dispute shown everywhere, so that the buyer's later sale carries the mark and the market prices it; the owner's loss bounded to the one sale.

**For the literal reader,** the texts are on its side in a way the question hides: a chain key is used rarely and for a few named acts (a fork, a closing, a transfer, a succession), so deception must get the owner to perform a ceremony, not click a button; the air-gapped safety key Module exists for exactly this owner; and the rule is plain, "a sale signed with your chain key is final", which a literal reader can hold. What is missing is one client line, since the rule is only as plain as the screen: *before a chain signature placing a transfer, the client shows the sale and that it is final, whatever happens to the keys afterwards.* With that, fine as it stands.

### 2.2 Termination terms may carry a veto or a minimum term

**Who gains:** the stronger party of a publication, a partner who brings visibility or a service that brings reach, who can ask for a minimum term or a veto and get it. **Who pays:** the owner who signed what it was shown and later wants out. **Did it do nothing wrong?** It signed; but the reader this question pictures signs what is put in front of it, does not negotiate, and cannot see a trap in a cMIP's parameters. The core's defence for it is what the client shows before signing; today that is "the cMIP's own declared summary", which is the cMIP describing itself.

**The alternatives.** (a) A floor in the core: termination terms may delay the owners' side's termination but never remove it. Nobody, allegedly, kept the veto out of the core and let parties bring it, and that is right under evolutionary design: a floor decides for every publication what only some want, and the market (a consumers' association is already in the rehearsal) can refuse vetoes better than the core can forbid them. (b) A floor in the task's grammar, not in the rule: task 15 produces, beside met, not yet met or unknown, **a declaration in the core's words**, for each party of the agreement: may it terminate alone, yes; after a point on the time reference; or never. The client MUST show that declaration before a signature, and MUST NOT sign terms whose cMIP produces none. The cMIP still decides what it likes; it can no longer describe it in its own words. A cMIP whose answers contradict its declaration is broken, which shows after the fact and is a stated cost, as a dishonest home is. Cost to the core: one line in the task's "produces", one client line; no rule changes. (c) Leave it: the stronger party writes the summary.

I recommend (b). It is the difference between "read the terms" and "you can never end this alone" in red, and the literal reader needs the second.

**For the literal reader who needs to leave without a confrontation,** the texts already keep one door that asks nobody: a holder transfers its own share (rule 10), to anyone, for anything, and is out; the owners may release the work (rule 55), after which the publication sells nothing. Both are stated beside termination as the way out no term reaches. They should also be on the screen: the same client line that shows the declaration shows *you can always sell your share*.

### 2.3 A broken vow's refund needs the buyer's contest, on a keeper's record

**Who gains:** sellers (a buyer who does nothing pays) and services (no custody rule beyond their cMIP's timing). **Who pays:** the buyer who was promised and not delivered and did not act by the vow's point. **Did it do nothing wrong?** It paid for a promise and received nothing; the only thing it did not do is contest in time, which is the one thing the reader in the question will not do.

**What the texts allow and what they do not.** The example in rule 22 is a seller-friendly plan: a "refund" role filled by the payer on a keeper's record of its contest, unfilled to the owners' line, so silence pays the seller. The mirror plan, buyer-friendly, would be a "delivered" role filled by the seller's side on evidence of delivery, unfilled *back to the payer*, so that silence refunds. That plan cannot be written: the plan's key 3 offers an unfilled share three destinations, the owners' line, held open, a named party, and "the payer of this payment" is not among them. So the grammar can make silence pay the seller and cannot make silence pay the buyer. That is a structural lean the core did not intend, and it falls on exactly the reader in the question.

**The alternatives.** (a) One line: a fourth destination in key 3, `[3]`, *back to the payer the payment's commitment names, at the refund rail its claim names* (Money rule 17 already has the payout). Then both defaults are expressible, a plan says which it chose, and a consumers' association can demand the buyer-friendly one. Cost: a payout kind that already exists, used in one more place; no custody rule, since the split cMIP's timing already holds the money to the vow's point. (b) A core default (silence refunds): decides for every vow what only some want, and needs the core to know what "delivered" means, which it does not. (c) Leave it: the seller's plan is the only plan.

I recommend (a). With it, a buyer's client can also refuse to pay a vow whose plan sends unfilled shares to the owners' line, which is the conformance line worth adding: *a buyer's client shows, before paying a vow, where an undelivered purchase's money goes.* The keeper's record stays where it is: a contest, or a delivery, must be recorded by someone the agreement named, and that is the right price for the buyer's word not deciding alone.

### 2.4 A lost chain key is lost for good

**Who gains:** everyone's security, since a recovery path is an attack path, and the identity itself, which no court, home or module can reassign. **Who pays:** the owner who lost it, who did nothing wrong. **The alternatives.** (a) Recovery in the core, social or otherwise: the thing that decides is then signed by someone other than the owner, which is the first of the four patterns, and every attack on it is an attack on every identity. (b) Nothing, as now: the identity freezes; its stakes are reachable by the absence clause (a collective) or the absence rules (a deal), so the market moves on and only the owner is locked out.

**What the texts already have, which the question does not mention:** succession is a rotation, and a rotation can be signed in advance. An owner holding its chain key may sign a rotation naming its successor and hold it, or have it held, to be published if the key is lost; nothing in Identity forbids it, and position competition handles it (it names the latest chain act, so it must be re-signed after each one, which is rare). That is a **standing succession**, and it is a Module's: who holds the pre-signed act, on what proof of loss it is released, and how the custodian is kept from releasing it at will are custody questions, which the air-gapped safety key Module already lives among. No task is needed: the released act is an ordinary rotation. The cost is the custodian's power, which is why it is a choice and not a rule.

**For the literal reader,** the right answer is at genesis, not in a rule: the client offers, when the chain key is made, where it will live and whether a standing succession is set up, in plain words. One italic sentence in Identity section 8 saying a succession may be signed in advance and held, and that this is a Module's, is all the core should add; the rest is fine as it stands.

## 3. What should change before a second round, and what is fine

**Change:**

1. **Plan key 3, a fourth destination:** `[3]`, back to the payer the payment's commitment names, at the refund rail its claim names. One line in the format and a clause in rule 22. It makes the buyer-friendly vow writable and removes the only structural lean I found.
2. **Task 15 produces a declaration** in the core's words (for each party: may terminate alone; after a point on the time reference; never), and **the client shows it** before a signature, beside *you can always sell your share*. One line in the task's "produces" in Agreements section 22 and Development's table, one client line.
3. **Rule 85's residue:** "A holder that neither signed a version as a party, bought in nor succeeded is judged under the clause in force." That is a holder named in the stake at founding without being a party, and it is R2's hole for exactly that holder, who accepted nothing and can be reached by any later clause. Under "whatever the holder held is what is being passed", the stake was created under the founding, so: *judged under the founding version's clause.* One clause.
4. **The say-it-once pass on the new sentences:** the escape's rule in one place (rule 34), cited in four; the mark in the format, cited twice; termination in Part one, cited in rules 11 and 47 and once in the MICS; "the same evidence not promised" once; receipt check 5's cost moved to section 13. About 2,500 words, meaning unchanged, so that the second round reads a text that says each new thing where it lives.
5. **Two client lines**, both italics: before a chain signature placing a transfer, the sale shown as final; and, in Identity section 8, that a succession may be signed in advance and held, a Module's.

**Fine as it stands:** the escape's outcome (the buyer stands, the dispute shown); receipts and audit across rotations; the absence clause per holder and the successor's clause, residue aside; the deal holder's sealing; the claim sentence; the collective's succession by chain signature; termination's definition, defaults and mark, and the veto allowed; a lost chain key lost for good; the refund built above the core from the plan and the split cMIP's timing, once both defaults can be written.
