## In plain words: the ten worst breaks, ranked by harm

1. **The keeper the seller runs is the shield's lock, and the seller holds the key.** Nothing stops the drafter naming their own relay as the agreement's keeper, or two relays they run as its two keepers. The keeper never signs "nothing later": its record is a list of what it shows, so a shorter copy and a longer copy never contradict (5.8.3's new rule catches only two signed answers that contradict). The seller shows a short record to one reader and a long one to another, and both answers are **per keeper**. The seller lets the keeper die, and every filling under the agreement is **unchecked** for anyone who kept no copy of its record, which nobody is told to keep ("It’s not something I would specify."); then the seller opens a new agreement that names the old one. The protected thing can no longer be shown; the unprotected one can.
2. **The shield is on the mechanism, not on the person.** "Agreements protects the unknown parties" (§0), but an unknown party is "a signer who takes part through a role" (§3.2). A seller who names each buyer as a signer in a version of her own, with a one-party update condition, sells to a known party, and the next version binds her by 5.6.1(c). A buyer who filled a role under version 1 is named as a signer by version 2 (valid without her), renews under it, and her renewal is "a known party's act", which the next version reaches. No byte tells a co-drafter from a buyer named for the purpose. His words allow it ("The rule used is meant for known parties"); the introduction's promise does not.
3. **One known party still un-signs an update in force, now by the text's own words.** 5.12.3 says a fork of a signer's line made after an update "does not reach back", and that the fork is made after the update "where the tip that competes ... is shown after acts enough to put the update in force"; "where nothing shows that, the update is **not shown** as in force". Ben forks his line with a second act that names nothing recent and that he never lodges at the keeper: a fork is seen "whatever a keeper shows" (5.7.1), and nothing shows it was made after. In a bare agreement the text gives **not shown** outright; with a keeper, two honest verifiers differ. Then Ben resolves his own fork, continuing whichever tip suits him.
4. **"Per keeper" names a reader-relative answer as the Terms' own.** A keeper's signed record "counts wherever a copy is held" (5.8.4), and a keeper that no longer shows anything is **unchecked** (§3.6) and **not shown** (§3.6, 5.8.3) in the same section. Which version is in force per a dead keeper depends on which copy a verifier holds; the text has no "latest" for a record and no word for a keeper's death. And where two keepers disagree, the deadlock has no door: a fork has a resolving update, a keepers' disagreement has nothing, and "An answer **not shown** for want of a keeper changes only when that keeper shows it" (5.8.3).
5. **A known party who fills a spot may lose their veto.** 5.5.5(a) and 5.5.7(e) say the default signers are "never a signer who fills a role". Read as written, a named signer who also fills a role is no longer a default signer; the other known party updates alone, and the update binds the filler by 5.6.1(c). Scenario 8.29 says the words "do not choose". A seller who offers a co-owner a bonus spot buys their veto with it.
6. **The keeper's record has no bytes, so every answer per keeper is unchecked today.** §3.4 defines it "for this text's own use"; Identity's rule 34 cites "the keeper record, type 2" of draft 24; encoding 3 writes Terms and Statements only, and leaves what a keeper's identifier is the hash of open (E6). K9 is asked on "the keepers' records the verifier holds": there is no record a verifier can hold. The same shape as the fifth attack's reports.
7. **A power in the Terms being signed cannot be used on them, or can: two answers.** 5.4.5 provides for an agent's assent to Terms not yet in force and for a counter signature under a power; 5.6.1(b) binds through a power only where `under` names Terms "which were in force when the act was made". A power written into version 1 and used to complete or cancel version 1 binds under one reading and never under the other. The lead's reading 8 of 20:55 chose the second; 5.4.5 still reads as the first.
8. **A power widens itself, now in so many words.** 5.6.5(f) makes an agent's assent to an update "that party's signing", and 5.6.1(b) counts "an assent counted in their name" as "their own signature ... on the version ... that gives the power". The fifth attack's reading B is the text; Ana "took the risk" of a hash in `powers`. Unchanged; stated.
9. **The byte choices that change a verifier's answer are still outside the hash.** The set's label and joining, a keeper's identifier, the K9 socket's key and form, which spec hashes are `TEXT`, how a cMIP declares a need, and the field a new first act names an old agreement in: open in encoding 3. Two verifiers under one `SPEC_DRAFT_26` still answer **pass** and **rejected**, or **pass** and **invalid**, to the same bytes; and encoding 3's E17 now tells a writer to put the shared version in a resolving Terms act's `previous`, which E23 and 5.7.1 make **invalid**.
10. **The way out of a dead keeper doubles the parties' bonds and strands the fillings.** "Dead" has no bytes: a keeper that is slow and one that is gone give the same silence. Parties who make the new agreement while the keeper is slow are bound by both once it shows their old update (5.8.3: "no update of it and no fork with it"). The fillings cannot follow: they stand only where a copy shows them, under a seller free to offer no spot in the new agreement. 5.5.5(d)'s "and none will" is a forecast no verifier can check.

The shape under them, as I read it (INFERRED): draft 26 made the keeper the agreement's order and the gate's witness, and made the unknown party's shield turn on the keeper showing her proof. ## 3. What the principle cannot protect, and what it protects too much

**Cannot protect** (each traced above): her proof's placement at a keeper the seller chose and may run (2.1.1); her filling once the keeper dies and nobody kept a copy (2.1.9, 2.2.1); her class, where the seller names her as a signer or she renews under a version that does (2.5.1); the K9 cMIP she cannot read, which decides which keeper's order is hers (2.1.6); her words, withheld, private or voided (2.9); her term, on the seller's clock or none (2.10); her share under a later version's K6 and the dissolve-and-re-form exit (the fifth attack's 2.1.4, 2.1.5, unchanged). In every one the acts and the keeper the grammar reads were chosen before she arrived.

**3.1 Protects too much: the shield is a cage, still.** She cannot leave (the fifth attack's 3.3); a defective version cannot be fixed for her, even in her favour (2.5.4); a thief's filling is permanent (the fifth attack's 3.4). Unchanged.

**3.2 Protects too much: the known party who fills a spot, the other way round.** The fifth attack's 3.1 (an insider's filling nothing reaches) is closed: a known party's filling is a known party's act (§3.2). What took its place is 1.4's reading B: the insider may lose their veto by filling. Protection moved from too much to too little in one sentence.

**3.3 Protects too much: the publisher.** The publisher cannot propose without being bound (2.4.1). "No unsigned act" protects whoever reads Terms from a drafter who is not party to them; it costs every drafter the draft.

**3.4 Protects what should not be protected: a dead agreement's last version per a stale copy.** 2.1.9(c): a seller who holds an old copy of a dead keeper's record keeps selling under a version the keeper had seen replaced. The copy is genuine; "what a keeper signed stands" (5.8.4).
