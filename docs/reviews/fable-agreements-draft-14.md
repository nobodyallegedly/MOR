## In plain words

The idea is good and the grammar is close. An agreement that can be a party, acts that name what they relied on, and reports that go one leg and no further: those three carry almost everything, and a stranger can see why each is there.

Three things stand between this text and a verifier.

1. **The central block is half built.** The text says an agreement can sign, and does not say by which act. Everything that makes a collective, a deal or a delegation by a group rests on that.
2. **The text protects whoever arrives later better than whoever was there at the start.** A buyer keeps exactly what they bought. An owner named in the agreement has no such sentence.
3. **It is not yet a text a stranger reads without its history.** About a fifth of it is rules. The rest is how they came to be.

On the wish: yes, one click can pay everyone, built on this. No, the one who clicks cannot see that it did, and the text says so itself.

## 1. The wish

**One click can reward everyone who contributed, where each of them is reachable through agreements and holds a key.** The blocks for it are in the grammar, and they are few: "Whatever is owed to an agreement goes to its receiver. Where the receiver is itself an agreement, that agreement is the next leg"; "a payment names the version of the work it read"; and the leg report. A film that names its songs as parties, each song naming its band, is a chain of receivers, and each leg divides by its own cMIP. That is the wish, built.

Where it cannot:

| Where | Whose it is |
|---|---|
| **The one who clicks cannot know it reached everyone.** "Whether the money reached everyone beyond is not shown to the buyer by default." | The grammar's, by choice. Seeing further is a data cMIP, and needs the signature of the people seen. The text's own open list calls this "open, and sharper". I think the choice is right, and that the wish as worded ("can I … reward") is met by paying, not by seeing. That reading is for Nobody, allegedly. |
| **Nothing makes a service divide as the shares say.** "Nothing in the grammar makes a service divide as the shares say, or pay at all." | A cMIP's, rightly. A hand is caught by its own report and the receiver's, not stopped. |
| **Whether the division matched the shares** is read only by the leg's cMIP; without it the answer is unchecked. | A cMIP's. The leg report stays readable, which is what rule 11.3 is for. |
| **Pools** (P6): money that gathers before it is divided. | Open, and partly the grammar's: "what a leg report (rule 11.3) names as received where what it passes on comes out of a pool" is a question about the block. |
| **Receivers that point onward, and loops** (P8). | The grammar's. "to what depth one receiver may point onward to another, and what a loop answers" must be said before anyone can find where the money physically goes. |
| **A contributor with no key**: "can be named in words but is no party, and nothing can arrive for them." | Nobody's. It is the edge of signing. |
| **A work that names no receiver** "cannot be paid". | The work's own choice. |
| **The payment must carry the version it read.** | Money's. The text says so. |

One sentence overstates: "The payer pays the work and needs to know nothing else." A payer who is to be recognised as having bought something fills a spot, and a spot is read by its cMIP. Without that cMIP the payer has paid and holds an answer of **unchecked**. That is honest under rule 9; the sentence should not promise more.

## 2. Blocks or solutions

### What still solves something

- **The buyer's contest (rule 11.8).** "These grammar rules read a buyer's contest only there: where what was bought was not delivered after payment." And: "A verifier MUST NOT read it as contesting a service those signers picked". A contest is a Statement made within the agreement the buyer bought under; by that alone it reaches that agreement and no other. The rest is a policy about who is blamed first, and a verifier computes nothing from it. This is a solution, and a cMIP of kind (K11) could say all of it.
- **The condition (rule 10.7).** One field, one rule, and an answer of its own, "no longer in force, by its own condition". But rule 10.6 says of rights: "these grammar rules do not read them". So the grammar gives a verdict on something it declares it does not read. The spot's cMIP (K12) already answers whether a spot takes new people; it could answer whether a filling's rights have stopped. P21 ("whether a version may name several") is the strain showing.
- **What a leg report carries (K8's row), and "MUST NOT read it as kept".** The five amounts are a format for one use, passing money on. A common format is defensible, since rule 9 would otherwise leave a sequence unreadable. But it is a format of the shared language or of Money, not of a grammar of agreeing; and the holder's own part is a fix for one case (a band member who receives for the band).
- **The buyer's leg (rule 11.7).** "By default, with no call written in any Terms, that receiver's confirmation that the payment was received is expected by the buyer." This is a default report with no call, in a block whose rule is "A report that names no call is no report" (R2). The two texts disagree.
- **The baker (rule 14)** I do not count as a solution. No cMIP can make a stranger's agreement invalid, because the stranger does not name your cMIP. It passes both questions. It is the only rule here that does what only a grammar can.

### Blocks that are missing, which no cMIP could supply

- **How an agreement signs.** The text's own words: "By which acts that signing is shown, and what it binds that agreement's own people to, are not said here (P17)." And `for` "holds a person". So rule 1.2, rule 4.4, rule 4.10, rule 5.5 for a delegator that is an agreement, and rule 11.2's call all rest on an act that does not exist. A cMIP cannot supply it, because it is what rules 4.1 and 5.1 read. This is the one I would settle first: it is the text's "central idea".
- **A named party's own part against a later version.** P15: "Whether a party named as a signer … also keeps exactly what its version offered against a later version made under a power is not said". See section 5. No cMIP can supply it, because the power is what was signed and the cMIP is chosen by whoever drafted.
- **What a fork removes** (P9): "whether a signer can make their own assent uncountable by forking on purpose, is open." Until it is said, forking one's own line may be a way to stop being bound. That is a hole in being bound, not a parameter.
- **Two joins.** A fork of Terms made under a power that lets fewer than all replace them is joined by "those the shared Terms require for their replacement", which is the same power. Two groups can each sign a join. "A join is not a branch", so two joins are not a fork, and the text has no word for what they are.

## 3. A stranger

**The rules can be understood without the history. The file cannot be read without wading through it.** The opening says the text is "written to be read by someone who knows nothing of how it came to be", and the reasoning under each rule then tells how it came to be: "Earlier drafts went further", "The MOR lead settled it", "draft 13's item 21". Section 8.2 is a list of what changed since draft 13.

Where a careful stranger would get lost, or read two ways:

1. **"Leg" means two things.** "Leg: one agreement of a sequence", and also a path with two sides. Then: "Paying is the buyer's own leg". The buyer's leg is not an agreement of a sequence. The report block has a third wording: "two sides who both signed". A buyer and a receiver signed nothing together.
2. **"Receiver" means three things**: the field in Terms; the side of a report that something was passed on to; and "receiver now". Rule 11.2 uses all three in one paragraph.
3. **"Receiver now" means receiver then.** It is "the `receiver` named by the version … which the act that gives or owes names". The word says the opposite of the rule.
4. **"Sequence" means two things**: money passing from work to work, and the baker's rights passing from holder to holder. One runs down to those a work stands on; the other runs forward in time.
5. **"Rule" means two things**: a grammar rule, and a cMIP answering K4. "as its rule says" and "rule 5" sit lines apart.
6. **"Chain", "line", "sequence".** The text admits "chain" is still used three ways.
7. **"In force"** is said of Terms and of a filling, with different tests.
8. **Invalid, or not shown.** Invalid is where "the acts held show that these grammar rules do not let it count". Then rule 4.8 answers invalid "per place or as shown, since an assent it does not hold may exist". By the definition that is *not shown*.
9. **Rule 4.10 reads two ways, and says so.** "available only until every party has signed, which these grammar rules read as: until those Terms are in force". Where Terms come into force on fewer signatures, the two differ.
10. **Who must sign a replacement.** Rule 4.1 counts "every party named as a signer"; rule 4.8 asks for "every party bound by them". Someone who filled a spot and assented is bound and is not "named as a signer".
11. **Whether Terms hold the Control task** is decided, in rule 11.2, by whether they "give a part to any other party". Shares have no form in the grammar ("The grammar gives them no form"), so a verifier without the body's cMIP cannot tell, and the text does not say that the answer is then unchecked.
12. **"a reader sees, before paying, which legs called"**. No rule makes anything be shown to anyone. The word "client" does not occur in the text.

## 4. The way out

**Leaving: it does not hold, and the text says so.** "No default way out." A party leaves only by a power the Terms gave, or when "everyone signs again". "If a party is gone", the agreement holds where it is. The text gives one default exit, to a delegator, "because … it is the way out for whoever delegates". The same reasoning applies to a party who wants to go, and no default is given to them. I judge this the grammar's, not a cMIP's: a default is what applies where the Terms are silent, and silence is where the person who does not negotiate stands.

**Ending a delegation: it holds between the two, and not against a third.** The delegator ends it alone by default; that is clear. But an act an agent makes after the delegation was replaced is read by rule 5.4 "per place if the Terms name one, otherwise as shown". Without a place, an agent can go on acting and whoever relies on it cannot know. I did not find that among the stated costs.

And a delegation "only the agent may end" is allowed: "A verifier MUST NOT answer that such a delegation can never be ended: whether the agent will end it is **not shown**". Mechanically sound. It is also a power over a person that the person can never take back. Section 5.

**A fork and its join: it holds, with two gaps.** Shown, never chosen; the Terms before it stay in force; what others relied on stands, as disputed. Those are right. The gaps are in section 2: two joins, and what a fork removes. And one cost is larger than its sentence: under a power that lets fewer than all replace Terms, any group able to use it can sign a rival replacement and so hold the agreement where it was. A fork is a veto.

**An agreement that never ends: it holds from the text.** "a verifier MUST NOT answer that Terms or an agreement have ended". It is consistent, and the example (owners who made a work free, one of whom then sells it alone) shows what it buys. What it costs is that nothing a named party owes can stop, in the grammar's eyes, except by a new version: the `condition` of rule 10.7 is read for fillings only.

## 5. Who it serves

**Where the text protects the person who reads literally and does not negotiate:**

- "Anything named otherwise is not part of it" (rule 2): no side piece, no document that changes behind them.
- "Whoever wrote them carries their unclearness" (rule 3).
- "Signing first does not bind you alone", and the counter signature (rule 4).
- "Nobody is bound by an act they did not sign" (rule 5), and "It MUST NOT later be counted as made for anyone else."
- "no later version changes them without the holder's own signature" (rule 10.6): what a buyer bought stays bought.
- "a payment names the version of the work it read" (rule 13): a payer is never told afterwards that they paid the wrong one.
- "People are seen from beyond their leg only where they agreed to it in an agreement they signed" (rule 11). For someone who does not see the trap nobody names, a default of not being watched is the strongest protection in the text.
- K3's "never a pass": an assurance never becomes proof that someone understood.

**Where it quietly favours the stronger or better advised:**

1. **The owner is protected less than the buyer.** Rule 10.6 guards a filling against every later version. For a party named in the Terms, P15 says it "is not said". So a co-owner who signed Terms giving a power of replacement to fewer than all can have their share rewritten under it. "Terms rule, because they were agreed by all signers". The person who drafted the power knew what it did. **This cannot be a cMIP's protection.** It needs one sentence of grammar, or it needs Nobody, allegedly to say that it is meant.
2. **"The grammar puts no limit on what such a signed condition may say."** A power given for ever, which only the one who holds it may end. "It is legible" is the text's defence. Legible to whoever reads the body through its cMIP; that is the better advised.
3. **No default exit** (section 4).
4. **"where its rules say the majority decides, the majority decides"**: an outvoted member is seen from beyond their leg. They signed the rule. They may not have seen what it would be used for.
5. **A contest reaches one leg.** The buyer "depends on them to contest the service in turn; nothing makes them".
6. **A future spot is "the authors' word".**
7. **What an agreement's signing binds its own people to "is not said".** Inside a group, that silence serves whoever holds the pen.
8. **Nothing is shown to anyone before they sign.** The protections above work for someone who reads the Terms through the right cMIPs. What a person is shown is no rule here and no task. That may rightly be a client's or a cMIP's. But then the text should say that this protection is nobody's rule, as it says of other costs.

## 6. Size

**The grammar is small. The text is not.**

By my count the file is 36,500 words. The numbered sentences a verifier applies come to about 6,800 words in 76 items. Rule 11 alone is 6,400 words. Each rule is told three times: in section 0, in its own plain words, and precisely.

As a set of rules it is near the size of what it does: two acts, some thirty fields, fourteen rules, thirteen tasks. I would call three parts larger than their work: rule 11.7 and 11.8 (section 2); rule 10.7; and the parts of rule 11 that say again what the block says.

And it is too small in one place: the act by which an agreement signs.

## 7. Ready for a box?

**No.** A stranger could not build a verifier from this text alone. What must exist first, in order:

1. **The shared language it leans on.** Section 8.1 lists what the text "needs from the other layers, and does not have": the act format, the fingerprint, what a place can answer, what a clock is, the envelope, the settlement, how an amount is written. And the report block, which is "a proposal, not a decision, and not yet written into any spec". A report has no fields in this text: "this text gives it no fields of its own."
2. **Five open points that decide verdicts**, not parameters: how an agreement signs (P17); what a fork removes (P9); what a verifier holds, a set given or one it must ask for (P5); depth of powers through powers (P2); depth and loops of receivers (P8).
3. **The tasks as interfaces.** "Accepts" and "produces" are in words. A box needs each task's inputs and outputs as data, so that a stub cMIP can stand in.
4. **The two disagreements with the block**: a default confirmation with no call (rule 11.7 against R2), and who the two sides are ("who both signed" against a buyer who signed nothing with the receiver).
5. **The rules alone, as one text**: the 76 items without their plain words and reasoning, each word with one meaning (section 3).
6. **Vectors.** There are none.

What is ready now, given only a core act format, is the part with no agreement as a party and no report: rules 2, 4 for signers, 5 for signers, 6, 12 and the order from citing of rule 13.3. That is a real verifier of "who is bound by which Terms", and a small box could seal it.

## 8. After reading the rest

*Written after sections 1 to 7, which were not changed.*

**What does not change.** Sections 1, 3, 6 and 7 stand as written. The click tests ran the grammar and found the money reaching everyone, three times; that agrees with section 1. The third test found by running what I found by reading: "A delegate can't say whom she signs for … Draft 14's `for` field holds a person". And the first two tests explain why rule 11 is as long as it is: each of its additions answers something a test broke.

**Where my view changes.**

1. **I missed the plainest case of section 5, and the test found it.** A band that owns 70% of a song is "named as an agreement", so by default it "isn't counted when the song is put in force or replaced". The producer alone sets and changes the shares. I had written that an owner is protected less than a buyer; I had not seen that an owner which is a group is, by default, not asked at all. The test's own sentence is the sharper one: "The grammar can't tell the two apart", an agreement pointed at and an agreement that owns.

2. **Nobody, allegedly has answered it, and his answer reaches further than that case.** "That deal could exist… but it's not the only option. I think we're fixing small problems often in the same way. The answer is often: you did not use the right cMIP then." The ledger's line under it, arrived at and still to check: "no change to the grammar". That answer applies equally to three things I called missing or unprotected: a named party's part against a later version (P15), a party who wants to leave, and a power that only its holder may end. In each, Terms that said otherwise could have been signed. So I withdraw "cannot be a cMIP's" as I wrote it in section 5, item 1. A rule named in the Terms can protect an owner's part.

   What I keep is narrower, and I put it to him as one question, not three fixes. **The grammar already gives defaults where Terms are silent, in three places, each time for the way out:** a delegator ends a delegation alone; an assent can be taken back until the Terms are in force; a filling keeps the rights of its version. It gives none in two others: a party's own part, and a party's own leaving. "you did not use the right cMIP" is a fair answer to a builder. It is a harder answer to the person of question 5, who by definition did not choose the cMIP. Whether those two belong with the three is his to say. I only show that the text is not even-handed between them.

3. **The privacy I praised has a hole inside the leg.** I called "seen from beyond their leg only where they agreed" the strongest protection in the text. The third test shows that within one leg "the song's service, paid by the film's service, is shown the film's whole split, with every owner's amount, though it signed nothing in the film's deal", and, read the other way, "money owed under a minimum is reported to nobody". Leg by leg holds between legs. Who the two sides of a leg are is not settled, and until it is, the protection is weaker than rule 11's sentence. This is section 3, item 1, with a cost attached.

4. **How an agreement signs is nearer than I said.** The ledger, after draft 14: "a delegate signing agreement y under agreement x signs on behalf of x's parties: a collective signature, under the power x gave. The members signed y through x". That is rule 5.1(b) doing the work, and it answers what a signing "binds its own people to". What is left of my first missing block is smaller: letting `for` name an agreement, and saying this once in the text. It is still first on the list for a box.

5. **The contest costs more than rule 11.8 shows.** "by the time the buyer contests, the money has already gone down every leg", and "nothing brings back what went further". That does not change my judgment that 11.8 is a solution a cMIP could state. It adds a line to section 1: one click rewards everyone at once, and so cannot be taken back from everyone.

6. **The buyer's leg has a reason I did not know.** The second test found that "The payer sees nothing after paying", not even the first leg. Rule 11.7 is the answer. The disagreement with the block's R2 (a report with no call) stands, but it is a seam to close, not a solution to remove.
