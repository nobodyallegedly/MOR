## In plain words first

F234 wants one thing, and the want is right: a way to sell something before its bytes exist, with a name that offers, claims, permissions and later acts can point at from the first moment. Nobody, allegedly, said it well: "MOR truly needed a structured way of selling something that has no bytes, or no bytes yet." The stream decisions (F230 to F232) are the worked example, and most of them hold. The trouble is in the three sentences that generalise the example to everything: *a claim on the announcement covers what it became*, *a ticket sold twice is caught as F224 catches a stake sold twice*, and *an announcement is an IOU*. Each promises something the core cannot keep as written, and two of them hand a power to the announcer that the rest of the protocol withholds from everyone.

The deepest problem is the first sentence. A work claim today proves one thing: the claimant held the bytes when the claim was made, and anchoring fixes when. A claim on an announcement proves nothing about bytes, by F231's own words ("reserves a name, not content"). If that claim then *covers* whatever the closing act names, an announcement becomes a claim with a blank for the work, filled in later by the announcer alone. Anchor the blank early, fill it in late, and you hold the earliest claim on anything you care to name. Rule 15 decides priority "where claims meet"; the text must say that a claim reaching a work through a closing meets other claims at the closing, never at the announcement, or the announcement is a backdated claim on anything.

What I found, by weight:

1. **An announcement is a backdated claim on anything** unless the covering claim's point is the closing's. BREAKS.
2. **The closing is the announcer's act, but it decides what the creators' claim binds.** A non-creator changes what a claim covers, and the creators are frozen at the announcement. CONFLICTS with rule 15 and F216.
3. **"A ticket sold twice is caught as F224 catches a stake sold twice" is false.** A seat is not a stake; the 501st payment is a purchase like the first, and nothing counts. BREAKS the sentence; the stance is a COST to state, or a quantity to add.
4. **When is an announcement's sale complete?** Rule 32 as written completes it at the seller's receipt, so an IOU is a finished sale of a promise with no open obligation; the other reading leaves it open with no delivery point in any format. UNCLEAR, with a cost either way.
5. **"Computed, never declared" overclaims for the recording.** A verifier without the segments' keys and the cMIP takes the closing's word. Rule 14a's shape is what the core can keep, and with it streams differ from other kinds by one sentence. CONFLICTS.
6. **Two closings, a closing naming a segment off the chain, and what a closing does to open offers** are not said. UNCLEAR.
7. **"Something MOR cannot hash" is every good and every service**, so announcements capture case studies 12 and 16 wholesale, and the offer gains a third form for one task. UNCLEAR, against the Modules rule.
8. **F230's stated cost misstates what a rotation voids**: the opener's own acts, never the name. COST, to restate.
9. **The definition of a purchase lives in five places; F234 lists one.** The mark on the name is absent from F234's list. CONFLICTS, editorial, mandatory.
10. **What held**: delivery for things that become works (rule 32 and 14 already answer); the payer's acknowledgement as the shape for the rest (F193); refunds under terms (F215, F219); naming the announcement in the offer closes the access gap my last review found; the common case carries no new rule, only a parsing surface.

Where I tried to break things and could not, I say what I tried.

## How findings are marked

- **BREAKS**: an attack works, or a common case does not work, as written.
- **COST**: the rule works and someone pays; the cost is to be stated, or is stated.
- **CONFLICTS**: two texts, or a text and the code, disagree.
- **UNCLEAR**: the text can be read two ways, or a verifier cannot check it as written.
- **HOLDS**: I tried an attack and it failed; I say what I tried.

Each finding gives what breaks, the smallest story, who gains and whether they can pull it, the rule and the line, and the test where one bears on it. Ordered by weight. Line numbers are of main at `17726e9`.

## 1. An announcement is a backdated claim on anything. BREAKS (F234: "a claim on the announcement covers what it became"; rule 15, Agreements line 654: "priority can only be decided where they meet: by anchoring, or by a keeper that recorded both"; F231: "a claim on a live work before it runs reserves a name, not content")

**What breaks.** A work claim with an anchor proves that the claimant held the bytes before a point. A claim on an announcement proves that the claimant held a *name* before a point, and F231 already says so: for a live work, "order is not evidence even of content". F234 then says the claim on the announcement covers what the closing names. Nothing says at which point the covering claim meets other claims on that work. Rule 15 has one answer today, the claim's own anchor or keeper record, and that is the announcement's point.

**The smallest story.** Mara announces "an album, next year", claims the announcement, and anchors the claim on the Bitcoin clock in January. In June a band she has never met publishes and claims a record that sells. In July Mara closes her announcement naming the band's work hash. Under F234 her claim now covers the band's work; the two claims meet by anchoring; hers is anchored in January, theirs in June. A verifier reading rule 15 as it stands gives Mara priority. Who gains: anyone who keeps a drawer of anchored announcements with vague words. Can they pull it: yes, for the price of one anchor per announcement, and the words may be "as sloppy as they wish" (F232, line 5). The band's recourse is a contest and the evidence that the closing came after their claim, which is exactly the evidence rule 15 says decides priority, pointed the other way.

**What the test pins.** `today_a_closing_act_naming_what_an_announcement_became_is_read_by_nobody`: Mara's claim on the announcement reaches no work a closing names, and a stranger's claim on that work binds uncontested. The build will change this on purpose; the question is at which point the derived claim is dated.

**What I would put to him.** One sentence, in rule 15 beside F231's: a claim that reaches a work through a closing act meets other claims on that work at the closing, never at the announcement; the announcement's anchor dates the name, the closing's anchor dates the content. It is the practice's own rule (F207's lesson: every "before" names the sequence it is read on), applied to the one place F234 leaves it unnamed.

## 2. The closing is the announcer's act, but it decides what the creators' claim binds; the creators are frozen at the announcement. CONFLICTS (rule 15: "A work is bound to its creators only by an explicit work claim"; core v21, "Stakes": "Any act that changes a stake needs its holder's signature"; F216: "The owners have to all sign … according to the rules of their deal"; F234: "A closing act its signer makes ends it and names what it became")

**What conflicts.** Two identities can stand behind one announcement: its signer (the announcer, the carrier of the thing to come) and the creators its claim names. For a stream they are the same (F231: the opener's segments, the opener's claim), which is why F232 reads cleanly. For a preorder through a label, a commission through a gallery, a concert through a promoter, they are not. F234 gives the closing to the signer alone and gives the covering to the claim. So a non-creator's act decides what the creators' claim binds.

**The smallest story.** A label announces the band's second album; the deal between them writes a stake in the announcement, the band's claim on it names the band as creators (reserving the name, F231). The band records. The label closes the announcement naming a cut the band never approved, or a different band's record it also publishes. The band's claim now covers that work, by F234, with no act of theirs. Who gains: the announcer over the creators; a carrier claims, which rule 15 forbids ("a publication is a neutral carrier"). Can they pull it: yes; the closing is "its signer's" act and needs no one else.

**The second half.** A work claim names its creators (field 1) and binds when every creator listed has signed (rule 15, the format at line 590). The claim on an announcement names the creators of a thing that does not exist. A session musician who joins in April is not among them; the album she played on inherits a claim that leaves her out, and her own claim on the album makes it contested among its own makers. For a standard work this cannot happen: the claim is made when the bytes exist and names who made them.

**What I would put to him.** The closing act is where the work's claim is made: a work claim (type 3) by the creators, completed by all of them as any claim is, citing the announcement in `objects`, under the announcement's agreement, which carries its stakes over to the work it names. "A claim on the announcement covers what it became" then means: the agreement that claimed the announcement claims the work the closing names, and the creators sign the closing as they would any claim. The announcer, where it is not a creator, carries the announcement as a publisher carries a publication (F216): a neutral carrier, its stake in the announcement's agreement, never in the work. For a stream nothing changes, since opener and creator are one.

## 3. "A ticket sold twice is caught as F224 catches a stake sold twice" is false; over-selling is counted by nobody. BREAKS the sentence; COST or a quantity (F233, the project lead's flag; F224, Agreements line 653; the offer format, line 562; rule 32a, line 694)

**What breaks.** F224 works because a stake is a number in millionths, a transfer is a chain signature on the seller's own line, and every verifier computes holdings alike, so an over-sale "confers nothing, and its payment is owed back". None of that exists for a ticket. A purchase is the buyer's payment naming an offer; it is on the buyer's rail and in the seller's receipt, not on the seller's identity line; the offer carries no quantity; and nothing in the core counts payments under an offer against anything.

**The smallest story.** The club announces a concert in a hall of 500 seats and sells access under a lone seller's offer whose words say "500 seats". Six hundred fans pay. Who gains: the club, by a hundred tickets. Can they pull it: yes, and the trail is six hundred receipts beside a sentence, which no verifier reads. The hundred turned away at the door have the offer's words and F219.

**What the test pins.** `today_an_access_offer_sells_without_count_so_a_limited_announcement_is_sold_past_its_limit`: the first and the last payment are purchases alike, and the club holds no stake in the concert that a sale could exhaust.

**What I would put to him.** Two honest answers. (A) The stance of F217 and F224, "legible, not banned", needs something to read: a quantity on the offer (a field) and a number on each receipt the seller signs under it, one numbering per offer as DQ6 numbers a service's splits; a receipt above the quantity confers nothing and its payment is owed back, a gap is the buyer's alarm. It works on request rails, where the seller receipts each payment; on push rails each holder's receipt numbers itself and the count is the seller's word, which is the push rail's cost everywhere (rule 32a). (B) No count in the core: "500 seats" is words, the ticketing Module counts and the door refuses, the refund is under the terms. Either is consistent; F233's sentence is neither, and should be withdrawn from the record of what was checked.

## 4. When is an announcement's sale complete? UNCLEAR, with a cost either way (rule 32, Agreements line 693: "The deal is complete when the offer's side delivers the content key or, for a publication whose key is public, receipts the payment naming the offer"; freeze suite line 81: "key delivery or delivery confirmation as the binding moment; refund owed otherwise"; F232, line 3; F234: "How delivery or attendance is confirmed is a Module's")

**What is unclear.** Rule 32 knows two completions: the key delivered, or the seller's receipt naming the offer where there is no key to deliver. An announcement has no key to deliver when sold. Read literally, the seller's receipt completes the sale, and "that proves payment was verified and honoured": a preorder is a finished sale of a promise, with no open obligation anywhere in MOR, and "IOU" is the wrong word in the core's own terms, since an IOU is an open obligation and this one never opens. Read the other way, the sale stays open until what the closing names is delivered, and a refund is owed meanwhile.

**The cost of each reading.** Under the first, non-delivery is never a state the core reports; the buyer's recourse is the offer's words and its refund terms (F215, F219), which is what Nobody, allegedly, said for streams ("Refunds are a case by case business between participants and the terms are what was agreed"). Under the second, every preorder a collective sells is an open debt until delivery; a collective closes only when it owes nothing (Agreements, "Closing", line 89: "every debt paid or released by its creditor"), so a label that sold preorders of an album it never made cannot close until its refund terms lapse, and with no refund terms (an offer's field 7 is optional) never. The second reading is arguably right and must be chosen with that cost in view.

**What neither reading has.** A delivery point. The offer's `until` (field 5) is when it stops taking payment; its refund terms (field 7) are until when a refund is *claimable*; neither is "by when it is delivered". F225's form kept the deadlines in the cMIP's terms the offer carries (`[2, [anchoring cMIP, terms]]`, "their deadlines are points on the offer's own time reference", line 581), so a default was judgeable. F234's form carries a description in words. So a seller writes refund terms that lapse before the delivery the words promise, and no client can flag it: not bad faith as F219 pictured it (a deadline too short to use) but a structural gap, since the two points live in different places and only one is machine-read. Rule 33 then applies to nothing: showing that delivery did not happen needs a deadline first.

**What I would put to him.** Say which reading, and give the offer that names an announcement a delivery point on its time reference, as F225's terms have one; "past its delivery point and not closed" is then a state the verifier reports, as "past its terms" is, judged with rule 33's evidence, and the refund terms are read from it. It is one field, and it is the F225 shape, not a new one.

## 5. "Computed, never declared" overclaims; rule 14a's shape is what the core can keep. CONFLICTS (F230; F232, line 4: "its recording's work hash is computed from its segments in order, never declared"; Envelopes rule 14a, line 272: "It cannot be judged without the media's key, so it is not a validity rule on the act; a client that unlocks the media checks it")

**What conflicts.** The recording's work hash is computable from the segments' plaintexts in order, as the live media cMIP defines the concatenation. A verifier that holds the segments' keys and implements the cMIP can compute it. Every other verifier cannot: paid segments are locked, and the concatenation is the cMIP's. For the claim on the stream to cover the recording, a Law client computing who owns hash R must know that R is stream S's recording; without the keys and the cMIP it knows that only because the closing act says so. So the tie is declared by the closing and checked by whoever can, which is rule 14a's exact shape, and "never declared" is not what the core can promise.

**Who gains.** Nobody by attack; the sentence misdescribes what a verifier does, so a build that honours it either refuses to read the closing (and the claim covers nothing for most clients) or reads it as declared and contradicts the text.

**What it decides about streams as a kind.** F234 keeps streams as the one kind the core spells out "because their segments chain and their recording is computed". Chaining is Envelopes rule 1 already. If the closing names the recording's hash, as every closing names what the announcement became, and a client holding the segments checks it as 14a checks a size, then a stream needs one sentence more than any other announcement: *for a stream, the closing also names the last segment, and a client that holds the segments and their keys checks the named work hash against their plaintexts in order, as the cMIP defines, showing a mismatch as inconsistent*. The buyer's check of F232 line 7 (each segment signed by the opener and chained) stays a SHOULD. F231 (only the opener's segments) then becomes a display rule while the stream runs, since the closing's walk back along the chain is what the recording is, and a segment the opener chained onto is the opener's choice, legible as rule 15 reads every claim. That is the least.

## 6. Two closings; a closing naming a segment off the chain; what a closing does to open offers. UNCLEAR (Envelopes rule 2, line 170: "Whether a fork is normal or a conflict is defined by the MIP that defines the object"; F232, line 4: "the closing act chooses the branch"; rule 32: a payment "arriving after a withdrawal" is owed back)

**Two closings.** F234 does not say that a closing names the announcement as its chain. If it does, two closings are a fork under rule 2 and the Announcements section, as the MIP defining the object, must say what the fork means; if it does not, two closings are two valid acts and nothing relates them. The smallest story: a seller closes a preorder naming a cheap cut for the buyers who paid early and, from another device, naming the full album for those who paid late; or simply closes twice by mistake. Who gains: a seller who wants two deliveries under one name. Can they pull it: yes; both acts are valid today (`today_a_closing_act_naming_what_an_announcement_became_is_read_by_nobody`), and "the earlier on the opener's own line" is not readable across devices until the next rotation (F223's lesson). The settled shape is the fork settlement's: while two closings stand, the announcement became nothing and the claim reaches neither; the opener closes again citing both, as a settling version names the tips it discards (F189). Nobody, allegedly's words for the stream fork fit: "a state not wished by MOR exists", shown.

**A closing naming a segment off the chain.** For a stream the closing "names the last segment". A segment not reached from the opening along the chain is either invalid (fail closed, Envelopes "Fail closed") or a fork of the opener's. Say which; I would say invalid, since the opener holds its own chain.

**A closing and the offers naming the announcement.** A closing ends the announcement; it does not end the offers naming it. Rule 32 makes a payment after a withdrawal owed back; nothing makes a payment after a closing anything. The story: the concert is over, closed, and the offer still takes payments. Who gains: the seller; can they: yes, legibly (the closing is public). The withdrawal analogy is right but "after" needs its sequence: the seller's receipt against the seller's own closing is readable on the seller's line only as F223 reads it, so the honest rule is W4's: a holder's receipt after its own closing is a wrong receipt, the rest is the buyer's client checking the announcement before paying (conformance, beside F188's "find the offer, verify that it is truly the last in its chain"). Or say nothing and leave it to `until`. Either, stated.

## 7. "Something MOR cannot hash" is every good and every service; the offer gains a third form for one task. UNCLEAR (F234, the definition; the offer's `sold`, line 575; F225; F232: "access to things that are not streams … left as today"; the Modules rule of 10 October)

**What is unclear.** A bowl, a repair, a ride, a seat, a delivery, an hour of a designer's time: none has bytes. By F234's definition each is an announcement. Case study 16 lists every product as "a standing offer: photos, description, price, stock, delivery terms"; case study 12 makes "the job an agreement" with a standing offer or a client's request. Today those sell under `[2, [cmip, params]]`, the access form, with the cMIP's terms; F225 just built the anchoring service that way, with deadlines and prices machine-read in the terms. F234 adds a form that names an announcement "with a description" in words. So one task, selling what has no work hash, has two forms, and the rule Nobody, allegedly, gave on 10 October applies: "If multiple options need to often or always exist side by side for the same task, they have to be modules." The project lead's check ("announcements add selling before the thing exists, replacing nothing") is true of the case studies as written and misses that the definition reaches them.

**Who gains.** Nobody by attack; builders lose: a listing cMIP must choose, a client must read both, and F216's layering (a carrier's agreement, a work's agreement) must be written twice.

**What I would put to him.** Draw the line by what an announcement is *for*: a name that other acts must point at before the thing exists (a stream's permissions and clips, a preorder that becomes a work, an event whose tickets are resold and whose claims are inherited), with a closing that names what it became. A good or a service that never becomes a work and needs no name beyond its offer stays as today, sold under a cMIP's terms. Or go the other way and make the access form *be* an announcement: the announcement act carries the cMIP and its terms, the offer names the act, and `[2]` is retired, so the anchoring service announces its terms and sells offers naming them. That is one form, and F225's deadlines survive as the announcement's terms. Either is coherent; both forms side by side is the one answer the Modules rule refuses.

## 8. F230's stated cost misstates what a rotation voids. COST, to restate (F230: "a rotation that voids the opening act voids the name, as for any everyday act"; Identity rules 16 and 17)

**What is wrong.** A rotation voids the signer's own acts after the kept tip. The name is the opening act's id, 32 bytes anyone may write into a claim, an offer or a stake; those acts are other identities' and stand. The thief's opening becomes a void act whose id is a free name.

**The smallest story, and the test.** `today_voiding_the_opening_act_voids_nothing_that_names_its_id`: a thief with Dario's everyday key opens a stream in his name and claims it; the club sells access naming it; a fan pays; Dario rotates to the tip before the thief's act. The opening and the thief's claim are void. The club's offer still counts, the fan's purchase is still a purchase, and the club's own claim on the void name binds the club as default holder. Who gains: the thief, for the hijack window, as stated already; and after it, whoever claims the void name first. Can they: yes.

**What to say instead.** A rotation that voids the opening act voids the opener's own acts on that line after the kept tip, the claim among them; what others signed naming the id stands, pointing at a void act, shown so; a stream whose opening is void has no opener and no recording. That is the cost, and it is the same cost as a publication whose act is voided: F169's "anchor or bear the loss", applied to a name.

## 9. The definition of a purchase lives in five places; F234 lists one; the mark is missing. CONFLICTS, editorial, mandatory

F234's core changes: an Announcements section, the offer's `sold`, rule 15, rule 32a, the core document's layers. A purchase of an announced thing also changes:

- Money draft 6, the definition (line 42: "A payment for a work under a Law claim") and rule 10c (line 209), which Finance-only wallets read, and which F225's principle says is Money's to carry and Agreements' to judge;
- core v21, "A purchase names the claim it pays under" (line 200) and the glossary rows "Purchase" (line 380) and "Work" (line 429), where "work keeps its one meaning" (the terminology note) must survive a claim that names a non-work;
- the freeze suite's component lines 66 ("a live stream sold by offer") and 81 ("key delivery or delivery confirmation as the binding moment"), the second being finding 4's question;
- the must-audit line 417, already moved by F232;
- the offer format's comment at line 576 ("something with no work hash yet (a live stream)"), once a stream is named by `sold`.

And the mark: the project lead's reading of my question 6 (a mark on the stream's name where it is used, so a verifier can tell it from a work hash) is recorded under F232 and absent from F234's changes; a verifier handed a work claim whose field 0 is an announcement id still cannot tell. The mark is small and belongs in the list.

**Two words in the amendment.** "IOU" and "to come at a later date" are findings-log words; if they enter Envelopes they put a consequence (what is owed, and when) in a layer that only Agreements can judge (F225: Money says money moved, Agreements say what follows; Envelopes says less than either), and "later" names no sequence (F207's lesson). The Envelopes section should define the act, its id as a name, its chain and its closing, and nothing about what is owed; the owing is the offer's, in Agreements.

## 10. What held, each with what I tried

**Delivery of what becomes a work.** For a preorder closed naming an album, the album is published and its key delivered to each buyer; rule 32 completes the sale at the key and rule 14 makes the buyer check the bytes against the hash the closing named. Nothing is left to a Module. F234's "how delivery is confirmed is a Module's" is true only of things that never become works (a seat, a chair), and the text should say so, or it throws away the one guarantee the core already has.

**Delivery of what never becomes a work.** No core act, since type 7 was retired (step 12b). What a verifier can say about "delivered": nothing, by rule. The shape that exists is F193's: only the payer the payment commits to acknowledges a delivery, in its claim; a Module's confirmation act is evidence the buyer's claim acknowledges, as a relay's delivery record is for a role share (rule 22). Nothing new in the core; the attendee's confirmation Nobody, allegedly, asked for is the buyer's acknowledgement, which the core already reads.

**Refunds and non-delivery under the offer's terms.** I tried a stream that stops after one segment, a preorder never closed, a concert cancelled: each is the offer's words and refund terms, judged by a client that reads Agreements (F215, F219, F225, F232 line 3); bad faith stays public. HOLDS as a stated cost, with finding 4's two gaps (the completion, the delivery point).

**Naming the announcement in the offer.** My last review's finding 2 (access names no work, so a claim on the stream reaches none of its sales) is closed by F232 line 5 once built: the offer names the stream by its opening act, rule 15b reads it, the lone seller's case stands. `today_an_access_offer_under_a_second_agreement_on_the_same_announcement_is_never_shown_outside_the_first` pins the gap as it is today for any access sale: the sponsor's deal sells access to a stream the club's deal also claims, and nothing shows it outside the club's; after the build, rule 15b shows it, as it shows a lone co-owner's publication of a song (scenario 2). What rule 15b cannot do, for announcements as for works, is choose between two claiming agreements: that is "whatever agreement claims it", read as every agreement holding a stake, a sale under another shown outside the first (rule 10c's reading, T-1).

**Moving money by a closing.** Nothing in Law reads a closing: a purchase names an offer and an agreement; stakes are in the agreement; the closing changes neither. A seller cannot move a buyer's purchase under another agreement by closing. HOLDS.

**A stranger's closing.** "A closing act its signer makes": the announcement's signer. A stranger's act naming the chain is a fork (rule 2) or invalid, as the section decides (finding 6); it never closes. HOLDS.

**A publisher's publication of what an announcement became.** F216 unchanged: the work's agreement is a participant of the publication's; the announcement's agreement is the work's once finding 2 is settled the right way. HOLDS.

**The common case.** A work never announced, a tip, a reader: no rule changes for them. What every client carries: two Envelopes act types (the announcement and its closing, implemented by every client or shown unknown), a third `sold` form in every Law client's purchase reading, the mark on a name where used, the stream branch of rule 14 (a SHOULD), and a verifier that computes a work's owners by scanning closings as well as claims. That is the parsing surface of putting the name in Envelopes rather than in a cMIP, and it is the cost my last review's finding 9 weighed; it is small, and it should be written down once.

## The questions, in the order I would put them to Nobody, allegedly

Each with his earlier words beside it, as the practice asks. The first two decide the rest.

**1. Does a claim that reaches a work through a closing meet other claims on that work at the closing, never at the announcement?** (Finding 1.) Beside it, his words: "a claim on a live work before it runs reserves a name, not content" (F231, "Agreed"); rule 15 as he approved it: "Order is not evidence of authorship … priority can only be decided where they meet"; and F218: "in the eyes of MOR and here are all that MOR could record about the claims."

**2. Is the closing act the creators' work claim on what the announcement became, completed by all of them under the announcement's agreement, rather than the announcer's act alone; and is an announcer who is not a creator a carrier, as a publisher is?** (Finding 2.) Beside it: "Yes." (F232 line 4, a claim on the stream covers its recording); "The owners have to all sign that agreement according to the rules of their deal" (F216); "A publisher may hold a stake in its publication, never in the work" (rule 16 before F216, laid beside it); and "What is a publication, it an envelope of an envelope, which carries two agreements" (F216).

**3. Does an offer naming an announcement carry a quantity, the seller's receipts under it numbered so that an over-sale confers nothing and is owed back; or is "500 seats" words for the Module and the door?** (Finding 3.) Beside it: "How is this policed?" and "Yes" (F224); "Yes, it's an acceptable cost. Make it legible, don't ban it. This one leaves plenty of bread crumbs" (F217); "One count across both branches would act as an alarm" (DQ6, 9 October); and the project lead's flag in F233, "a ticket sold twice is caught as F224 catches a stake sold twice", which this review finds false.
