## In plain words

The principle is right, and it is my own sentence taken to its end. I said a module that holds the constitution holds the collective; the trim kept the doors out in the core and let the model hold the clauses that decide who is gone, and the hostile reader walked straight through them. The fix is to say what "the constitution" is, once, and keep all of it under one rule: it changes only under the constitutional change rule, every member by default or what the founding explicitly wrote, never under a power a model names or adds; a model governs everyday acts and nothing else. That closes all six of the hostile reading's successes and both ambiguities, and it closes the stranger's first leak as a consequence. It does not close the second leak, which is not a side door into the constitution but a stated cost the founding chooses, and the text should say so in the core.

It also makes the text simpler than the trim left it: one thing kept whole instead of a list of exceptions, and the corner verdict that moved (question 3) moves back on its own.

## 1. The principle, and the list

**Right.** The admission test's second half is "a guarantee no module may be able to break", and the constitution is the thing every guarantee of part three rests on: who is a member, who declares absence and on what proof, who records, who judges, who inherits, what clock. A model that can change any of those can, through them, reach the way out, as the hostile reader did in two steps.

**What "the constitution" covers.** Terms fields 0 (the parties), 1 (the words), 3 (the keepers and their rule), 5 (the clone rule), 6 (the time reference), 9 (the abandonment clause: its authority, its outcomes, its absence-proof cMIP and parameters), 12 (the key grammar), 13 (the arbitrators and verifiers), 16 (the succession plans), 17 (the refund terms, since they bind buyers), 18 (the constitutional change rule), 19 (the layers' rules), 21 (the chain of judgment), 22 (the departed members entry), 24 (the holders' rule of the stake in itself) and 35 (the control model, its cMIP and its parameters), with the collective's split agreement (the act it names for its own payouts): each changes only under field 18's rule, and a model names no power for any of them.

Checked against the hostile reading: (1) the judicial clauses handed to one member: closed, fields 3, 9, 13, 16 and 21 are constitutional, and the declaration itself is already the core's (rule 98.4). (2) Dilution: closed by one guarantee moved into the core, below. (3) Parameter changes escaping `[0]`: closed by striking rule 98.3's exception; field 35's parameters are constitutional, as areas were in draft 17 (its field 19 row). (4) A publication paying a member's company: not a constitutional door; see 3. (5) The buyer bait: closed by question 11's sentence, which is part two's guarantee (the buyer relied on the rule at signing). (6) A keeper or clock chosen by one member's power: closed with (1). The ambiguities: a model adding powers to a constitutional clone is forbidden (question 12, (a)); a model refusing a change of field 35 is void of effect, since field 35 is the core's.

**Dilution, the one guarantee to move back.** Pre-emption is not a model's: an issuance that is not first offered to every holder of the stake in proportion shrinks each holder's share without them, which rule 98.2 forbids. *Sentence, rule 98.2: an issuance a collective's control model allows counts only where every holder of the stake was first offered its share in proportion, at the same price, for the same period; without that, the stake shrinks without its holders, and the issuance counts for nothing.* With that, no floor on the holders' rule is needed: a rule of one millionth binds only holders who accepted it, and dilution under it is each holder's choice at pre-emption. The client shows the rule at purchase (question 9's (b) for the share).

**What it costs a collective that wants a lighter constitution.** Changing a keeper, a judge or a plan takes every member unless the founding wrote field 18 lower. That is enough freedom: field 18 is the founding's own, shown at signing, and a founding of thirty that wants a two-thirds constitution writes it. What is forbidden is only a *later* lowering by a model's power, which is the attack. A two-member collective loses nothing; a large one chooses once.

**The sentence, replacing rule 98.3's definition and its exception:** *A constitutional change is a change of the collective's constitution: fields 0, 1, 3, 5, 6, 9, 12, 13, 16, 17, 18, 19, 21, 22, 24 and 35 (its cMIP and its parameters alike), and the collective's split agreement. A clone making one needs the constitutional change rule, `[0]`, whatever its control model asks; a model names no power for it and adds none to it; only this MIR reads which change is constitutional. A control model governs the collective's everyday acts, and which operational changes need which power, and nothing else.*

## 3. The stranger's two leaks

**(e) The broken collective's way back when the model is not held.** The principle closes it, with one sentence to make the closing visible. Which agreement is in force for the way out is read through constitutional clones alone, and every constitutional clone is judged by the core alone under field 18; an operational clone (one changing only what a model governs) changes nothing the way out reads. *Sentence, in rule 98's "not held" paragraph: for the way out, and for whether a collective is broken, the agreement in force is read along the line's constitutional clones alone, each judged by this MIR under the constitutional change rule; a clone changing only what the control model governs changes nothing those read.*

**(i) A token price or a greedy partner through a publication.** The principle does not close it, and should not: a publication a collective enters is an act of its Agreements layer, under the rule the founding named (rule 60), and a founding that named "any one member" there chose that risk and was shown it (F402). What protects the owners is sight and exit, which is the stated cost question 10 moves back beside rule 48. Two things the core should say so that it is a stated cost and not a hole: *entering a publication, or any agreement, as a party is an act of the Agreements layer under its rule, never of a control model's power alone*; and the cost sentence, in the core, in these words: *prices, partners and publications are no owners' matter; owners are protected by sight, the split being on the record, and by exit.* Legible, not forbidden.

## 4. The size

My estimate holds for what it was: the text after a say-it-once pass. The trim moved 12,000 words out and lost about 9,000 from the core, because the models' prose was the smaller half of the weight, as the report says. What remains is said two or three times: the glossary restates rules; "how it runs most of the time" paragraphs retell the rule above them; the formats re-explain the rules their fields serve; the stated costs repeat wherever the rule is touched. Cut in that order: the glossary first (keep one line per word, pointing to the rule); then the "how it runs" paragraphs where the rule is short enough to read; then the formats' commentary, which should say what a field holds and cite the rule, not restate it; then duplicate stated costs, kept once at the rule and cited elsewhere. I would now put the result at about 55,000 words with italics and 40,000 without, a little above the first estimate, because rules 98 and 99 and the constitutional list are new text that earns its place.

When: after the code session and before the third box, so that the box seals the text the C verifier will read, and so that the pass cuts prose against a core that already follows the rules, never meaning. It is a texts session of its own, with one rule: a rule's words may move or be said once, never change.
