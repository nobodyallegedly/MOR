# Collections and the core: a debate, written as a report

*11 October 2026. A debate, not a decision: nothing in `spec/`, `cmips/`, `modules/` or the code is changed by it. Written against core v21, Agreements draft 10 (with F237 to F253 written in), Envelopes draft 7, the website cMIP draft 4, the release manifest cMIP draft 2, and findings F216, F235, F237, F244, F248, F250, F252 and F253. The review `docs/reviews/case-studies-vs-vows.md`, which F252 and F253 cite for Q-G1 and Q-G2, is not in the repository on any branch; Q-G2 is read here as F253 records it, and Q08-1 was found nowhere. For Nobody, allegedly, to read and decide.*

## The question

Nobody, allegedly, asked: "Are collections, as a bundle of works and/or vows, something the core should track and structure? It trickles down to a whole lot… even a food delivery is a collection of vows item by item. I think it can be inherited in a whole lot of ways. It opens a few new questions. It feels like it unlocks powers…"

The project lead's position, tested here and not adopted in advance: no new core mechanic. A collection of bytes is already a work (a manifest of hashes, as a website's version is), claimable, offerable and splittable, its agreement designating one pointer and its split paying each member's pointer (F235, F244, F248). A collection of promises is already a vow: a vow holds promises, which may be bundled (F253). A mix is a vow whose promises name works. Formats (an album, a season, a menu, a curriculum) are cMIPs.

The yardstick is the core's own admission test: something belongs in the core only if it is part of the shared language every act is written in, or a guarantee no module may be able to break. Both sides are argued at their strongest, then each guarantee a core mechanic might give is tested against what the core already gives.

## In plain words first

A collection is a whole made of parts someone else may own: an album of tracks, a season of matches, a menu of dishes, a course of lessons, a delivery of items, a website of files. Three things about it matter to anyone: who agreed to be in it, how its money reaches its parts, and what state it is in when some parts are fine and some are not.

MOR already answers the three, by two tools. The first is that a list of fingerprints is itself a thing with a fingerprint: a manifest is a work, so everything the core does for a work (claim it, sell it, split its money, contest it) it does for the collection. The second is that money passes envelope by envelope: the collection's agreement does not divide its parts' shares, it pays each part's one designated address and stops, and each part's own agreement takes it from there. Consent is a signature: a part is in a collection because its owners signed the collection's agreement, by their own rule.

What the core does not do is look inside the list. It cannot tell which parts a manifest holds, in what order, or which promise of a bundled vow failed. Whether it should is the debate.

## What the core already holds

An inventory, so that neither side argues against a core that does not exist.

- **A manifest is a work.** The website cMIP and the release manifest cMIP describe one version of a site or a release as "a manifest of the parts" (Envelopes, "Media"): a media object with its own work hash, described by a publication, every file entered by path, work hash, size and lock. The collection's identity is the manifest's hash; a change is a new manifest, a new work, naming the one before (website cMIP, "One version names the one before").
- **A publication carries two agreements** (F216, rule 16). The work's agreement divides what reaches the work; the publication's agreement sets the terms of use and divides the publication's income; the work's agreement is a participant of the publication's, consenting by its own rule. An agreement is named as a party and as a stake holder by its version's hash; a deal inside a deal runs to eight layers (the layers build, R5).
- **Money passes layer by layer** (F235, F244, F248). Each layer's split names, as the receiver of the next layer's share, the one pointer the next agreement designates; the layer above never divides the next layer's share; an agreement that designates no pointer cannot be paid through a layer. "Work Agreement states pointer, publishing agreement states cut. Publisher delivers cut and its responsibility ends there."
- **The split is the cMIP's; the guarantee is the core's** (F250). Money goes only to an address the owners signed; the participant's check is "once the agreement's condition is triggered, is my cut at my flow address?"
- **A vow holds promises, which may be bundled** (F252, F253). A vow names in words anything MOR cannot hash, existing or to come; a seller bundles its promises as it chooses, in public; each sale of it is pending, confirmed or contested (rule 32b); a contest names the sale and may say, in its words or a cMIP's terms, which promise was not kept. What the promises are and how they are listed is the vow's words or a cMIP's (F237: the core keeps the grammar, the cMIPs the substance).
- **An offer sells a list.** The standing offer's field 1 is `[* sold]`: several publications, services or vows under one price (OF5 a: one amount). A co-owners' offer names their agreement and is signed under its rule; a lone seller's offer is itself the agreement (F215).
- **A sale outside the claiming agreement is shown as such** (rule 15b, F247), for a work and for a vow alike, by the name the offer carries.
- **Acts name acts.** `objects` places an act on a chain; `refs` says "look at this"; a payer's claim acknowledges an act about a vow (F193's shape). Any act can point at any other; the core reads the pointer, never the pointed-at bytes.

## Side A: for collections in the core, at its strongest

The case for a core mechanic is not that collections are impossible today. It is that they are everywhere, that every fork rebuilds the same thing, and that some facts about a whole are facts every verifier should compute alike, module or no module. What the core would track, and the guarantee each item claims:

1. **Membership.** A core collection act would list its members by act id or work hash: "these works, these vows, this collection." Guarantee claimed: every client, in every fork, can see what a collection holds without the format cMIP that wrote it, so a buyer paying for "the album" knows which tracks, and a track's owner can find every bundle that carries their work. Today a manifest is media: a client without the website or release cMIP sees a blob with a hash.

2. **Consent of the parts.** With members visible, the core could check each member's claiming agreement against the collection's agreement: a bundle selling a claimed work whose agreement is not a participant is shown as outside that claim, as rule 15b shows a single work's sale. Guarantee claimed: compilation cannot launder. Today rule 15b fires on the work the offer names; a compilation is a different work, with a different hash, and the tracks inside it are invisible to the rule.

3. **State rolled up from members.** A delivery is one order and many promises; a season is one ticket and many matches. The core could define a collection's state from its members' sale states: pending until every item is confirmed; partly contested when one is; partly refunded when one refund is discharged. Guarantee claimed: every verifier reads the same state for the whole, so a refund owed for one undelivered item is a fact, not a client's opinion. Today the state is per sale, and a bundled vow's sale is one sale with one state; which promise failed is words or a cMIP's field.

4. **Inheritance, both ways.** Downward: a collection's terms reach its members, so an album's licence binds each track's use, and a course's agreement honours each lesson's offer (learning case study: "if it does not, its published split shows exactly that"). Upward: a member's terms reach the collection, so a track whose agreement forbids resale below a price constrains any bundle it enters, and a member's stake transfer changes who the bundle pays. Guarantee claimed: no bundle silently loosens a part's terms, and no part silently changes what a bundle owes.

5. **A bundle price against item prices.** The core could relate the one price of a bundle to the prices of its members' standing offers, so a discount is visible as a discount and each member's share of it is computed, not declared. Guarantee claimed: a bundle cannot pay a member less than the member's own offer would, unless the member signed the discount.

6. **A member in several collections.** A work in many playlists, a lesson in many courses, a match in many season tickets. The core could keep the many-to-many relation so a part's owner sees all its wholes and a buyer of two wholes is not sold the same part twice.

7. **The shared-language argument.** The website and release manifest cMIPs already share one `file` entry; a music cMIP, a course cMIP, a menu cMIP would copy it a third, fourth, fifth time. One grammar, written once in the core, is what the vow was for: "MOR truly needed a structured way of selling something that has no bytes, or no bytes yet." A collection is a structured way of selling many things as one. The vow entered the core by that reasoning (F234, F237); the collection is the same reasoning again.

8. **The pervasiveness argument,** in Nobody, allegedly's words: "it trickles down to a whole lot." A concept that reaches commerce, delivery, music, sport, learning, software releases and websites is not a domain's concern; it is the shared layer's.

That is the strongest case. It rests on two claims: that a collection has facts of its own that must be computed alike everywhere, and that a core grammar would give a guarantee the cMIPs cannot.

## Side B: against, at its strongest

The case against is not thrift. It is that each of the eight items either already exists, under another name, or asks the core to do something its own rules forbid.

1. **Membership is a list of hashes, and a list of hashes is bytes.** The core's shared language for "this thing" is already its hash; the shared language for "these things" is a list, which deterministic CBOR writes in one way. What differs between an album, a season, a menu and a curriculum is everything else in the entry: order, duration, date, quantity, learning outcome, path. A core membership format either carries none of these, and every fork wraps it in a cMIP anyway, or carries some, and freezes one domain's shape on all the others. The core's own line: "The core keeps only this grammar: what is promised, delivered or became, quantities, kinds and streams belong to cMIPs" (Envelopes, "Announcements"). A member list is the collection's quantities and kinds.

2. **Consent is already a signature, and no list can make the unlisted visible.** A part is in a collection, in MOR's eyes, when its agreement is a participant of the collection's agreement, consenting by its own rule (F216, rule 16): the inheritance Side A asks for is given by signing. As for laundering: a core member list is declared by the one who writes the bundle. The honest declare; the dishonest omit a track, or ship a single file with the track mixed in, and no core field reaches into bytes. The guarantee "a compilation cannot launder" cannot be given by any mechanism, core or cMIP; the core already says so for the general case: "Stakes paid on every sale of a work" is outside the core, left to client rules and reputation services built on claims, contests and splits. What the core can give, legibility of declared members, a format cMIP gives in its fork, and the fork is where the buyers of albums are.

3. **A rolled-up state would make the core judge substance.** F237 drew the line that Nobody, allegedly, called an overreach to cross: the core wants to know whether a sale is pending, confirmed or contested, never what was promised or which promise failed. A collection state "partly contested" requires the core to know the members and to read, in a contest, which member it names; that is the substance F253 left to words and cMIPs. And the roll-up adds no fact: given the sale states, every conforming client computes the same whole, so a client that shows "two of three items confirmed" is reading, not deciding. A reading that every client can compute from acts it already holds is, by the fourth review rule, a client matter, not a core one.

4. **Inheritance across sellers is forbidden by the core's first promise.** "Only your own signature binds you… no rule ever decides that someone owes something they did not sign." A delivery's order pending until the courier confirms is fine when one seller, the platform, sold one bundled vow and subcontracts the rest: the platform owes, and the restaurant and the courier are its business. The moment the core rolls three sellers' sales into one state, it either makes the courier's failure the restaurant's problem, which no signature authorised, or it makes no one liable for the whole, which is no guarantee. F248's reason applies: "clear segmentation also helps pinpointing the cause of problems, or bad actors." Per-layer responsibility is the design; a cross-layer state blurs it.

5. **A bundle price is the collection agreement's cut, consented by its participants.** Each member's agreement signs the collection's agreement, and that agreement's split plan says what each gets of one price (F235: pools and subscriptions split "according to its stated rules"). Whether a member is paid less than its own offer would pay is already visible: the member's offer is public, the collection's split is published and delivered to every holder it pays. A core rule comparing the two would need the core to decide that one offer's price binds another agreement's split, which it never does: prices are terms, consented, not computed.

6. **A member in several collections is an index.** Discovery and indexing are outside the core by name. Selling the same part twice through two bundles is not a double sale: a work has no scarcity, and a vow's seats and quantities are the cMIP's (F237). Where scarcity exists (a stake), F224 already settles it on the seller's own line.

7. **The vow precedent cuts the other way.** The vow entered the core because it carried a state no cMIP could give uniformly: a sale of nothing hashable needed pending, confirmed or contested, read alike by every Agreements client, so that a buyer's refund is a fact. A collection has no state of its own; its states are its sales' states, which the core already keeps. The one shared `file` entry in two cMIPs is a format reused, as cMIPs may reuse each other's shapes by naming them; it is not a core absence.

8. **Pervasiveness is the reason for a cMIP, not a core rule.** Specialised forks are the evolutionary model: "a set of cMIPs and Modules made for one domain, speaking the same core language." A collection of vows in a delivery fork and a collection of works in a music fork speak the same core language already: agreements, offers, splits, pointers, sale states. What they share beyond that, a list, each writes.

## The test: which guarantees would only a core mechanic give?

Each guarantee Side A claims, against what already gives it and what nothing can give.

| Guarantee claimed | Already given by | What a core mechanic would add | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A bundle's money balances exactly and reaches every member's own address | Splits sum exactly; layer by layer to each agreement's designated pointer; no address the owners did not sign (F235, F244, F248, F250) | Nothing | Already core |
| No part is in a bundle without its owners' consent | The part's agreement signs the collection's agreement by its own rule (F216, rule 16) | A declared member list, checked under rule 15b, for declared members only | Legibility for the honest; nothing for the dishonest; the format cMIP gives it in its fork |
| The whole's state is read alike everywhere | Per-sale states (rule 32b); the whole computed by any client from acts it holds | A core state that requires reading which promise a contest names: the substance F237 excluded | Refused by F237 |
| One seller's failure is visible against the whole | A bundled vow sold by one seller: that seller owes (F253, rule 32) | Liability across sellers who signed nothing together | Forbidden: only your own signature binds you |
| A collection's terms bind its parts; a part's terms bind the collection | Consent by signature, at each envelope; a part's stake transfer reaches the bundle by pointer indirection (F248) | A rule that one agreement's terms override another's | Not a core power today; would need the core to read terms |
| A bundle pays no part less than the part's own offer | The split is public, the offer is public, the part's owners signed the plan | A comparison of prices across agreements | Prices are terms, not computed |
| A part's owner can find every bundle carrying it | Indexers | A many-to-many index | Outside the core by name |
| A buyer of two bundles is not sold one part twice | Works are not scarce; stakes are settled on the seller's line (F224); seats are the cMIP's (F237) | Scarcity the core does not define | Not the core's |

One row survives, weakly: a declared member list read under rule 15b. It is a field, not a mechanic, and it is the cousin of the open question QJ3 (`[ 1, work ]`, an offer selling any publication carrying a work). It passes the admission test only as shared language, since the guarantee it gives, legibility of what the honest declare, a format cMIP gives too. Its cost is a frozen shape for "the parts" that every domain will find too thin or too thick.

## Where the project lead's position bends

Three places where "it is already a work, a vow, or both" rests on a reading rather than on a rule, named so they are not taken for granted.

1. **An agreement as a party of any agreement.** Rule 16 makes a work's agreement a participant of a publication's agreement. A collection sold as a manifest is a work with a claim and an agreement; for its members to consent, their agreements must be participants of the collection's agreement. The cleanest reading is that the collection is published like anything else, and its publication's agreement has the member agreements as participants, exactly rule 16 with several works instead of one. The layers build took "a deal inside a deal, up to eight layers" as a reading (R5). This is the one reading the position depends on, and it should be confirmed rather than assumed: that the mechanic of rule 16, an agreement named by its version's hash as a party and a stake holder, is general and not written for a single work.

2. **Partial failure of a bundled sale.** A contest of a bundled vow's sale names the sale and may say which promise failed (F253). The core then knows the sale is contested; how much is owed back for one undelivered item of a priced bundle is the offer's or the agreement's refund terms (F215, F225, field 17), judged by a client holding the cMIP. The core says "owed back", never "how much". That is consistent with the core's stance (it has no court; a seller who refuses a valid claim is visibly in the wrong), but it is a stated cost, and should be written where vows are redrafted: a bundle's partial refund is terms, not grammar.

3. **The collection the core cannot see.** A manifest's members are bytes to the core. Rule 15b reads a vow's name as a work's (F247) but cannot read a work's parts. A compilation of claimed tracks sold under the compiler's lone agreement is, to the core, one work with one claim and one sale, not a sale outside each track's claim. The cost is the same as quoting, sampling and remixing, and the core already states it; it should be stated once more, for bundles, so that nobody later reads a manifest as a claim on its parts, or its parts' claims as reaching the manifest.

## Worked examples, mapped to today's grammar

- **A food delivery.** The platform publishes a vow: "this meal, delivered to this address, by this time", three promises bundled. The customer pays its offer; the sale is pending. The platform's agreement names the restaurant and the courier as payees or role shares; its split pays each one's pointer (F235). The courier's delivery act, acknowledged in the customer's payer's claim, confirms the sale (F193's shape, rule 32b); a contest names the sale and says the meal was cold. The restaurant and the courier are the platform's business; the customer's is the platform. With a courier cooperative selling direct, there are two sellers, two vows, two sales and two states, and a client shows them side by side. There is no third thing called "the order" beyond the agreement, and none is needed: in the commerce case study's words, "an order is an agreement."
- **An album.** Finished: a manifest of the tracks' hashes, a work, claimed by whoever assembled it; published under a publication's agreement whose participants are each track's agreement, each consenting by its own rule; one price, one split, each track's line paid to its designated pointer. Not yet recorded: a vow, "the album, next spring", sold by preorder; when the album exists, an ordinary work claim dated by itself, and the publication naming the vow in `refs`, which the buyer's claim acknowledges to confirm. Track order, artwork, liner notes: the album cMIP's.
- **A season ticket.** A vow with twenty promises, one per match (F253's own example); one sale per buyer; a contest naming the sale and the match that was not played; the refund per match the offer's terms.
- **A course of lessons.** The learning case study already: "the course names its lessons, and when it earns, lineage pays each lesson's teacher, if the course's agreement honours the lessons' offers; if it does not, its published split shows exactly that." The course's agreement with the lessons' agreements as participants is rule 16; a course that leaves a lesson out of its participants sells, to a client with the course cMIP, a declared part outside its claim; to a client without it, a work.
- **A website.** Already built: the site version is a manifest, a work, a publication, with versions naming the one before; the display client checks every file against its entry. It needed no core collection, and it is the strongest piece of evidence that none is needed: the first real collection on MOR was built, tested and shipped on a cMIP alone.

## Verdict

The project lead's position holds. A collection is a work when it is bytes, a vow when it is promises, a vow naming works when it is both; its parts consent by signing its agreement; its money passes to each part's one address and stops; its state is its sales' states. Of the guarantees a core mechanic might give, every one is either already given, forbidden by the core's own rules (only your own signature binds you; the core keeps the grammar, not the substance), or outside the core by name. The one field that survives, a declared member list read under rule 15b, gives the honest a legibility their fork's cMIP already gives and gives nothing against the dishonest.

What the question did expose, and what is worth carrying into the redraft of Agreements (F251), is not a mechanic but three statements: that rule 16's agreement-as-party is general; that a bundle's partial refund is terms, not grammar; and that a manifest neither claims its parts nor is reached by their claims, a stated cost in the family of quoting and sampling.

## For Nobody, allegedly, one at a time

1. **First:** does the reading stand that an agreement may be a party of any agreement, by its version's hash, as rule 16 makes a work's agreement a party of a publication's, so that a collection's members consent exactly as a work consents to its publisher? If yes, no collection needs more than rule 16. If no, a collection of several owners' works has no consent mechanism today, and that is the finding.
2. Then: is a bundle's partial refund (one item of a priced bundle not delivered) an accepted stated cost, left to the offer's or the agreement's terms, with the core knowing only "contested" and "owed back"?
3. Then: should the offer or the work claim be allowed to declare the works a manifest contains, read under rule 15b across forks (QJ3's cousin), or does that stay the format cMIPs', with the laundering cost stated once for bundles? The project lead's lean: the cMIPs', cost stated.
