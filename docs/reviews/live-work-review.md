# Hostile review of F229: a live work named by its opening segment, a class of its own; its recording a separate work naming it

*10 October 2026, night. Written for Nobody, allegedly, against main at `64b71f2`, before anything of F229 is built. Read against Envelopes draft 7 ("Work hash", "Publication", the media paragraph, "Encryption keys and key delivery", rules 10, 14, 14a; the line F229 reverses), Agreements draft 10 (rules 5, 15, 15b, 16 as rewritten for F216, 32, 32a; the standing offer and the work claim as built in step 12b), Money draft 6 (rule 10c), core v21 (the admission test, "Media", "Offers", "Client conformance"), the freeze suite (component line 66, scenarios 1, 2 and 6), F215, F216, F218, F224, F227, case study 17 draft 4, and the working practice at the end of the findings log. Nothing in `spec/`, `cmips/`, `modules/` or the code is changed. One test file, `core/tests/review_live_work.rs`, pins what the core does today where the questions touch it (five tests, all passing); each finding names the test that bears on it.*

## In plain words first

F229 wants one thing, and the want is right: a stream needs a fixed name from its first second, so that permissions, claims, clips and sales can point at it while it runs. The text of F229 gets there by calling that name a work hash and giving the stream a class of its own. That is where the trouble starts, because a work hash in MOR means one precise thing, approved twice: *the fingerprint of a work's complete plaintext, what ownership points to*. A fingerprint can be checked by anyone holding the bytes. A name cannot: it is a promise about bytes that do not exist yet.

The line F229 reverses ("a live stream has no work hash until it ends") was itself a correction from review round 2. The working practice asks, before any reversal, whether we are fixing a fix. My answer: the correction was right about fingerprints, and F229 is right about names, and the two do not contradict each other once the live name stops being called a work hash. A stream has a *name* from its first segment and a *work hash* when it ends. Keep both sentences, and most of this review's findings shrink to questions of where the name lives.

What I found, by weight:

1. **The name is not defined, and each reading breaks something.** "The fingerprint of its first signed segment" can mean the fingerprint of the segment's bytes, or the id of the signed act that carries them. Bytes are copyable: every stream that opens with the same slate, the same black frame, the same encoder preamble, and every rival who copies Dario's first two seconds, has the *same live work*. An act id is unique and signed, but it is an everyday act of one identity: a rotation that voids the opening act voids the name, and every claim, publication, offer and purchase pointing at it points at a void act. Neither cost is stated.
2. **Access sales name no work, so a claim on a live work reaches none of them**, and F229's own change list keeps it so. Today an offer selling access (`[2, [cmip, params]]`) names nothing the core reads; a purchase under it is judged with no work in sight. The club's 20%, rule 15b's "outside the claiming agreement", F216's outside-in line: none of them fire on a sale of access. This hole is older than F229 (the formats proposal named it, in the cost of OF1 (c)); F229 is the moment to close it, and its text leaves the access form as built.
3. **"Published while it runs" conflicts with what a publication is.** A publication names a locked object: its hash, its size once unlocked, its nonce, one media key delivered to each buyer, and a buyer MUST check the unlocked media against the work hash before a purchase counts as delivered (rule 14). A running stream has none of these. A live work's publication would have to describe a manifest so far, under a name that no unlocked media will ever hash to, with a key that opens either everything to come or only what exists. Which key delivery completes its sale (rule 32: "when the key is delivered") is undefined.
4. **What a claim on a live work binds is a name whose content is open-ended**, extended by whoever may sign a segment, and F229 does not say who may. If anyone may, a stranger appends to Dario's stream and the claim covers it. If only the opener's identity may, the live work is that identity's sequence under another name, and the core already has sequences and chains. Either way, a claim anchored before the stream proves nothing about content, where for a standard work it proves you held the bytes.
5. **Forks are already answered, by a rule F229 does not cite**: Envelopes rule 2, a fork is "defined by the MIP that defines the object". Segments are the live media cMIP's acts; the cMIP decides. F229's "anyone can check a segment belongs to it, in order" promises more than the core can keep across a fork.
6. **A stream that never ends, or stops early, costs nothing by core rule**: the sale completes at the first key delivered, and nobody can say "stopped" without a clock. That is a stated cost under the offer's terms (F215, F219), not a hole, provided "ended" becomes an act someone signs.
7. **The recording's tie to the live work is a reference the core does not read.** "Naming the live work as its source" is `refs`, "look at this". A stranger who records the public stream and claims its fingerprint is a competing claim like any other. There is one way to make the tie checkable, and it is cheap: the complete recording's work hash is computable from the chain.
8. **Clips, F216 and the common case hold**, each with one cost: lineage is a cMIP's and the name gives it a target from the first second; a publisher's publication of a live work runs F216 unchanged for publication sales, and skips it for access (finding 2); every client implements Envelopes, so whatever the class adds there is carried by the genesis client and the barebone client too, and a free stream nobody sells could already point at its opening act today.
9. **"A class of its own" does not survive the admission test as a class; a mark does.** A verifier handed a hash cannot tell a live name from a work hash: the class must be visible where the name is used, a field or a tag, and that mark is shared language. The chain, who may extend it, forks, order, the end and the recording's computation are all the live media cMIP's, as the core already says of segmentation.
10. **The reversed line lives in five places; F229 lists three.**

Where I tried to break things and could not, I say what I tried.

## How findings are marked

- **BREAKS**: an attack works, or a common case does not work, as written.
- **COST**: the rule works and someone pays; the cost is to be stated, or is stated.
- **CONFLICTS**: two texts, or a text and the code, disagree.
- **UNCLEAR**: the text can be read two ways, or a verifier cannot check it as written.
- **HOLDS**: I tried an attack and it failed; I say what I tried.

Each finding gives what breaks, the smallest story, who gains and whether they can pull it, the rule and the line, and the test where one bears on it. Ordered by weight. Line numbers are of main at `64b71f2`.

## 1. The name: a fingerprint of bytes is copyable; an act id is one identity's and voidable. BREAKS under one reading, COST under the other, UNCLEAR which (F229: "named from its start by the fingerprint of its first signed segment"; Envelopes "Work hash", line 186)

**What breaks.** F229 names the live work "by the fingerprint of its first signed segment". Two readings.

*(a) The work hash of the first segment's plaintext*, `tagged_hash("MOR/work", segment 1)`, in the spirit of "work hash". Then the name depends on bytes alone, and bytes are copyable. The smallest story: Dario's stream opens, as most do, with a slate or a second of black before the match. The club's second volunteer opens the second camera with the same slate from the same encoder. Two live works, one name. Or: the stream's opening is public (a free preview is how access is sold), a rival fetches Dario's first segment, signs it as his own opening and chains his pirate feed onto it: same name, two chains, and the rival's claim on the name is a second claim on the same work, openly contested (rule 15), with priority decided "only where they meet". Dario's permission from the club, his clips, his offers, all point at a name the rival shares. Who gains: whoever wants to ride a name, or to muddy one. Can they pull it: yes, with two seconds of public bytes; nothing in the core distinguishes the two openings, since a work hash is "the same wherever the work travels".

*(b) The id of the signed act carrying the first segment.* Then the name is unique (an act id commits to signer, position, running summary, salt) and the opener is known. But it is an everyday act of one identity, judged by Identity's rotation rules. The smallest story: Dario's everyday key is stolen on Saturday morning; the thief opens a stream under Dario's name; Dario rotates to a point before it (Identity); the opening act is void, so is the name, and every act that pointed at it (a claim, a publication, an offer, purchases, clips' references) points at a void act. For a standard work this cannot happen: a work hash is content, nobody's act. Who gains: a thief, for the hijack window; and, more quietly, Dario himself, who can unmake his own stream's name by rotating past it, with the purchases under it (rule 32 then owes refunds, as for any withdrawn offer; but the clips and permissions naming it name nothing). Can they pull it: yes; the cost is the ordinary one of everyday acts, and it is not stated for live works.

**The rule and the line.** Envelopes, "Work hash" (line 186): "the fingerprint of a work's complete plaintext … It is the same wherever the work travels and however it is locked or cut, and it is what ownership points to (Law)." Core v21, line 431: "Work hash: the fingerprint of a work's complete plaintext." F229 gives a live work a name that is either not a fingerprint of a complete anything (b) or the fingerprint of something incomplete and copyable (a), and calls it a work hash in both readings.

**What the test pins.** `today_a_work_claim_binds_any_hash_before_any_content_exists`: the work claim's field 0 is any 32 bytes; the core checks no form of it. Dario claims the id of his opening act and is bound to it at once; a stranger claims a name nobody has used and is bound to that; a stranger claiming Dario's opening makes it contested. So under reading (b) nothing in the claim format changes, and under reading (a) nothing stops the copied-opening claim either. The test is the baseline the build will change on purpose, or not.

**What I would put to him.** Reading (b), stated as what it is: a live work is named by the id of the act that opens it, the opener's own act, so its name is as durable as that act under Identity, and no two streams share a name. The cost (a rotation voiding the opening voids the name) stated once, beside the hijack window's cost in Envelopes. And the word "work hash" kept for what it means: the live work has a *name*; it has a work hash when it ends, which is finding 7's point.

## 2. Access names no work, so a claim on a live work reaches none of its sales; F229 keeps it so. BREAKS, pre-existing, closable here (the offer's `sold` form `[2, [cmip, params]]`, Agreements line 574; rule 32a; rule 15b; F216)

**What breaks.** Today a standing offer sells either a named publication, `[0, publication]`, or access, `[2, [cmip, params]]`, "something with no work hash yet". The access form names a cMIP and opaque parameters; the core reads none of them (`selling.rs`, lines 185 and 209: `Sold::Access(..) => None`). So no sale of access is ever "a payment for a work under a Law claim" (rule 32a, Money rule 10c): a purchase following an access offer names no claim and needs none, and every rule that keys on the work skips it: the club's share through the claiming agreement, rule 15b's "a publication or standing offer for a work with an existing claim, not signed under that claim's agreement, … visibly contradicts a signed agreement", F216's outside-in line to the work's agreement.

**The smallest story.** The club, a collective, and Dario have a deal: the club's 20% of what the stream earns (case study 17, section 1), written as a stake in the live work under F229. Dario publishes a *lone seller's* offer for access (field 0 absent, F215), parameters naming his stream in the cMIP's own words, and sells Saturday's match under it. Every payment is a purchase under his own terms (F215). The deal's claim on the live work is not contradicted, not even visibly: the offer names no work for rule 15b to read. Who gains: the streamer, against his co-owner. Can he pull it: yes, today, with the built formats; and under F229's change list, which says "the offer (F227: access, or a live work's publication)", still, because access stays unnamed.

**What the test pins.** `today_an_access_offer_names_no_work_so_a_claim_on_the_stream_reaches_none_of_its_sales`: Dario claims the stream's name; a stranger's lone offer for access to "Dario's match, live" counts; a fan's payment following it is a purchase under the stranger's terms; Dario's claim is not contested by it and the sale is not outside it. The two never meet.

**How we got here.** The formats proposal, OF1 (c) (line 115), stated the cost when it added access: "an offer for access names no work, so whether it is under a claim at all is a question of its own, which neither OF2 nor OF3 settles". Fable's formats review read `[2]` as given by rule 32 and the freeze suite's component line, and did not reopen it. F227 decided what an offer sells and did not touch access. Nobody has decided that access is sold outside every claim; it fell out of "no work hash yet". F229 removes the premise ("no work hash yet") and keeps the consequence.

**What I would put to him.** Once a live work has a name from its first second, access to it is access *to a work*, and the offer can name it: `[2, [live work, cmip, params]]`, or simply the live work's publication under `[0]` with the cMIP's parameters in the publication. Then rule 32a, rule 15b and F216 apply to a sale of access as to any sale, and the F215 lone-seller case stays what it is (a lone seller with no co-owners names no claim). The only access that still needs the unnamed form is access to something that is not a work at all (a service's app, freeze scenario 6), which is a question of its own.

## 3. "Published while it runs" against the publication format, rule 14, rule 14a and the key delivery. CONFLICTS (Envelopes "Publication", lines 190 to 206; rules 10, 14, 14a; rule 32 "complete when the offer's side delivers the content key")

**What conflicts.** F229: "a live work can then be claimed, published and priced while it runs." A publication (Envelopes type 0) describes a media object by its locked bytes' hash (field 2), its size once unlocked (field 3), its nonce (field 4), and, for media on sale, one media key delivered to each buyer. Rule 14: "A client MUST check unlocked media against the work hash before treating a purchase as delivered." Rule 14a: a publication whose size is not the unlocked media's is shown as inconsistent. The media paragraph allows the fields to "describe the whole, or a manifest of the parts".

A running stream has no locked bytes' hash, no size and no single nonce; a manifest of the parts so far has all three, but it changes with every segment, and a publication is an act: nothing in MOR is updated, it is published again (core v21, "Nothing in MOR is updated"). So "published while it runs" is one of: (i) a new publication per manifest, each a new act id, so that an offer naming the publication by id (F227) is stale at the next segment, and a withdrawal (type 3) names one of them; or (ii) one publication whose fields 2 to 4 describe a manifest that will be replaced, which rule 14a then shows as inconsistent the moment it is; or (iii) a publication whose fields 2 to 4 describe nothing (impossible: the fields are required). Under every reading, rule 14's check fails by construction: no unlocked media will ever hash to the opening segment's name, so a buyer's client that honours the MUST never treats a live purchase as delivered, and one that does not honour it is non-conforming.

**The key.** Envelopes, "Key delivery": a key delivery "MUST name the act or publication whose key it carries" (rule 10) and carries "the publication whose media key it carries". One key per publication. For a live work, that key opens either everything to come (one media key for the whole stream, delivered once: then a refund claimed mid-match leaves the buyer with the key, a leaked key opens the rest of the season, and no subscription can end mid-stream, every one of which Envelopes already assigns to "access modules": "Group keys, subscriptions and rights management are access modules") or only what exists at delivery (then every later segment needs a delivery, a sealed container per buyer per segment, which is not a protocol, it is a denial of service on the seller). The brief's question, "key delivery for segments not yet made", has no answer in the core and should not: it is the cMIP's key schedule under whatever one key the publication delivers, as it is today for access.

**Which delivery completes the sale.** Rule 32: "The deal is complete when the offer's side delivers the content key". With a live work's publication there is one media key, so the sale completes at that delivery and finding 6 follows. With per-segment keys, "the" key is undefined, and so is the moment a refund stops being owed.

**Who gains.** Nobody by attack; the common case fails. A seller who publishes a live work and prices it has a publication no conforming buyer can treat as delivered.

**What I would put to him.** Two shapes, both honest. (A) A live work is *claimed and priced* while it runs, through an offer naming it (finding 2), and *published* when it ends, as its recording (finding 7): the publication format, rule 14 and the key delivery are untouched, and "published while it runs" is withdrawn from F229. (B) A live work's publication is a publication of its *manifest*, re-published as the cMIP defines, its media key the root of the cMIP's key schedule, and rule 14 gains one branch: for a live work, the buyer's client checks each segment belongs to the chain, as the cMIP defines. (B) is a change to a MUST every client carries; (A) is none. The want F229 states ("permissions, claims, clips and sales can point at the stream while it runs") is met by (A).

## 4. What the claim binds: a name with open-ended content, and nobody said who may extend it. UNCLEAR, with a COST under each answer (rule 15; F229 "each later segment names the one before, signed as it goes"; case study 17 "each feed is a work with its own maker")

**The attack, as asked.** Dario opens a stream with a slate reading "Match A", claims it, and gets the club's permission (a live-rights cMIP act naming the live work). Then he chains onto it a professional feed he has no right to show, or Sunday's match the club did not permit. The claim says nothing about content; the permission points at the name. Who gains: the streamer, against the rights holder (the broadcaster's exclusive; the club's choice of what is streamed). Can he pull it: yes. Every segment is signed, so the trail shows what was streamed under the name; the club contests, withdraws its grant, publishes its own. That is rule 15b's shape, "visible, never prevented", and MOR's chosen stance ("the core records claims and their order, never legitimacy"). I mark it a cost, to be stated, not a break: it is already the case for a standard work that anyone may publish a stranger's bytes under a claim of their own.

**What is new, and is a break of a promise rather than a rule.** For a standard work, a claim carrying a pre-publication commitment (rule 15: "a claim cMIP MAY carry a pre-publication commitment") and an anchor proves that the claimant held the bytes before the point. For a live work, a claim before the stream proves only that the claimant reserved a name, and names are free: the claim cMIP's commitment has nothing to commit to. "Order is not evidence of authorship" (rule 15) needs a live reading: for a live work, order is not even evidence of *content*. Nothing to fix; a sentence to add.

**Who may extend the chain.** F229: "each later segment names the one before, signed as it goes". Signed by whom? Three answers, each with a cost:

- *Anyone.* A stranger's segment naming Dario's last one is a segment of Dario's live work; Dario's claim covers it; rule 15 binds "a work" to its creators, and the work now has content its creators did not make. A verifier cannot tell Dario's segments from the stranger's without a rule. BREAKS, if this is the reading.
- *The opener's identity only* (its everyday key, and its grant keys, since what a grant key signs is the grantor's own act, F128). Then the live work is a chain of one identity's acts, which Envelopes already defines ("Chains": an act names, in `objects`, the chain and the act it follows; "Sequences": single lines, parallel devices separate sequences). The second camera (case study 17, section 3) is then not in Dario's live work, which the case study already says: "each feed is a work with its own maker". The broadcaster's commentary team under grant keys (section 5) works: their segments are the broadcaster's own acts, the broadcaster is the opener. This reading costs nothing new.
- *Whoever the live media cMIP allows*, the claim covering only segments its creators (or their grant keys) signed. This is the second reading with the cMIP free to show more; it needs the sentence "a live work's segments are the acts of its creators naming its chain; a segment by anyone else is shown as a fork, never as the work".

**What the test pins.** `today_a_work_claim_binds_any_hash_before_any_content_exists` (finding 1): the claim binds a name and reads no content.

## 5. Forks: already decided by Envelopes rule 2, which F229 does not cite; "in order" over-promises. HOLDS as a reading, UNCLEAR as written (Envelopes "Chains, acknowledgements and references", rules 1 to 3; rule 5 of Agreements)

**What I tried.** Two segments naming the same previous one, three ways. *A restart:* Dario's phone dies, the client resumes from the last segment it has, and one segment it had already sent is unknown to it; two segments name one. *Two devices:* a laptop and a phone both chaining to the same opening, "parallel devices keep separate sequences" (Envelopes), so each device's segments are on its own sequence but both name the same chain. *A deliberate split:* Dario forks his stream into a free branch and a paid branch, both naming the opening, and sells the paid one.

**What the core already says.** Envelopes rule 1: an act belongs to an object's chain by naming, in `objects`, the chain and the act it follows; "a chain may branch and merge". Rule 2: "Two valid acts naming the same predecessor in the same chain are a fork, provable from their signatures and insides. Whether a fork is normal or a conflict is defined by the MIP that defines the object (F4)." Segments are acts of the live media cMIP, so the cMIP defines the object and decides whether a fork is a restart to be merged, two devices to be shown side by side, or a split that ends the stream. Rule 3: "A chain's order records when acts were added to it, never when the things they refer to were created." So the live work, across a fork, is the chain with its branches, as held: the name covers every branch, the claim covers every branch, a purchase of access is complete at its key delivery whichever branch the buyer watched (rule 32), and the recording is per branch (finding 7).

**What over-promises.** F229: "anyone can check a segment belongs to it, in order". Belongs, yes: it names the chain. In order, no: across a fork there is no one order, and the core says so. The sentence should stop at "belongs to it".

**The deliberate split, who gains.** Dario, if anything in Law read "the branch": nothing does. The club's share is on the agreement's stakes and the offer's payee (rules 18, 26), not on a branch; a purchase names the offer, not a segment. I could not make a branch move money. HOLDS.

**A stolen key mid-stream.** The thief's device appends segments to the chain with Dario's everyday key: a fork at the theft. Dario's rotation voids the thief's acts (Identity), the chain is cut there, and buyers who received keys for void segments received what they received: the hijack window's stated cost, unchanged. The one new thing: under finding 1's reading (b), the thief's segments do not void the name, since the opening is Dario's; under reading (a), a thief who *opened* the stream owns a copyable name. HOLDS under (b).

## 6. A stream that never ends, or ends without a recording, or stops early. HOLDS as a stated cost, provided "ended" is an act (rule 32; F215; F219; the working practice on "before", "after", "later")

**What is owned.** The name and the chain so far, by the claim (finding 4). A stream that never ends is one with no closing segment held, and nobody can say more: MOR has no clock ("Showing that something did not happen before a deadline needs evidence: an anchoring cMIP or a named keeper", rule 33).

**What is sold.** Access under an offer (today), or the live work's publication (finding 3). A purchase is complete when the key is delivered (rule 32). So a stream that stops after one segment has completed every sale whose key went out. Who gains: the seller. Can they pull it: yes, and the buyer saw the terms: a lone seller's offer carries "its words, its deadline and, if the seller sets any, its own refund terms" (F215); co-owners' offer takes the agreement's refund terms (field 17); "a refund past the terms the buyer accepted by paying is ended" and "bad faith terms remain public" (F219). A promise such as "until the final whistle" is the offer's words, or a condition cMIP, and a refund for a stream that stopped early is owed under them, never by core rule. This is the pattern Nobody, allegedly, chose for anchoring services too (F228: "the service makes a promise and states a price … If the service they offer ends up losing money, they are to take on that burden"). COST, stated by the texts that exist; nothing new to write beyond one sentence saying so for live works.

**What must be an act.** F229 says "its recording, once complete". Complete on which sequence? The practice (F207's lesson): "any rule or suggestion that says 'before', 'after', 'later' or 'first' names the sequence it is read on". The only sequence here is the live work's chain, and "complete" needs a last segment that says it is last: a closing segment signed by the opener, as the cMIP defines, the way a collective closes by a closing act. The core's review rule says it: "a rule that rests on a fact nobody can check becomes an act someone signs". Without it, "ended" is undetermined for every verifier, and finding 7's recording has no chain to compute from. UNCLEAR in F229's wording; nothing for the core to carry once the cMIP has the act.

**Ends without a recording.** Then there is a live work (name, chain, claim) and no standard work: the stream was a performance. Owned: the name and chain, by the claim. Sold: what was sold. Owed: nothing beyond the terms. Clips cut from it (finding 8) keep their lineage to the name. HOLDS.

## 7. The recording's claim against the live work's; a stranger's recording; an edited one. UNCLEAR as written ("naming the live work as its source"), with one cheap way to make the tie checkable (rule 15; `refs`, Envelopes rule 5)

**What the core reads today.** Nothing. The recording is a standard work with its own work hash; "naming the live work as its source" is a reference (`refs`, "look at this"), or a field of the recording's cMIP; the core reads neither. A stranger who records Dario's public stream and claims its fingerprint is the recorded owner of that recording's work (F218: the creator who opened the claim), uncontested unless Dario claims the same bytes; the stranger's claim naming Dario's live work "as source" is neither checked nor needed. An edited cut, a second camera's recording, a recording with the commentary stripped: each its own work, each free to name the live work, truthfully or not.

**What the test pins.** `today_the_recording_and_the_live_name_are_two_works_with_no_tie_the_core_reads`: a stranger claims the live name, Dario claims the recording's fingerprint and his publication of it names the live work in `refs`; neither claim is contested by the other; the reference changes nothing on either side.

**Who gains.** The faster recorder, as in the journalism case study's copied photo: visible, contested, never prevented. F229 adds no new hole; it adds a sentence ("naming the live work as its source") that reads as a tie and is not one. That is the same gap finding 4 names from the other side: the live work's claim does not reach its own recording, and the recording's claim does not reach the live work.

**The cheap tie.** A live work's chain, from its opening to its closing segment, with no fork, determines one byte string: its segments' plaintexts in order. Its work hash, `tagged_hash("MOR/work", that plaintext)`, is then *computable* from the chain by anyone holding the segments and their keys, not declared by anyone. So "the complete recording of live work L" can be a definition, not a claim: the standard work whose plaintext is L's segments in order, as L's cMIP defines the concatenation. Then the live work's claim covers its own recording's hash by construction (one claim, two names), a stranger's claim on that hash is a second claim on the live work's own content, contested where they meet, and an edited cut is what it is: another work, whose lineage, if any, is a cMIP's. Across a fork, each branch has its recording, which is right. This is Nobody, allegedly's own sentence, read the other way round: "if that stream then becomes a VOD it can have another hash pointing to the live as evidence". The pointing can go from the live to the VOD, by computation, which is evidence; from the VOD to the live, by declaration, is not.

## 8. Clips, a publisher's publication (F216), and the common case. HOLDS, each with a cost named

**Clips cut during the live.** A clip is a standard work (its bytes), claimed by the clipper. Its lineage to the live work is a lineage cMIP's (case study 8: "a remix-lineage cMIP: how a derivative work names its sources"; case study 13; the core defines no lineage, and `grep lineage spec/` finds only an agreement's versions). F229's gain is real here: the live work has owners from its first second (the claim, the agreement), so a lineage cMIP that pays the source's agreement has a target before the match ends, and "a work whose content is unfinished" costs it nothing, since lineage pays an agreement, not a content. HOLDS. One cost: a clip cut from a branch later voided (a thief's segments) names a void segment as its source; the clip's own claim stands, its lineage shows a void source. Rare, legible.

**A publisher's publication of a live work, F216.** The broadcaster carries Dario's live work by its own publication; the publication's agreement sets terms of use and divides income, the live work's agreement is one participant, consenting by its own rule; a sale through the publication passes outside in. Nothing in rule 16 reads the work's content, so a live work's agreement is a participant like any work's, from the first second. HOLDS for sales *of the publication*. It skips every sale *of access* (finding 2), and a broadcaster sells access (freeze scenario 6: "sells access under a standing offer requiring its own app"), so in the case study's own ordinary story the line to the live work's agreement is never passed. Finding 2 is where this is fixed. The grant-key case: Dario in the broadcaster's commentary team opens a stream with a key the broadcaster granted him; what he signs that way is the broadcaster's own act (F128), so the opener, hence the owner of the live name under reading (b), is the broadcaster; "what he publishes under his own identity … remain his" (case study 17, section 5) holds exactly. HOLDS.

**The common case.** A recorded work carries no new weight: the live class is beside standard works, and nothing in the work hash, the claim, the publication or the purchase rules changes for a work that was never live. HOLDS, with one check: rule 14 is a MUST every client carries, and Envelopes is implemented by every client ("Every client implements Identity, Envelope and Text"). Whatever the live class adds to Envelopes is carried by the genesis client, the barebone client and the reader, or they show live acts as unknown; that is the cost of putting it in Envelopes rather than in a cMIP, and it is the cost finding 9 weighs. A stream nobody sells (the phone in the stands, section 1) needs: a name to point at. It has one today: the opening act's id, which a claim can name (`today_a_work_claim_binds_any_hash_before_any_content_exists`) and a reference can name. Most of F229's stated want ("permissions, claims, clips and sales can point at the stream while it runs") is met by that convention with no core change; what the convention lacks is a *purchase* reading the name (findings 2 and 3) and a mark saying what kind of name it is (finding 9).

## 9. "A class of its own" under the admission test: a mark in the core, the rest in the cMIP. COST, to be chosen (core v21, "Admission test"; "Guiding question"; Envelopes "Tasks", media interpretation; Nobody, allegedly on cMIPs and Modules)

**The test.** "Something belongs in the core only if it is part of the shared language every act is written in, or a guarantee no module may be able to break." And: "what is the least the core needs to know to make this work?"

**What is shared language.** A verifier handed a claim, a publication, an offer or a purchase that names a hash cannot tell a live name from a work hash by looking: both are 32 bytes. Under reading (b) of finding 1 the name is an act id, so a verifier could fetch the act and see that it is a live media cMIP's act, if it implements that cMIP; one that does not, or has not fetched it, sees a hash. So the *kind* of name must be visible where the name is used, or no client can apply rule 14 (finding 3) or know that rule 15's claim is on a name rather than a content. That mark is shared language: a field on the work claim and the publication ("the work named is a live work: the id of its opening act"), or a domain-separated name (`tagged_hash("MOR/live-work", opening act id)`, which never collides with a work hash and still does not announce itself), or both. The mark is the core's. It is small.

**What is a guarantee no module may break.** That a live name is unique and the opener's (reading (b)); that a claim on it is a claim like any other (rule 15 unchanged); that a sale of access to it is under its claim (finding 2); that its complete recording is computed, not declared (finding 7). Each is one sentence in a core text, none is a mechanism.

**What is the cMIP's**, by the core's own words: "Segmentation, chunk fingerprints, streaming and live media are defined by the cMIP the publication names" (Envelopes, line 210), and F229's own note, "segmentation stays the live media cMIP's". So: the segment format; who may sign a segment and how a stranger's is shown (finding 4); what a fork is (finding 5, by Envelopes rule 2 as it stands); the closing segment (finding 6); the concatenation that defines the recording (finding 7); the key schedule under the one delivered key (finding 3). Nobody, allegedly's rule for cMIPs and Modules applies: "If multiple options need to often or always exist side by side for the same task, they have to be modules." Live media types will exist side by side; the live media cMIP names the task, Modules carry the formats, and the core carries the name.

**Against "a class of acts".** F229's words, "a different class of acts from standard works", read literally put a new act type in the core (a live work's own publication, say). I could find no act the core must define: the opening segment and every later one are the cMIP's acts, the claim is type 3, the offer type 6, the publication type 0 with a mark. "A class of its own" is true of the *name* and of nothing else.
