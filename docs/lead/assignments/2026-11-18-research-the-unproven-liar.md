## The question

MOR proves who said what, in what sequence. It never proves that what was said is true. An issuer signs "six eggs delivered"; no eggs arrived. The record is perfect, and the lie is in the world, not in the bytes.

**What has made a lie costly where it could not be proven?** And what did those arrangements need from the record they ran on?

Research how people have answered this, from public sources. At least:
- **Witnesses and attestation:** notaries, bills of lading and proof of delivery, auditors, inspectors, surveyors. Why was a witness's word worth more than a party's, and what made a witness lie?
- **Repeated dealing and reputation:** the Maghribi traders' coalition (Greif) and its critics; the medieval law merchant and the fairs (Milgrom, North and Weingast) and its critics; Macaulay on contracts businesses never enforce; online reputation systems, what they measured and how they were gamed.
- **Something to lose, posted in advance:** bonds, stakes, hostages and collateral (Williamson); guilds and joint liability; escrow.
- **Price as the verdict:** note discounts and vouchers (your own earlier report, `docs/research/issuer-pricing-prior-art.md`); markets for credence goods, where the buyer cannot check even after buying.
- **Two who lie together:** collusion between a party and its witness, or between payer and receiver. What caught it, if anything did.
